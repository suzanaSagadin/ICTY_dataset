 6             As the registrar announced, the case on our agenda today is the
 7     Prosecutor versus Miroslav Deronjic.  This is an appeal from the
 8     sentencing judgement rendered by the Trial Chamber on 30th March 2004.
 9     At the plea hearing held on 30 September 2003, Mr. Deronjic pleaded
10     guilty to one count arising out of events that took place in the Muslim
11     village of Glogova in the Bratunac municipality in Eastern Bosnia and
12     Herzegovina.
13             According to the second amended indictment, Mr. Deronjic was then
14     president of the Bratunac Crisis Staff, participated in the joint
15     criminal enterprise which had as objective the permanent removal by force
16     or other means of Bosnian Muslim inhabitants from the village of Glogova
17     in the municipality of Bratunac.
18             On 9 May 1992, as part of his participation in this joint
19     criminal enterprise, he launched an attack on the village of Glogova.
20     The village was burned down in part and the Bosnian Muslim residents were
21     forcibly displaced from the village.  As a result of this attack, 64
22     Muslim civilians from the village were killed, Bosnian Muslim homes,
23     private property, and the mosque were destroyed, and a substantial part
24     of Glogova was razed to the ground.
25             Based on the plea agreement, the second amended indictment and
 1     the factual basis, the Trial Chamber entered a conviction under Article
 2     7(1) of the Statute against Mr. Deronjic for one count of persecutions
 3     committed on 9 May 1992, a crime against humanity, pursuant to Article
 4     5(H) of the Statute.
 5             On 18 March 2004, the Trial Chamber sentenced Mr. Deronjic, Judge
 6     Schomburg dissenting, to 10 years of imprisonment.  Mr. Deronjic has
 7     appealed his sentence.
 8             Today we will hear arguments from both parties, but before giving
 9     the floor to counsel, I will summarise briefly the grounds of appeal
10     advanced by the Defence as stated in its written submissions.
11             In his appellant's brief, Mr. Deronjic has raised four grounds of
12     appeal:
13             One, in his first ground of appeal, Mr. Deronjic contends that
14     "The Trial Chamber erred in law and fact and abused its discretion in
15     reaching conclusions and findings which are based upon evidence not
16     specifically contained in the second amended indictment, the plea
17     agreement, or the factual basis.
18             The second ground of appeal is that "The Trial Chamber erred in
19     finding the lex mitior principle is not applicable in Mr. Deronjic's
20     case.
21             In his third ground of appeal, Mr. Deronjic says that "The Trial
22     Chamber erred with respect to the factors it took into account in
23     aggravation."
24             Finally, in his last ground of appeal, Mr. Deronjic alleges that
25     "The Trial Chamber erred when considering the mitigating factors."
 1     I will now remind the parties about the standard of review applicable to
 2     sentencing appeals.  The relevant provisions on sentencing are Articles
 3     23 and 24 of the Statute and the Rules 100 to 106 of the Rules of
 4     Procedure and Evidence.
 5             Under Article 25 of the Statute, an appeal is not a trial de
 6     novo.  The role of the Appeals Chamber is limited to correcting errors of
 7     law invalidating a decision and errors of fact that have occasioned a
 8     miscarriage of justice.  Trial Chambers are vested with broad discretion
 9     to tailor the penalties to fit the individual circumstances of the
10     accused and the gravity of the crime.  In general, the Appeals Chamber
11     will not revise a sentence unless the Trial Chamber has committed a
12     discernible error in exercising its discretion.  A Trial Chamber's
13     decision may, for example, be disturbed on appeal if the appellant shows
14     that the Trial Chamber erred in the weighing process, either by taking
15     into account what it ought not to have considered or by failing to take
16     into account what it ought to have considered.

 1     position is that the Court is bound by the indictment and that there must
 2     be objective identity between the judgement and the indictment, both with
 3     respect to the factual description of a crime and with respect to the
 4     legal qualification of an act for which the accused is charged.
 5             By applying a narrow interpretation to this concept of objective
 6     identity, jurisprudence took the view that the Court is strictly bound by
 7     the indictment.  If a somewhat broader interpretation is applied, then
 8     the Court is authorised to deviate from the factual description of the
 9     crime in the indictment and also from the legal qualification of that act
10     but only if it is in favour of the accused.
11             Such position taken in the jurisprudence in our jurisdiction did
12     not foresee a situation in which criminal proceedings could conclude with
13     a guilty plea of the accused reached on the basis of plea bargaining with
14     the Prosecution, because our criminal legislation until recently did not
15     provide for such a situation.
16             However, here before this Tribunal, the situation is much
17     clearer.  The Statute provides for a possibility for proceedings to be
18     concluded with an agreement reached between the accused and the
19     Prosecutor, providing that the conditions in Rule 62 bis are met.  The
20     Trial Chamber established that the requirements from Rule 62 bis were met
21     after holding several hearings during which all dilemmas were resolved
22     largely on the basis of the facts which were made undisputable by the
23     Prosecution and Defence.

12             Your Honours, the process of rehabilitation is a complex process

18             Rehabilitation represents a combination of procedures and

 3     The appellant in the second ground of appeal alleges that the Trial
 4     Chamber erred in finding that the principle of lex mitior is not
 5     applicable in this case with regard to the law in the former Yugoslavia.
 6     It is now settled in the jurisprudence of this Tribunal that it is not
 7     bound by the principle of lex mitior.  This has been held by the Appeals
 8     Chamber in the Blaskic and the Dragan Nikolic case.
 9             The Blaskic appeal judgement, in footnote 1.433, stated that - I
10     quote - "The International Tribunal is not bound to apply the more
11     lenient penalty under the jurisdiction on the territory of the former
12     Yugoslavia (the lex mitior principle)."
13             This was confirmed by the Appeals Chamber in the Dragan Nikolic
14     case in paragraphs 77 through 86 of the judgement.  It is explained there
15     that "It is an inherent element of the principle of lex mitior that the
16     relevant law must be binding upon the Court" because it is only where it
17     is binding that the defendant has a protected legal position.  However,
18     the Tribunal is not bound by the law of the former Yugoslavia.  It has
19     merely to take it into consideration.  Moreover, otherwise, the states in
20     the former Yugoslavia would have the power to undermine the sentencing
21     discretion of the ICTY Judges by simply reducing the national sentencing
22     ranges.  This would be incompatible with the Tribunal's primacy provided
23     for in Article 9 of the Statute.  Therefore, the Trial Chamber did not
24     commit an error by not applying the principle of lex mitior.

16     counsel for the appellant.  Allegedly the Trial Chamber erred by taking
17     into account:  One, factors which as such cannot be taken into account in
18     aggravation; two, factors which were part of the factual basis; three,
19     factors which constitute an element of the offence; and, four, factors
20     which were already subsumed in the overall gravity of the offence.
21             The main question here is whether the Trial Chamber impermissibly
22     counted factors twice.  It is impermissible to consider circumstances
23     which form an element of the crime as an aggravating factor and it is
24     impermissible to consider a circumstance as an aggravating factor which
25     has already been dealt with to determine the gravity of the crime.
 1             As it is apparent from footnote 289, the Trial Chamber was well
 2     aware of this principle, as it stated - I quote - "Each circumstance may
 3     only justly be considered once."  And it is our submission that the Trial
 4     Chamber did not violate this principle.  The mere fact that the Trial
 5     Chamber considered the gravity and the aggravating factors together under
 6     one heading in paragraph 178 and following does not mean that it
 7     considered the factors twice.
 8             It is clear from paragraphs 184, 185, and 223 that the Trial
 9     Chamber looked at both the gravity of the crime and the aggravating
10     circumstances.  Whether it gave the correct label to each of them -
11     meaning, whether it called them an aggravating factor - or used it to
12     determine the gravity of the crime is in our submission irrelevant as
13     long as when determining the aggravating factors it only took into
14     account factors which can be taken into account in aggravation, which do
15     not constitute an element of the crime, and as long as it did not take
16     the factors into account twice for sentencing.
17             I would now like to address the individual aggravating factors.
18     The first is the large number of victims.  The large number of victims
19     can be considered as an aggravating factor because persecution does not
20     require a large number of victims, so it is not an element of persecution
21     as such.  And it is the fact that the acts of the accused must be part of
22     a widespread or systematic attack does not mean that the large number of
23     victims cannot constitute an aggravating factor.  It is correct that the
24     widespread nature refers to the large-scale nature and the number of the
25     targeted persons, but it is not required that the accused himself is
 1     individually responsible for the large number of victims.  Here, however,
 2     he is as a co-perpetrator in the joint criminal enterprise responsible
 3     for having committed crimes against a large number of victims.
 4             Therefore, this additional factor can be taken into account as an
 5     aggravating factor, and it has not been considered by the Trial Chamber
 6     to determine the gravity, but clearly, as can be seen from paragraph 222,
 7     as an aggravating factor.
 8             With regard to the second aggravating factor, the launching of a
 9     meticulously planned attack.  We would like to note in addition to what
10     we have argued in our response brief that the Trial Chamber for the
11     second factor also used the word "launching," which indicates setting the
12     plan or attack in motion.  Both parts, the launching and the planning,
13     could be used as an aggravating factor.  Mr. Deronjic not only decided to
14     attack Glogova and participated in the planning of this operation but
15     also launched the attack in the sense that he ordered the attack to
16     start.
17             His involvement in the planning and the launching of the attack
18     do not constitute necessary elements of the crime of persecution or the
19     mode of liability of joint criminal enterprise.  Joint criminal
20     enterprise requires acting on the basis of a common plan, but it does not
21     require a phase of meticulous planning.  Moreover, as the Kvocka
22     Appellate Chamber has held, it only requires "a contribution," not even a
23     significant or substantial contribution.  And therefore this very
24     substantial contribution by the appellant could be considered as an
25     aggravating factor.
 1             And planning is not a necessary element of the systematic attack
 2     either.  A plan or policy is not a legal element of a crime against
 3     humanity.  And, moreover, it is not required that the defendant
 4     personally was involved in the planning.  His acts do only need to be
 5     part of the systematic attack.
 6             The appellant did not demonstrate that the launching of the
 7     meticulously planned attack was taken into account to determine the
 8     gravity of the crime and therefore failed to demonstrate that the Trial
 9     Chamber erred by considering it an aggravating factor.

16     aggravating factor.  Similar to the aggravating factor just discussed,
17     the ordering of additional torching constitutes another increase in the
18     personal involvement of the accused more than just a contribution to the
19     joint criminal enterprise, and therefore it was not a necessary element
20     of the offence.  And it is obvious from paragraph 202 of the judgement
21     that the Trial Chamber considered it as an aggravating factor and not as
22     part of the gravity.
23             Regarding the last aggravating factor, the special vulnerability
24     and defencelessness of the victim, we have set out in paragraphs 4.87 and
25     4.88 of the brief that the vulnerability of the victim is a factor which
 1     is accepted as aggravating in the jurisprudence of the Tribunal.  It does
 2     not constitute an element of the crime, as the jurisprudence of this
 3     Tribunal distinguishes between the status of being a civilian, which
 4     constitutes an element of the crime against humanity, and the special
 5     vulnerability of the victim, which does not constitute an element.  The
 6     cumulative effect of being disarmed and the false promise of safety put
 7     the victims here in a much more vulnerable position than the ordinary
 8     civilian.

11     concludes - I quote - "It has to be considered an aggravation of the
