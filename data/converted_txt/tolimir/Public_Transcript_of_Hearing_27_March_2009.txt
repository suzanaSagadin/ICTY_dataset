 4                           [The accused entered court]
 5                           --- Upon commencing at 9.04 a.m.
 6             JUDGE AGIUS:  Good morning.
 7             Madam Registrar, could you call the case, please.
 8             THE REGISTRAR:  Good morning, Your Honours.
 9             This is the case number IT-05-88-R.77.1, the contempt proceedings
10     against Dragan Jokic.
11             JUDGE AGIUS:  Thank you.
12             For the record, Ms. Isailovic is present, as well as the accused.
13             Mr. Jokic, before we proceed, I want to make sure you are
14     receiving interpretation in a language you understand.
15             THE ACCUSED: [Interpretation] Yes, Your Honour, I can hear the
16     interpretation in a language I understand.
17             JUDGE AGIUS:  The Trial Chamber is sitting today to deliver its
18     judgement in the contempt case of Dragan Jokic.  Jokic is charged with
19     contempt of the Tribunal, punishable under Rule 77 of the Rules.
20             For the purposes of this hearing, the Chamber will briefly
21     summarise the procedural history of the case, the applicable law, certain
22     submissions of the party, and the Chamber's findings.  We would like to
23     emphasise that this is but a summary and that the only authoritative
24     account of the Chamber's findings is the written confidential judgement
25     which fully describes the facts and the Chamber's reasoning.  There is
 1     also a public version of the judgement.
 2             We shall now give you the procedural history of the case.
 3             At the request of the Prosecution, Dragan Jokic was issued a
 4     subpoena to appear before the Chamber to give oral testimony in the case
 5     of Prosecutor versus Popovic et al.  The decision stated that there were
 6     good grounds to believe that Jokic's testimony could be material to the
 7     facts at issue.  The Chamber also ordered Jokic's testimony to be given
 8     in closed session and that he could be represented by Defence counsel in
 9     the course of his testimony.
10             On 31st October 2007, when asked to take the solemn declaration,
11     Jokic submitted that he was unable to testify.  Jokic was instructed to
12     provide a confidential and ex parte filing, explaining his refusal.
13             On November 1st, 2007, the Chamber, having read Jokic's filing,
14     decided that it did not justify a refusal to testify.  The Chamber then
15     held that it had reason to believe that Jokic may be in contempt of the
16     Tribunal, and decided, pursuant to Rule 77(D)(2) to prosecute the matter
17     itself.
18             Oral hearings were held on 19th November 2007, 10th of December,
19     2007, and 15th December 2008.  Jokic pleaded not guilty to the contempt
20     charges.  Additionally, he adduced seven exhibits, all of which were
21     admitted, and further led evidence from two witnesses, including a
22     Defence expert who submitted a report.
23             The Chamber instructed the Registrar to appoint a psychiatric
24     expert to examine Jokic.  Two opinions were requested from the Chamber
25     expert on the mental condition of Jokic, prior to and after the service
 1     of the subpoena, and as to Jokic's fitness to stand trial and his state
 2     of mind when he refused to testify.  Two expert reports were filed in
 3     response to these requests:  One dated the 16th of June, and the other
 4     the 18th of August, 2008.  Jokic was subsequently given an opportunity to
 5     cross-examine the Chamber expert.
 6             Jokic filed a final trial brief on 13th November 2008 and a
 7     supplemental brief, after the cross-examination of the Chamber expert, on
 8     12th January 2009.
 9             We shall now move to the relevant law as applied in this case.
10     While the Statute does not address contempt of court, the Appeals Chamber
11     has held that the power to pursue contempt proceedings is within the
12     inherent authority of the Tribunal and is reflected in Rule 77.
13     Rule 77(A)(1) specifically provides that:
14             "The Tribunal, in the exercise of its inherent powers, may hold
15     in contempt those who knowingly and willfully interfere with the
16     administration of justice, including any person, who being a witness
17     before a Chamber, contumaciously refuses or fails to answer a question."
18             From analysis that can be found in the judgement, the Chamber
19     concluded that Rule 77(A)(1) imposes criminal liability where a witness
20     knowingly and willingly interferes with the Chamber's administration of
21     justice by persistently refusing or failing to answer a question without
22     reasonable excuse while being a witness before a Chamber.
23             We shall now move to Jokic's submissions.
24             On a preliminary procedural point, Jokic contends that the
25     conduct of the proceedings in his contempt trial deprived him of his
 1     procedural rights under Rule 94 bis, as his right to a public hearing was
 2     violated by the order that the cross-examination of the Chamber expert
 3     was to be held in closed session, a violation which was not remedied by
 4     the Chamber's subsequent decision to open the proceedings to the public.
 5     The Chamber has already addressed the issue in a confidential decision
 6     filed on 18th November 2008 and during the hearing of 15th December 2008.
 7     Therefore, it finds no reason to readdress it in this judgement.
 8             On the substantive issues, Jokic does not challenge the fact that
 9     after being served with the subpoena, he appeared before the Chamber and
10     declared that he did not feel fit to testify.  Jokic submits that he did
11     not act with the specific intent required to interfere with the
12     administration of justice under Rule 77.  He avers that the evidence he
13     adduced at trial demonstrates that "there were reasonable excuses for the
14     fact that he did not testify before the Chamber."
15             Moreover, Jokic questions the qualifications of the Chamber
16     expert by compare his qualifications with the Defence experts.  He
17     further contends that the two Chamber expert reports corroborate the
18     conclusions of the Defence expert, except with regard to the issue of the
19     reliability of any information obtained from Jokic during testimony.
20             With regard to the second Chamber expert report, Dragan Jokic
21     submits that the Chamber delegated its inherent and exclusive authority
22     when it asked the Chamber expert to address Jokic's state of mind at the
23     time he refused to testify, and thereby violated the basic principles of
24     its judicial function.
25             We shall now move to the Chamber's findings with regard to the
 1     Chamber expert.
 2             The Chamber expert is a psychiatrist whose name is included in
 3     the Tribunal list of experts prepared pursuant to Rule 74 bis, and has
 4     previously appeared as a court expert.  He was assigned to this task
 5     following the Chamber's approval because of his professional expertise
 6     and experience.  The Chamber has no doubt that the Chamber expert is
 7     qualified to serve as an expert witness.  The fact that the Defence
 8     expert is also very experienced does not undermine the qualifications of
 9     the Chamber expert.
10             The role of an expert witness, in proceedings before the
11     Tribunal, is to provide Chambers with evidence based upon the expert's
12     specialised knowledge.  The Chamber is vested with the ultimate authority
13     and responsibility to adjudicate on the disputed matters, assess the
14     reliability of the evidence, including expert evidence, and to determine
15     what weight should be accorded to it.  Here, the appointment of the
16     Chamber expert assisted the Chamber's consideration and did not
17     compromise the fairness of the process.
18             It is not disputed that on 31st October and 1st November 2007,
19     Dragan Jokic repeatedly refused to testify in the case of Popovic et al.
20     He remained persistent in his refusal to testify, even after it was
21     explained to him that he could possibly be indicted for contempt.  We
22     shall now consider whether Jokic had reasonable excuse not to testify.
23             Witnesses summoned by subpoena are under a duty to testify.  It
24     is a basic principle of this judicial institution and goes to the heart
25     of the notion of justice.  It ensures that the evidence required for the
 1     proper administration of justice is available.  Only a few exceptions are
 2     available.  The decision on whether a proposed witness falls under these
 3     exceptions is to be exercised solely by the relevant Chamber and not by
 4     the witness.  Insofar as there may be concerns for the safety of
 5     witnesses or their families, other mechanisms have been developed by this
 6     Tribunal to address these concerns and to protect witnesses whose
 7     testimony may put them or their families in danger.  Concerns for the
 8     safety of witnesses or their relatives do not automatically override the
 9     duty to testify.  Here, protective measures were granted to Dragan Jokic
10     when he was subpoenaed.  The Chamber is, therefore, not convinced that
11     Jokic's security problems provide a reasonable excuse for his refusal to
12     testify.
13             Jokic further contends that he is afraid of falsely incriminating
14     someone.  His concern is supported, to a certain extent, by the Defence
15     expert.  A different conclusion was reached by the Chamber expert.  While
16     the Statute and Rules do not provide a standard on competency to testify
17     before the Tribunal, applying the plain meaning of the words "competency
18     to testify" requires that the proposed witness has a basic capacity to
19     understand the questions put to him and give rational and truthful
20     answers to those questions.  A witness's credibility and the reliability
21     of his answers can be questioned by the parties and must be assessed by
22     the Chamber.  A health condition does not automatically disqualify a
23     witness from testifying.  To undermine the capacity of a person to serve
24     as a witness, such a condition must have a substantial effect on his
25     credibility which consequently empties his evidence from having any
 1     probative value.  Applying this standard with reference to the evidence
 2     before it, the Chamber is not satisfied that Jokic lacked competency to
 3     testify.
 4             The Chamber will now turn to examine whether, by refusing to
 5     testify, Jokic knowingly and willfully interfered with the Chamber's
 6     administration of justice.
 7             After a careful reading of the Chamber and Defence expert
 8     reports, as well as hearing Jokic and observing his demeanour, the
 9     Chamber cannot accept the submission of the Defence expert that Jokic did
10     not make the decision not to testify.  What emerges from the evidence is
11     that Jokic's subpoena presented him with a dilemma and a choice.  He made
12     a conscious decision not to testify and understood the consequences of
13     his behaviour.  The possibility that he was motivated by other
14     considerations is not relevant.
15             Mr. Jokic, will you please stand up.
16                           [The accused stands up]
17             JUDGE AGIUS:  For the reasons summarised, which are articulated
18     in full in the decision, the Trial Chamber is convinced beyond reasonable
19     doubt that you, Mr. Dragan Jokic, by persistently refusing to testify
20     without a reasonable excuse in the case of the Prosecutor versus
21     Popovic et al, knowingly and willfully interfered with the Tribunal's
22     administration of justice.  You are, therefore, hereby found guilty of
23     contempt of the Tribunal, pursuant to Rule 77(A)(1).
24             We will now turn to sentencing.
25             For contempt, pursuant to Rule 77(G), the Chamber can impose a
 1     term of imprisonment of up to seven years, a fine not exceeding 100.000
 2     Euros, or both.  In deciding the punishment to be imposed, the Chamber
 3     has taken into consideration both the gravity of the conduct involved and
 4     the need to deter such conduct in the future.  The Chamber considers that
 5     you, Mr. Jokic, have committed a serious offence which goes to the
 6     essence of the notion of justice.  By your refusal to testify, you have
 7     deprived the Chamber of relevant evidence and acted against the interests
 8     of justice.  The Chamber, however, also takes into account your personal
 9     circumstances, as well as the fact that you have no past record of
10     interfering with the administration of justice before this Tribunal.  It
11     considers these factors as mitigating circumstances.
12             Mr. Jokic, the Trial Chamber sentences you to a single term of
13     imprisonment of four months.  This sentence shall be served consecutively
14     to any other prison term you are currently serving.
15             This is the summary of the judgement.  The hearing is now
16     adjourned.
