 1            The Appeals Chamber is here today to deliver its judgement on
 2    appeal in the case of the Prosecutor against Mr. Radislav Krstic.  Both
 3    the Prosecution and the Defence have appealed from the judgement issued by
 4    Trial Chamber I of this Tribunal on the 2nd of August, 2001.  This
 5    followed a trial which began here at The Hague on the 13th of March, 2000,
 6    and ran for just one year.
 7            The facts of this case relate mainly to events which took place in
 8    the town of Srebrenica around July 1995.  Srebrenica is located in Eastern
 9    Bosnia and Herzegovina.  It gave its name to a United Nations so-called
10    "safe area," which was intended as an enclave of safety set up to protect
11    its civilian population from the surrounding war.  Since July 1995,
12    however, Srebrenica has also lent its name to an event the horrors of
13    which form the background to this case.  The depravity, brutality, and
14    cruelty with which the VRS, the Bosnian Serb army, treated the innocent
15    inhabitants of the safe area are now well known and documented.  Bosnian
16    women, children, and elderly were removed from the enclave, and between
17    seven to eight thousand Bosnian Muslim men were systematically murdered.
18            Srebrenica is located in the area for which the Drina Corps of the
19    VRS was responsible.  Radislav Krstic was a general major in the VRS and
20    commander of the Drina Corps at the time the crimes at issue were
21    committed.  For his involvement in these events, the Trial Chamber found
22    Radislav Krstic guilty of genocide; persecution through murders, cruel and
23    inhumane treatment, terrorising the civilian population, forcible transfer
24    and destruction of personal property as crimes against humanity; and
25    murder as a violation of the laws or customs of war.  For these
 1    convictions, the Trial Chamber sentenced Mr. Krstic to 46 years'
 2    imprisonment.
 3            Following the practice of the Tribunal, I will not read out the
 4    text of the Appeal Judgement except for the disposition.  Instead, I will
 5    summarise the issues on appeal and the reasoning and findings of the
 6    Appeals Chamber so that you, Radislav Krstic, together with the public
 7    will know the reasons for the Appeals Chamber's decision.  I emphasise,
 8    however, that this is only a summary and that it does not in any way form
 9    part of the judgement of the Appeals Chamber.  The only authoritative
10    account of the findings of the Appeals Chamber is in the written judgement
11    which will be available today at the end of these proceedings.
12            Because of the importance of this appeal, the summary of the
13    judgement which I will now read is longer than our customary practice.  To
14    help you understand the judgement, let me point out in advance that there
15    are two cardinal issues on which the Appeals Chamber is unanimous.  The
16    first is the finding that genocide was committed in Srebrenica in 1995.
17    The second is the sentence which I will announce at the end of today's
18    proceedings.
19            In this case, the Prosecution bases its appeal on two grounds.
20    First, the Prosecution appeals the Trial Chamber's conclusions on
21    impermissibly cumulative convictions.  Secondly, the Prosecution appeals
22    the sentence imposed by the Trial Chamber.  It requests the imposition of
23    a life sentence on Radislav Krstic with a minimum of 30 years'
24    imprisonment.
25            The Defence bases its appeal on four grounds.  First, it appeals
 1    the conviction for genocide of Radislav Krstic, alleging that both factual
 2    and legal errors have been committed by the Trial Chamber.  Secondly, it
 3    appeals on the basis of various disclosure practices of the Prosecution
 4    which it alleges deprived Mr. Krstic of a fair trial.  Thirdly, the
 5    Defence alleges that the Trial Chamber made a number of other factual and
 6    legal errors.  And fourthly, it appeals the sentence handed down to
 7    Mr. Krstic, alleging that the Trial Chamber failed to adequately take into
 8    account the sentencing practice in the former Yugoslavia and to give
 9    sufficient weight to the mitigating circumstances.
10            I will now set out in more detail these grounds of appeal as well
11    as the Appeals Chamber's findings in respect of each.
12            1.  The Trial Chamber's Finding that Genocide Occurred in
13    Srebrenica.
14            The Defence appeals Radislav Krstic's conviction for genocide
15    committed against Bosnian Muslims in Srebrenica.  The Defence argues that
16    the Trial Chamber misconstrued the legal definition of genocide in two
17    ways.
18            (A) The Definition of The Part of the Group
19            First, Mr. Krstic contends that the Trial Chamber's definition of
20    the part of the national group he was found to have intended to destroy
21    was unacceptably narrow.
22            Article 4 of the Tribunal's Statute, like the Genocide Convention,
23    covers certain acts done with, and I quote, "intent to destroy, in whole
24    or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group as such."  The
25    targeted group identified in the indictment, and accepted by the Trial
 1    Chamber, was that of the Bosnian Muslims.  The Trial Chamber determined
 2    that the Bosnian Muslims were a specific, distinct national group, and
 3    therefore covered by Article 4.  This conclusion is not challenged on
 4    appeal.
 5            As is evident from the indictment, Mr. Krstic was not alleged to
 6    have intended to destroy the entire national group of Bosnian Muslims but
 7    only a part of that group.  The first question presented in this appeal is
 8    whether, in finding that Radislav Krstic had genocidal intent, the Trial
 9    Chamber defined the relevant part of the Bosnian Muslim group in a way
10    which comports with the requirements of both Article 4 and the Genocide
11    Convention.
12            It is well established that where a conviction for genocide relies
13    on the intent to destroy a protected group "in part," the part must be a
14    substantial part of that group.  The aim of the Genocide Convention is to
15    prevent the intentional destruction of entire human groups, and the part
16    targeted must be significant enough to have an impact on the group as a
17    whole.
18            Thus the intent requirement of genocide under Article 4 of the
19    Statute is satisfied where evidence shows that the alleged perpetrator
20    intended to destroy at least a substantial part of the protected group.
21    The determination of when the targeted part is substantial enough to meet
22    this requirement may involve a number of considerations.  The numeric size
23    of the targeted part of the group is the necessary and important starting
24    point, though it is not in all cases the ending point of the inquiry.  The
25    number of individuals targeted should be evaluated not only in absolute
 1    terms but also in relation to the overall size of the entire group.  In
 2    addition to the numeric size of the targeted portion, its prominence
 3    within the group can be a useful consideration.  If a specific part of the
 4    group is emblematic of the overall group or is essential to its survival,
 5    that may support a finding that the part qualifies as substantial within
 6    the meaning of Article 4.
 7            In this case, having identified the protected group as the
 8    national group of Bosnian Muslims, the Trial Chamber concluded that the
 9    part the VRS Main Staff and Radislav Krstic targeted was the Bosnian
10    Muslims of Srebrenica or the Bosnian Muslims of Eastern Bosnia.  The size
11    of the Bosnian Muslim population in Srebrenica prior to its capture by the
12    VRS forces in 1995 amounted to approximately 40.000 people.  Although this
13    population constituted only a small percentage of the overall Muslim
14    population of Bosnia and Herzegovina at the time, the importance of the
15    Muslim community of Srebrenica is not captured solely by its size.  As the
16    Trial Chamber explained, Srebrenica and the surrounding Central Podrinje
17    region were of immense strategic importance to the Bosnian Serb
18    leadership.  Without Srebrenica, the ethnically Serb state of Republika
19    Srpska they sought to create would remain divided into two disconnected
20    parts and its access to Serbia proper would be disrupted.  The capture and
21    ethnic purification of Srebrenica would, therefore, severely undermine the
22    military efforts of the Bosnian Muslim state to ensure its viability, a
23    consequence that the Muslim leadership fully realised and strove to
24    prevent.  Control over the Srebrenica region was consequently essential to
25    the goal of some Bosnian Serb leaders of forming a viable political entity

 1    in Bosnia as well as to the continued survival of the Bosnian Muslim
 2    people.  Because most of the Muslim inhabitants of the region had, by
 3    1995, sought refuge within the Srebrenica enclave, the elimination of that
 4    enclave would have accomplished the goal of purifying the entire region of
 5    its Muslim population.
 6            In addition, Srebrenica was important due to its prominence in the
 7    eyes of both the Bosnian Muslims and the international community.  The
 8    town of Srebrenica was the most visible of the "safe areas" established by
 9    the United Nations Security Council in Bosnia.  By 1995, it had received
10    significant attention in the international media.  In its Resolution
11    declaring Srebrenica a safe area, the UN Security Council announced that
12    it "should be free from armed attack or any other hostile act."  This
13    guarantee of protection was reaffirmed by the commander of the United
14    Nations Protection Force in Bosnia (UNPROFOR) and reinforced with the
15    deployment of UN troops.  The elimination of the Muslim population of
16    Srebrenica, despite the assurances given by the international community,
17    would have served as a potent example to all Bosnian Muslims of their
18    vulnerability and defenselessness in the face of Serb military forces.
19    The fate of the Bosnian Muslims of Srebrenica would be emblematic of all
20    Bosnian Muslims.
21            The Defence does not argue that the Trial Chamber's
22    characterisation of the Bosnian Muslims of Srebrenica as a substantial
23    part of the targeted group contravenes Article 4 of the Tribunal's
24    Statute.  Rather, the Defence contends that the Trial Chamber made a
25    further finding, concluding that the part Mr. Krstic intended to destroy
 1    was the Bosnian Muslim men of military age of Srebrenica.
 2            In making this argument, the Defence misunderstands the Trial
 3    Chamber's analysis.  The Trial Chamber stated that the part of the group
 4    Radislav Krstic intended to destroy was the Bosnian Muslim population of
 5    Srebrenica.  The men of military age who formed a further part of that
 6    group were not viewed by the Trial Chamber as a separate, smaller part
 7    within the meaning of Article 4.  Rather, the Trial Chamber treated the
 8    killing of the men of military age as evidence from which to infer that
 9    Radislav Krstic and some members of the VRS Main Staff had the requisite
10    intent to destroy all the Bosnian Muslims of Srebrenica, the only part of
11    the protected group relevant to Article 4 analysis.
12            The Trial Chamber's determination of the substantial part of the
13    protected was correct.  The Defence's appeal on this issue is dismissed.
14            (B) The Determination of the Intent to Destroy.
15            Secondly, the Defence submits that the Trial Chamber impermissibly
16    broadened the definition of genocide by concluding that an effort to
17    displace a community from its traditional residence is sufficient to show
18    that the alleged perpetrator intended to destroy a protected group.
19            The Appeals Chamber agrees that the Genocide Convention and
20    customary international law in general prohibit only the physical or
21    biological destruction of a human group.  The Trial Chamber expressly
22    acknowledged this limitation and eschewed any broader definition.  Given
23    that the Trial Chamber correctly identified the governing legal principle,
24    the Defence must discharge the burden of persuading the Appeals Chamber
25    that, despite having correctly stated the law, the Trial Chamber erred in
 1    applying it.
 2            The main evidence underlying the Trial Chamber's conclusion that
 3    the VRS forces intended to eliminate all the Bosnian Muslims of Srebrenica
 4    was the massacre by the VRS of all men of military age from that
 5    community.  The Trial Chamber based this conclusion on a number of factual
 6    findings which must be accepted as long as a reasonable Trial Chamber
 7    could have arrived at the same conclusions.  The Trial Chamber found that
 8    in executing the captured Bosnian Muslim men, the VRS did not
 9    differentiate between men of military status and civilians.  The Trial
10    Chamber also found that some of the victims were severely handicapped and,
11    for that reason, unlikely to have been combatants.  Moreover, as the Trial
12    Chamber emphasised, the term "men of military age" was itself a misnomer
13    for the group killed by the VRS included boys and elderly men normally
14    considered to be outside that range.  The Trial Chamber was also entitled
15    to consider the long-term impact that the elimination of seven to eight
16    thousand men from Srebrenica would have on the survival of that community.
17            In this case, the factual circumstances as found by the Trial
18    Chamber permit the inference that the killing of the Bosnian Muslim men
19    was done with genocidal intent.  The scale of the killing, combined with
20    the VRS Main Staff's awareness of the detrimental consequences it would
21    have for the Bosnian Muslim community of Srebrenica and with the other
22    actions the Main Staff took to ensure that community's physical demise is
23    a sufficient factual basis for the finding of specific genocidal intent.
24    The Trial Chamber found, and the Appeals Chamber endorses this finding,
25    that the killing was engineered and supervised by some members of the Main
 1    Staff of the VRS.  The fact that the Trial Chamber did not attribute
 2    genocidal intent to a particular official within the Main Staff does not
 3    undermine the conclusion that Bosnian Serb forces carried out genocide
 4    against the Bosnian Muslims.
 5            Among the grievous crimes this Tribunal has the duty to punish,
 6    the crime of genocide is singled out for special condemnation and
 7    opprobrium.  The crime is horrific in its scope; its perpetrators identify
 8    entire human groups for extinction.  Those who devise and implement
 9    genocide seek to deprive humanity of the manifold to richness its
10    nationalities, races, ethnicities and religions provide.  This is a crime
11    against all of humankind, its harm being felt not only by the group
12    targeted for destruction but by all of humanity.
13            The gravity of genocide is reflected in the stringent requirements
14    which must be satisfied before this conviction is imposed.  These
15    requirements - the demanding proof of specific intent and the showing that
16    the group was targeted for destruction in its entirety or in substantial
17    part - guard against a danger that convictions for this crime will be
18    imposed lightly.  Where these requirements are satisfied, however, the law
19    must not shy away from referring to the crime committed by its proper
20    name.  By seeking to eliminate a part of the Bosnian Muslims, the Bosnian
21    Serb forces committed genocide.  They targeted for extinction the 40.000
22    Bosnian Muslims living in Srebrenica, a group which was emblematic of the
23    Bosnian Muslims in general.  They stripped all the male Muslim prisoners,
24    military and civilian, elderly and young, of their personal belongings and
25    identification, and deliberately and methodically killed them solely on
 1    the basis of their identity.  The Bosnian Serb forces were aware when they
 2    embarked on this genocidal venture that the harm they caused would
 3    continue to plague the Bosnian Muslims.  The Appeals Chamber states
 4    unequivocally that the law condemns in appropriate terms the deep and
 5    lasting injury inflicted and calls the massacre at Srebrenica by its
 6    proper name:  Genocide.  These responsible will bear the stigma, and will
 7    serve as a warning to those who may in the future contemplate the
 8    commission of such a heinous act.
 9            In concluding that some members of the VRS Main Staff intended to
10    destroy the Bosnian Muslims of Srebrenica, the Trial Chamber did not
11    depart from the legal requirements of genocide.  The Defence appeal on
12    this issue is dismissed.
13            2.  Alleged Factual Errors Relating to Joint Criminal Enterprise
14    to Commit Genocide.
15            In the next ground of appeal, the Defence argues that even if the
16    finding of genocide was correct, the Trial Chamber erred in finding the
17    evidence sufficient to establish that Radislav Krstic was a member of a
18    joint criminal enterprise to commit genocide.
19            It is well established that the Appeals Chamber will not lightly
20    overturn findings of fact by a Trial Chamber.  However, as the Appeals
21    Chamber has stated, when the Prosecution realise upon proof of a state of
22    mind of an accused by inference, that inference must be the only
23    reasonable inference available on the evidence.
24            The Trial Chamber based its conclusion that Radislav Krstic shared
25    the intent of a joint criminal enterprise to commit genocide on inferences
 1    drawn from its findings with respect to his knowledge about the situation
 2    facing the Bosnian Muslim civilians after the take-over of Srebrenica, his
 3    interaction with the main participants of the joint criminal enterprise
 4    and the evidence it accepted as establishing that the resources and
 5    soldiers under his command and control were used to facilitate the
 6    killings.  Relying on this evidence, the Trial Chamber held that from the
 7    evening of 13 July 1995, Radislav Krstic intentionally participated in the
 8    joint criminal enterprise to execute the Bosnian Muslims of Srebrenica.
 9            In attacking this conclusion, the Defence advances three
10    arguments.  First, the Defence challenges the Trial Chamber's finding that
11    the Radislav Krstic assumed effective command over the Drina Corps and the
12    Drina Corps assets on 13 July 1995 and not later.  The arguments the
13    Defence now puts forward were extensively considered by the Trial Chamber.
14    The Trial Chamber, relying on eyewitness and documentary evidence, found
15    that the transfer of command to Radislav Krstic took place on 13 July.
16    The conclusions of the Trial Chamber are entirely reasonable and supported
17    by ample evidence.  The Defence has failed to demonstrate any error on the
18    part of the Trial Chamber, much less that the finding was one that no
19    reasonable Trial Chamber could have reached.
20            Secondly, the Defence argues that the Trial Chamber erred in
21    rejecting its claim that the executions were ordered and supervised
22    through a parallel chain of command maintained by the VRS security forces
23    over which Radislav Krstic did not have control.  According to the
24    Defence, this chain of command originated with General Mladic, went
25    through his security commander Colonel Beara of the VRS Main Staff, to

 4    commandeer the Drina Corps assets without consulting the Drina Corps
 5    Command.
 6            In support of these arguments, the Defence adduced as additional
 7    evidence three police reports made by Dragomir Vasic, chief of the Centre
 8    of Public Security at Zvornik, as well as the statement of a protected
 9    witness.  These reports do indeed lend support to the Defence's argument
10    that the MUP was acting on its own in carrying out the executions.  The
11    Trial Chamber, however, did not disagree.  In fact, it expressly refused
12    to, and I quote, "discount the possibility that the execution plan was
13    initially devised by members of the VRS Main Staff without consultation
14    with the Drina Corps Command generally and Radislav Krstic in particular,"
15    and that General Mladic may have directed the operation.  As the Trial
16    Chamber emphasised, however, the Main Staff lacked the resources to carry
17    out the execution on its own and, therefore, had to call on the resources
18    of the Drina Corps.  The Trial Chamber found, moreover, that the Drina
19    Corps Command knew about the Main Staff's requests and about the
20    subsequent use of the resources of the Drina Corps. These findings are
21    supported by two combat reports of 16 and 18 July 1995, signed by Radislav
22    Krstic as the commander of the Drina Corps, which the Prosecution
23    introduced as rebuttal material on appeal.
24            The Appeals Chamber is of the view that the Trial Chamber's
25    rejection of the Defence's argument as to parallel chain of command, even
 1    when examined in light of the Defence's additional evidence, is not one
 2    that no reasonable trier of fact could have made.
 3            Thirdly, the Defence challenges the finding of the Trial Chamber
 4    that Mr. Krstic directly participated in the executions and argues that
 5    even if the evidence before the Trial Chamber is sufficient to establish
 6    knowledge on his part about the genocide committed in Srebrenica, it is
 7    not sufficient to establish that he intended to commit genocide.
 8            Specifically, the Defence argues that the Trial Chamber erred in
 9    concluding that on 16 July 1995, members of the Bratunac Brigade
10    participated in the killing at Branjevo farm and the Pilica Cultural Dom.
11    The evidence of Drazen Erdemovic, a member of the 10th Sabotage Brigade
12    who participated in the killings at Branjevo farm, formed a crucial
13    factual basis for the conclusions of the Trial Chamber.  With respect to
14    the identification of the men from Bratunac, Mr. Erdemovic's evidence was
15    that he had heard that they were from Bratunac.  They were dressed in VRS
16    uniform, and they knew some of the Bosnian Muslim men of Srebrenica, which
17    suggested to him that they were local.  Mr. Erdemovic provided no evidence
18    that these men belonged to the Bratunac Brigade rather than to other
19    military units.  In fact, the only men Mr. Erdemovic positively identified
20    from photographs belonged to another military unit, one not commanded by
21    Mr. Krstic.  As such, the evidence of Mr. Erdemovic is insufficient to
22    establish that the men were from the Bratunac Brigade.
23            The insufficiency of Mr. Erdemovic's evidence is highlighted by
24    the testimony of the Prosecution military expert, Richard Butler.
25    Correcting evidence he gave during trial, Mr. Butler made clear during the
 1    appeals hearing that Mr. Erdemovic had never said that the men who were
 2    sent to assist in the executions were from the Bratunac Brigade, only that
 3    they were from the town of Bratunac.  Mr. Butler also confirmed that one
 4    of the men referred to by Mr. Erdemovic was identified as being a member
 5    of the Panteri Unit of the East Bosnia Corps.  In light of this fact,
 6    Mr. Butler now concluded that the men that arrived to assist in the
 7    executions did not belong to the Bratunac Brigade.
 8            Given the evidence relied upon by the Trial Chamber and the
 9    corrections made to that evidence by Mr. Butler, the finding of the Trial
10    Chamber that men from the Bratunac Brigade were dispatched by Mr. Krstic
11    to assist in the executions at Branjevo farm and Pilica Dom is one that no
12    reasonable trier of fact could have made.  The evidence fails to establish
13    the direct involvement of the Drina Corps in carrying out the executions
14    and as such cannot be relied upon as evidence of Radislav Krstic's direct
15    involvement in assisting the executions.
16            The evidence does, however, establish the involvement of the Drina
17    Corps personnel and assets in facilitating the executions.  The Trial
18    Chamber's finding on that point is supported by Mr. Erdemovic's evidence
19    that his unit was accompanied to the Branjevo Military Farm by two Drina
20    Corps military police officers and that military police officers wearing
21    the insignia of the Drina Corps escorted the buses of Bosnian Muslim
22    civilians to Branjevo Military Farm and supervised their unloading.
23            In light of these findings, the Appeals Chamber must determine
24    whether the Trial Chamber erred in finding that Radislav Krstic shared the
25    genocidal intent of a joint criminal enterprise to commit genocide against
 1    the Bosnian Muslims of Srebrenica.
 2            The case against Radislav Krstic was one based on circumstantial
 3    evidence, and the finding of the Trial Chamber was largely based upon a
 4    combination of circumstantial facts.  In convicting Mr. Krstic as a
 5    participant in a joint criminal enterprise to commit genocide, the Trial
 6    Chamber relied upon evidence establishing his knowledge of the intention
 7    on the part of General Mladic and other members of the VRS Main Staff to
 8    execute the Bosnian Muslims of Srebrenica, his knowledge of the use of
 9    personnel and resources of the Drina Corps to carry out that intention
10    given his command position, and upon evidence that Radislav Krstic
11    supervised the participation of his subordinates in carrying out those
12    executions.
13            The Trial Chamber found the contacts between Mr. Krstic and
14    General Mladic to be crucial to establishing Radislav Krstic's genocidal
15    intent.  The parties agreed that General Mladic was the main figure behind
16    the killings.  The Trial Chamber found that Mr. Krstic and General Mladic
17    were in constant contact throughout the relevant period.  The Trial
18    Chamber concluded that, and I quote:  "If General Mladic knew about the
19    killings, it would be natural for Mr. Krstic to know as well."
20            In reaching this conclusion, the Trial Chamber relied upon the
21    presence of Mr. Krstic at the second and third of three meetings convened
22    by General Mladic at the Hotel Fontana on 11 and 12 July 1995.  All three
23    meetings were attended by UNPROFOR leaders and Bosnian civilian leaders
24    selected by UNPROFOR.  The fate of the Bosnian Muslims following the fall
25    of Srebrenica was discussed in these meetings.  Based on his presence at
 1    two of these meetings, the Trial Chamber concluded that Radislav Krstic
 2    "was put on notice that the survival of the Bosnian Muslim population was
 3    in question following the take-over of Srebrenica."
 4            However, the most that Radislav Krstic's presence at these
 5    meetings establishes is his knowledge about General Mladic's decisions to
 6    transfer the population from Potocari to Muslim-held territories on buses
 7    and to screen the male members of this population for war criminals prior
 8    to transportation.  As the Trial Chamber acknowledged, the decision to
 9    screen was neither criminal nor unreasonable.  The Bratunac Brigade had
10    drawn up a list of over 350 suspected war criminals thought to be in the
11    Srebrenica area.  Although General Mladic also announced that the survival
12    of the population depended upon the complete surrender of the army of
13    Bosnia and Herzegovina, it is unlikely that General Mladic would be
14    disclosing his genocidal intent in the presence of UNPROFOR leaders and
15    foreign media or that those present at the meeting, including Mr. Krstic,
16    would have interpreted his comments in that light.  There was no evidence
17    to suggest that at this time Radislav Krstic knew about the intent on the
18    part of General Mladic to execute the Bosnian Muslim civilians who were to
19    be transferred.
20            Further, the Trial Chamber relied upon the presence of Radislav
21    Krstic in and around the Potocari compound for between one and two hours
22    in the afternoon of 12 July, at which time he was seen conferring with
23    other high military officers, including General Mladic, as evidence of his
24    growing knowledge that genocide would be committed.  The Trial Chamber
25    found that as a result of his presence there, Mr. Krstic "must have known
 1    of the appalling conditions facing the Bosnian Muslim refugees and the
 2    general mistreatment inflicted upon them by VRS soldiers on that day."
 3    The Trial Chamber further found that based on Mr. Krstic's presence at the
 4    White House compound in Potocari, he was aware that the segregated men
 5    were being detained in terrible conditions and were not being treated in
 6    accordance with accepted practice for war crime screening.  The Trial
 7    Chamber concluded that he must have realised, as did all other witnesses
 8    present around the compound, that the fate of these men was terribly
 9    uncertain but that he made no effort to clarify this with General Mladic
10    or anyone else.
11            However, the Trial Chamber also concluded that it was not until
12    the 13th of July, 1995, that the Dutch Battalion troops witnessed definite
13    signs that Bosnian Serbs were executing some of the Bosnian Muslim men who
14    had been separated; that it was not until all the Bosnian Muslim civilians

11            The Trial Chamber did not actually establish from Mr. Krstic's
12    contacts with General Mladic during the relevant period that Krstic in
13    fact learned of the intention to execute the Bosnian Muslims as a result
14    of these contacts.  The Trial Chamber's assertion was without a proper
15    evidentiary basis.  Without having established that Mr. Krstic knew of
16    that intention on the part of General Mladic, no reasonable Trial Chamber
17    could have made the further inference that Mr. Krstic shared that
18    intention.  This erroneous finding of the Trial Chamber casts doubt upon
19    its overall conclusion that Radislav Krstic shared genocidal intent.
20            The Appeals Chamber is of the view that all that the evidence can
21    establish is that Mr. Krstic was aware of the intent to commit genocide on
22    the part of some members of the Main Staff, and with that knowledge, he
23    did nothing to prevent the use of the Drina Corps personnel and resources
24    to facilitate those killings.  This knowledge on his part alone cannot
25    support an inference of genocidal intent.  Genocide is one of the worst
 1    crimes known to humankind, and its gravity is reflected in the stringent
 2    requirement of specific intent.  Convictions for genocide can be entered
 3    only where that intent has been unequivocally established.  There was a
 4    demonstrable failure by the Trial Chamber to supply adequate proof that
 5    Radislav Krstic possessed genocidal intent.  Mr. Krstic, therefore, is not
 6    guilty of genocide as a principal perpetrator.
 7            The issue that arises now is the level of Radislav Krstic's
 8    criminal responsibility in the circumstances as properly established.
 9    All of the crimes that followed the fall of Srebrenica occurred in the
10    Drina Corps zone of responsibility.  There was no evidence that the Drina
11    Corps devised or instigated any of the atrocities and the evidence
12    strongly suggested that the criminal activity was being directed by some
13    members of the VRS Main Staff under the direction of General Mladic.  At
14    the time the executions commenced, Mr. Krstic was engaged in preparing for
15    combat activities at Zepa and, from 14 July 1995 onwards, directing the
16    attack itself.
17            It was reasonable for the Trial Chamber to conclude that at least
18    as from 15 July 1995 Radislav Krstic had knowledge of the genocidal intent
19    of some of the members of the VRS Main Staff.  Radislav Krstic was aware
20    that the Main Staff had insufficient resources of its own to carry out the
21    executions and that without the use of the Drina Corps resources, the Main
22    Staff would not have been able to implement its genocidal plan.  Krstic
23    knew that by allowing Drina Corps resources to be used, he was making a
24    substantial contribution to the execution of the Bosnian Muslim prisoners.
25    Although the evidence suggests that Radislav Krstic was not a supporter of
 1    that plan, as commander of the Drina Corps he permitted the Main Staff to
 2    call upon Drina Corps resources and to employ those resources.  The
 3    criminal liability of Mr. Krstic is therefore more properly expressed as
 4    that of an aider and abettor to a joint criminal enterprise to commit
 5    genocide and not as that of a perpetrator.  This charge is fairly
 6    encompassed by the indictment which alleged that Radislav Krstic aided and
 7    abetted in the planning, preparation or execution of genocide against the
 8    Bosnian Muslims in Srebrenica.
 9            Mr. Krstic's responsibility is accurately characterised as aiding
10    and abetting genocide under Article 7(1) of the Statute and not as
11    complicity in genocide under Article 4(3)(e).  The charge of complicity
12    was also alleged in the indictment as Count 2.  The Trial Chamber did not
13    enter a conviction on this count, concluding that Krstic's responsibility
14    was that of a principal perpetrator.  There is an overlap between Article
15    4(3) as the general provision enumerating punishable forms of
16    participation in genocide and Article 7(1) as the general provision for
17    criminal liability which applies to all the offences punishable under the
18    Statute, including the offence of genocide.  There is support for a
19    position that Article 4(3) may be the more specific provision, lex
20    specialis, in relation to Article 7(1).  There is, however, also authority
21    indicating that modes of participation enumerated in Article 7(1) should
22    be read, as the Tribunal's Statute directs, into Article 4(3), and so the
23    principal -- the proper characterisation of such individual's criminal
24    liability is that of aiding and abetting genocide.
25            The Appeals Chamber concludes that the latter approach is the
 1    correct one in this case.  Article 7(1) of the Statute, which allows
 2    liability to attach to an aider and abettor, expressly applies that mode
 3    of liability to any, and I quote "crime referred to in Articles 2 to 5 of
 4    the present Statute," including the offence of genocide prohibited by
 5    Article 4.  Because the Statute must be interpreted with the utmost
 6    respect to the language used by the legislator, the Appeals Chamber may
 7    not conclude that the consequent overlap between Article 7(1) and Article
 8    4(3)(e) is a result of an inadvertence on the part of the legislator where
 9    another explanation, consonant with the language used by the Statute is
10    possible.  In this case, the two provisions can be reconciled because the
11    terms "complicity" and "accomplice" may encompass conduct broader than
12    that of aiding and abetting.  Given the Statute's express statement in
13    Article 7(1) that liability for genocide under Article 4 may attach
14    through the mode of aiding and abetting, Radislav Krstic's responsibility
15    is properly characterised as that of aiding and abetting genocide.
16            This, however, raises the question of whether, for liability of
17    aiding and abetting to attach, the individual charged need only possess
18    knowledge of the principal perpetrator's specific genocidal intent, or
19    whether he must share that intent.  The Appeals Chamber has previously
20    explained on several occasions that an individual who aids and abets a
21    specific intent offence may be held responsible if he assists the
22    commission of the crime knowing the intent behind the crime.  This
23    principle applies to the Statute's prohibition of genocide, which is also
24    an offence requiring a showing of specific intent.  The conviction for
25    aiding and abetting genocide upon proof that the defendant knew about the
 1    principal perpetrator's genocidal intent is permitted by the Statute and
 2    case law of the Tribunal.  The same approach is followed by many domestic
 3    jurisdictions, both common and civil law.
 4            The fact that the Trial Chamber did not identify individual
 5    members of the Main Staff of the VRS as the principal participants in the
 6    genocidal enterprise does not negate the finding that Radislav Krstic was
 7    aware of their genocidal intent.  A defendant may be convicted for having
 8    aided and abetted a crime which requires specific intent even where the
 9    principal perpetrators have not been tried or identified.  In Vasiljevic,
10    the Appeals Chamber found the accused guilty as an aider and abettor to
11    persecution without having had the alleged principal perpetrator on trial
12    and without having identified two other alleged co-perpetrators.
13    Accordingly, the Trial Chamber's conviction of Mr. Krstic as a participant
14    in a joint criminal enterprise to commit genocide is set aside and a
15    conviction for aiding and abetting genocide is entered instead.
16            The Appeals Chamber's examination of Radislav Krstic's
17    participation in the crime of genocide has implications for his criminal
18    responsibility for the murders of the Bosnian Muslim civilians under
19    Article 3, violations of the laws or customs of war, and for extermination
20    and persecution under Article 5, all of which arise from the executions of
21    the Bosnian Muslims of Srebrenica between 13 and 19 July 1995.  There was
22    no evidence that Mr. Krstic ordered any of these murders or that he
23    directly participated in them.  All the evidence establishes is that he
24    knew that those murders were occurring and that he permitted the Main
25    Staff to use personnel and resources under his command to facilitate them.
 1    In these circumstances, the criminal responsibility of Radislav Krstic is
 2    that of an aider and abettor to the murders, extermination and
 3    persecution, and not of a principal co-perpetrator.
 4            I turn to The Disclosure Practices of the Prosecution and Radislav
 5    Krstic's Right to a Fair Trial.
 6            The Defence has alleged, as a further ground for appeal, that the
 7    Prosecutor's disclosure practices violated Krstic's right to a fair trial
 8    under Article 20 of the Statute.  In its judgement, the Appeals Chamber
 9    has addressed each of the alleged practices which the Defence argues
10    resulted in prejudice to its case.  These are:  Withholding copies of
11    exhibits for tactical reasons; concealing a tape for later submission as
12    evidence in cross-examination, various violations of Rule 68 (disclosure
13    of exculpatory material), and the questionable credibility of the
14    testimony of two witnesses.
15            As a general proposition, where the Defence seeks a remedy for the
16    Prosecution's breach of its disclosure obligations under Rule 68, the
17    Defence must show, first, that the Prosecution has acted in violation of
18    its obligations under Rule 68; and secondly, that the Defence's case
19    suffered material prejudice as a result.  In other words, if the Defence
20    satisfies the Tribunal that there has been a failure by the Prosecution to
21    comply with Rule 68, the Tribunal, in addressing the aspect of appropriate
22    remedies, will examine whether or not the Defence has been prejudiced by
23    that failure to comply before considering what remedy is appropriate.
24            In this case, the Defence has failed to establish that it suffered
25    any prejudice as a result of the four alleged practices.  This ground of

11    68 bis (Failure to Comply with Disclosure Obligations).

14    sanctions on a defaulting party, proprio motu if necessary.

13            I turn to The Trial Chamber's Analysis of Cumulative Convictions.
14            In its first ground of appeal, the Prosecution challenges the
15    Trial Chamber's non-entry, as impermissibly cumulative, of Radislav
16    Krstic's convictions for extermination and persecution of the Bosnian
17    Muslims of Srebrenica between 13 and 19 July 1995, and for murder and
18    inhumane acts as crimes against humanity committed against the Bosnian
19    Muslim civilians in Potocari between 10 and 13 July 1995.  The Trial
20    Chamber disallowed convictions for extermination and persecution as
21    impermissibly cumulative with Mr. Krstic's conviction for genocide.  It
22    also concluded that the offences of murder and inhumane acts as crimes
23    against humanity are subsumed within the offence of persecution where
24    murder and inhumane acts form the underlying acts of the persecution
25    conviction.
 1            The Defence urges a dismissal of the Prosecution's appeal because
 2    the Prosecution does not seek an increase of the sentence in the event its
 3    appeal is successful.  However, the import of cumulative convictions is
 4    not limited to their impact on the sentence.  Cumulative convictions
 5    impose additional stigma on the accused and may imperil his eligibility
 6    for early release.  On the other hand, multiple convictions, where
 7    permissible, serve to describe the full culpability of the accused and to
 8    provide a complete picture of his criminal conduct.  The Prosecution's
 9    appeal is therefore admissible notwithstanding the fact that it does not
10    challenge the sentence.
11            The first vacated conviction that the Prosecution seeks to
12    reinstate is the conviction for extermination under Article 5 based on the
13    killing of the Bosnian Muslim men of Srebrenica.  The Trial Chamber held
14    that this conviction was impermissibly cumulative with Krstic's conviction
15    for genocide under Article 4, which was based on the same facts.  Both
16    statutory provisions contain a materially distinct element not contained
17    within the other.  Genocide requires proof of an intent to destroy, in
18    whole or in part, a specified protected group, while extermination
19    requires proof that the crime was committed as part of a widespread and
20    systematic attack against a civilian population.  The Trial Chamber's
21    conclusions that convictions for extermination under Article 5 and
22    genocide under Article 4 are impermissibly cumulative was accordingly
23    erroneous.
24            The Prosecution next argues that the Trial Chamber erred in
25    setting aside Mr. Krstic's conviction for persecution under Article 5 for
 1    the crimes resulting from the killings of Bosnian Muslims of Srebrenica as
 2    impermissibly cumulative with the conviction for genocide.  For the
 3    reasons just given with respect to the offence of extermination, the
 4    offence of genocide does not subsume that of persecution.  The Trial
 5    Chamber's conclusion to the contrary was erroneous.
 6            The Prosecution appeals the non-entry of two other convictions.
 7    The first is the conviction for murder as a crime against humanity of
 8    Bosnian Muslim civilians in Potocari.  The Trial Chamber set aside this
 9    conviction as impermissibly cumulative with the conviction for persecution
10    perpetrated through murder of these civilians.  The second is the
11    conviction for inhumane acts based on the forcible transfer of Bosnian
12    Muslim civilians to Potocari.  The Trial Chamber concluded that this
13    conviction was subsumed within the conviction for persecution based on the
14    inhumane acts of forcible transfer.  The Trial Chamber's conclusions
15    comport with the Appeals Chamber's holdings on these issues in Krnojelac
16    and in Vasiljevic.  The Prosecution's appeal on these issues is therefore
17    dismissed.
18            5.  Sentencing.
19            The Trial Chamber imposed on Radislav Krstic a single sentence of
20    46 years' imprisonment.  Both parties have appealed this sentence.
21            The Prosecution argues that the sentence imposed by the Trial
22    Chamber was inadequate because it failed properly to account either for
23    the gravity of the crimes committed or for the participation of Radislav
24    Krstic in those crimes; is inconsistent with ICTR jurisprudence in
25    comparable genocide cases; is based on Mr. Krstic's "palpably lesser
 1    guilt"; and because the Trial Chamber erred in finding that premeditation
 2    was inapplicable as an aggravating factor in this case.  Consequently, the
 3    Prosecution argues that the Trial Chamber imposed a sentence beyond its
 4    discretion and that the sentence should be increased to life imprisonment
 5    with a minimum of 30 years.
 6            The Defence argues that in imposing the sentence, the Trial
 7    Chamber failed to have due regard to the sentencing practice of the former
 8    Yugoslavia and the courts of Bosnia and Herzegovina, or to give adequate
 9    weight to what the Defence submits are mitigating circumstances.  The
10    Defence accordingly argues that the sentence should be reduced to a
11    maximum of 20 years.
12            The Appeals Chamber has emphasised that the imposition of a
13    sentence is a discretionary decision.  The Appeals Chamber has further
14    explained that only a "discernible error" in the exercise of that
15    sentencing discretion by the Trial Chamber may justify a revision of the
16    sentence.
17            For the reasons set out in the Appeal Judgement, the Appeals
18    Chamber has not found a discernible error on the part of the Trial Chamber
19    in imposing a sentence of 46 years on Radislav Krstic in respect of any of
20    the submissions put forward by either the Prosecution or the Defence.
21            However, the Appeals Chamber has reduced Mr. Krstic's
22    responsibility for genocide and for the murder of the Bosnian Muslim
23    civilians under Article 3 from that of a direct participant to that of an
24    aider and abettor.
25            In this light, an adjustment of the sentence is necessary.  The
 1    Appeals Chamber has the power to do so without remitting the matter to the
 2    Trial Chamber.
 3            The general sentencing principles applicable in this case
 4    include:  One, the gravity of the crime or crimes alleged; two, the
 5    general practice of prison sentences in the courts of the former
 6    Yugoslavia; three, the individual circumstances of the convicted person;
 7    and four, any aggravating or mitigating circumstances.
 8            Regarding the gravity of the crimes alleged, as the Appeals
 9    Chamber recently acknowledged in the Vasiljevic case, aiding and abetting
10    is a form of responsibility which generally warrants lower sentences than
11    responsibility as a co-perpetrator.  This principle has also been
12    recognised in the ICTR, in the law of the former Yugoslavia, and in many
13    national jurisdictions.  While Radislav Krstic's crime is undoubtedly
14    grave, the finding that he lacked genocidal intent significantly
15    diminishes his responsibility.  The same analysis applies to the reduction
16    of Mr. Krstic's responsibility for the murders as a violation of the laws
17    or customs of war committed between 13 and 19 of July 1995 in Srebrenica.
18    As such, the revision of Mr. Krstic's conviction to aiding and abetting
19    these two crimes merits a considerable reduction of his sentence.
20            The Appeals Chamber has also concluded that the Trial Chamber
21    erred in setting aside Radislav Krstic's convictions for Counts 3,
22    extermination as a crime against humanity, and 6, persecution as a crime
23    against humanity, as impermissibly cumulative with the conviction for
24    genocide.  The Appeals Chamber concluded, however, that Krstic's level of
25    responsibility with respect to these two offences was that of an aider and
 1    abettor and not of a principal perpetrator.  While these conclusions may
 2    alter the overall picture of Radislav Krstic's criminal conduct, the
 3    Prosecution did not seek an increase in sentence on the basis of these
 4    convictions.  The Appeals Chamber therefore does not take Mr. Krstic's
 5    participation in these crimes into account in determining the sentence
 6    appropriate to the gravity of his conduct.
 7            As regards the general sentencing practice of the courts of the
 8    former Yugoslavia, the Appeals Chamber has already explained that the
 9    Tribunal is not bound by such practice and may, if the interests of
10    justice so merit, impose a greater or lesser sentence than would have been
11    imposed under the legal regime of the former Yugoslavia.  In its
12    judgement, the Appeals Chamber has considered the sentencing practice of
13    the courts of the former Yugoslavia applicable in this case and has taken
14    those practices into account.  In particular, the law of the former
15    Yugoslavia provided that the sentence of a person who aided a principal
16    perpetrator to commit a crime can be reduced to a sentence less than the
17    one given to the principal perpetrator.
18            The Appeals Chamber believes that four additional factors must be
19    accounted for in mitigation of Mr. Krstic's sentence, namely:  One, the
20    nature of his provision of the Drina Corps assets and resources; two, the
21    fact that he had only recently assumed command of the Drina Corps during
22    combat operations; three, the fact that he was present in and around
23    Potocari for at most two hours; and four, his written order to treat
24    Muslims humanely.
