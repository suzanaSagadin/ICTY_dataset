     4      MR FENRICK:  Thank you very much, your Honour.
     5                The defence has submitted that the court should
     6          disregard the failure to punish provision in Article 7.3
     7          of the Statute.  It is the submission of the prosecution
     8          that the defence motion should be denied, because
     9          failure to punish liability does not infringe the nullum
    10          crimen principle and because individual criminal
    11          responsibility for failure to punish is an integral part
    12          of the well-established doctrine of command
    13          responsibility.
    14                The prosecution would emphasise the obvious, that
    15          this is an International Tribunal applying international
    16          criminal law, and that it has been created by the
    17          Security Council with a particular mandate, and the
    18          defence is submitting that the Tribunal should, in fact,
    19          disregard that mandate.
    20                It is the submission of the prosecution that the
    21          Tribunal most emphatically should apply all of the law
    22          in the Statute, and that there is, in fact, no sound
    23          reason whatsoever for disregarding Article 7.3, the
    24          failure to punish provision, and Article 7.3 of the
    25          Statute.
     1                International law consists of treaties, custom and
     2          the general principles of law recognised by civilised
     3          nations.  The nullum crimen principle is not violated if
     4          an act or omission is, at the time it is committed, a
     5          criminal offence under applicable national law, or a
     6          criminal offence under any of the components of
     7          international law, custom applicable treaties or general
     8          principles of law.
     9                Further, it is not essential that the act or
    10          omission be classified as the same crime at both time of
    11          commission and time of prosecution.  It is sufficient if
    12          the act or omission is a crime at the time of
    13          commission, and a crime at the time of prosecution.
    14                It is the submission of the prosecutor that
    15          personal criminal liability is imposed on a commander
    16          for his failure to punish subordinates by both customary
    17          law and applicable treaty law, although, strictly
    18          speaking, it is only necessary to establish such
    19          liability under either customary or applicable treaty
    20          law to comply with the nullum crimen principle.
    21                The doctrine of command responsibility, whereby
    22          commanders can be held criminally responsible for the
    23          acts of their subordinates in certain circumstances, is
    24          well-established in customary law.  The doctrine has two
    25          aspects:  first, a commander may be held liable for
     1          offences which he has ordered to be committed; and
     2          secondly, in certain circumstances a commander may be
     3          held liable for the actions of his subordinates where he
     4          fails to exercise his duty to control his subordinates.
     5                The judgments in the Yamashita case and that by
     6          the International Military Tribunal for the Far East
     7          clearly indicated that commanders are held responsible
     8          for their failure to carry out a wide-ranging duty to
     9          control their subordinates.  This duty includes a
    10          requirement to take practicable measures to punish
    11          offending subordinates.
    12                As opposed to the view asserted by the defence, it
    13          is the submission of the prosecutor that existing case
    14          law does provide support for the application of failure
    15          to punish liability, and other sources of customary law,
    16          such as national military manuals, provide an additional
    17          basis for this assertion.
    18                The defence draws an artificial and inappropriate
    19          distinction between failure to punish as an abstract
    20          concept and the commander's duty to prevent the
    21          occurrence, or recurrence, of offences.
    22                In several cases, accused were convicted for
    23          failure to punish, and part of the rationale for failure
    24          to punish constituting an offence was that the
    25          commander, as part of his general duty to control his
     1          troops, had a duty to prevent the recurrence of
     2          offences.
     3                Punishment is intended to deter the commission of
     4          future offences.  When, however, a commander is held
     5          culpable for failure to punish, he is held culpable for
     6          offences which have already occurred, not for future
     7          offences.  As examples, in the High Command case, Field
     8          Marshal von Kuechler was held responsible for the
     9          illegal execution of Red Army soldiers, which occurred
    10          before and after he took up command, because reports of
    11          these executions were made to his headquarters after he
    12          took up command and he took no punitive action.
    13                In the Hostages case, General Dehner was held
    14          responsible for the unlawful killing of hostages and
    15          reprisal prisoners ordered by his subordinates, because
    16          he failed to punish these subordinates, and in the Tokyo
    17          trial, General Tojo was held responsible for the death
    18          of prisoners on the Batan death march, because he failed
    19          to punish subordinates who ordered the march.
    20                These are some cases which provide support for
    21          failure to punish liability.  The prosecutor must
    22          emphasise, however, that the case law for any punishment
    23          of international crimes is relatively spartan.  This is
    24          not the equivalent of a domestic jurisdiction, where we
    25          have thousands upon thousands of cases.  The cases that
     1          we have are relatively few.  They occur at great
     2          intervals.  In fact, the defence had suggested that they
     3          could find no examples of an International Tribunal
     4          holding someone criminally responsible for failure to
     5          punish.  The prosecution submits that we have provided
     6          these examples, but, if one argued that simple lack of
     7          cases was an adequate basis for deciding that one should
     8          never punish or should never prosecute something, one
     9          might also point out that, until this Tribunal was
    10          established, no court has ever prosecuted for a grave
    11          breach of the Geneva Conventions, but it is submitted
    12          that no one would ever suggest that grave breaches of
    13          the Geneva Conventions were not legitimate international
    14          crimes.
    15                National law of war manuals also constitute
    16          evidence of state practice and provide important
    17          national assessments of customary international law.
    18          Two leading national law war manuals dating back to the
    19          1950s, those of the British and American armies, assert
    20          that commanders are personally liable for failure to
    21          punish.  The more recent German manual and the 1988
    22          Yugoslav Regulations contain similar provisions.  The
    23          Yugoslav Regulations are of particular significance.
    24          They state in part:
    25                "The commander who knows that the violations of
     1          the laws of war took place and did not charge those
     2          responsible for the violations is personally
     3          responsible.  In case he is not authorised to charge
     4          them and he did not report them to the authorised
     5          military commander, he would also be personally
     6          responsible."
     7                The defence read a portion of the Yugoslav
     8          Regulations.  We have perhaps read a slightly different
     9          portion.  It is, however, submitted that no one could
    10          read the portion that we have just read without coming
    11          to a conclusion that a commander under the Yugoslav
    12          Regulations could be held criminally responsible for
    13          failure to punish.
    14                Further, General Blaskic, as a former professional
    15          officer in the Yugoslav army, could be expected to be
    16          intimately familiar with this particular provision.  It
    17          is submitted by the prosecutor that personal criminal
    18          liability is imposed on a commander for his failure to
    19          punish subordinates by customary international law.
    20                In addition, however, to customary law, the
    21          international law doctrine of command responsibility is
    22          applicable to conflicts in the territory of the former
    23          Yugoslavia, as a result of treaty obligations,
    24          specifically as a result of the 1977 Geneva Protocol 1
    25          additional to the Geneva Conventions of 12th August
     1          1949.
     2                This protocol applied throughout the territory of
     3          the former Yugoslavia throughout the conflict, and a
     4          number of articles in that protocol, specifically
     5          Articles 43, 86 and 87, all essentially to a degree
     6          codify the doctrine of command responsibility, and also
     7          incorporate a specific provision relating to criminal
     8          liability for failure to punish.
     9                The negotiating history of Article 87 in
    10          particular clearly indicates it was intended to
    11          elaborate upon the obligations referred to in Article 86
    12          in so far as the appropriate superiors were also
    13          military commanders.
    14                Additional support for the position that Articles
    15          86 and 87 assign criminal responsibility for failure to
    16          punish is found in the 1988 Yugoslav Regulations, which
    17          state in part:
    18                "The commander who knows that the violations of
    19          the law of war took place and did not charge those
    20          responsible for the violations is personally
    21          responsible."
    22                It is submitted that the prosecution has
    23          adequately and fully established that the failure to
    24          punish provision in Article 7.3 does not in any way
    25          violate the nullum crimen principle.
     1                Further, it is submitted by the prosecution that,
     2          as a matter of substantive policy, the Tribunal should
     3          apply the doctrine of failure to punish liability.  The
     4          fundamental premise of the doctrine of command
     5          responsibility is that a commander has a duty to control
     6          his subordinates, and to take all practicable measures
     7          to ensure that they comply with international
     8          humanitarian law.
     9                The Tokyo trial in particular emphasises the need
    10          to have a system in place to ensure law compliance, and
    11          to ensure the continuous and efficient working of the
    12          system.  If the commander does not have a duty to
    13          enforce the law when his subordinates commit violations,
    14          both the doctrine and the disciplinary system break
    15          down.  The commander has a duty to punish in order to
    16          deter future potential violators.  A failure to punish
    17          condones the offence.
    18                Discussing the trials of German war criminals
    19          after the Second World War, the defence suggests on
    20          several occasions that failure to punish was only an
    21          element of another crime; for instance, the failure to
    22          prevent the recurrence of crimes.  However, a reading of
    23          the Secretary-General's report pursuant to Security
    24          Council Resolution 808 containing the Tribunal's Statute
    25          clearly shows that the object and purpose behind
     1          Article 7.3 is precisely to prevent the commission of
     2          further crimes and to deter future unlawful behaviour.
     3                In paragraph 57 of the report, it is stated that,
     4          while a commander should be held responsible for the
     5          crimes committed pursuant to an order given by him, he
     6          should also be held responsible for failure to prevent a
     7          crime or to deter the unlawful behaviour of his
     8          subordinates.
     9                This imputed responsibility, or criminal
    10          negligence, is engaged if the person in superior
    11          authority knew or had reason to know that his
    12          subordinates were about to commit, or had committed
    13          crimes, and yet failed to take the necessary and
    14          reasonable steps to prevent or repress the commission of
    15          such crimes, or to punish those who had committed them.
    16                A distinction between failure to punish and
    17          failure to prevent is artificial.  The failure to punish
    18          and the failure to prevent recurrence of criminal
    19          behaviour are intricately connected and a failure to
    20          punish subordinates who have committed crimes would, in
    21          most circumstances, also constitute a failure to prevent
    22          future crimes, and a failure to control one's own
    23          forces, the overarching duty of the military commander.
    24                The defence has indicated its concern that the
    25          principle contained in Article 7.3 is in violation of
     1          some unidentified norm of customary international law.
     2          It is the submission of the prosecution that one of the
     3          organisations which is most concerned about the rights
     4          of the accused under all circumstances is Amnesty
     5          International.  Amnesty International produced a report
     6          entitled "The International Criminal Court making the
     7          right choices" in January of 1997.  I quote from page 69
     8          of that Amnesty International report:
     9                "A superior who fails to take measures to punish
    10          crimes which the superior knows, or has reasons to know,
    11          the subordinate has committed will send a message that
    12          such crimes can be committed with impunity.  A superior
    13          who fails to take measures to prevent such crimes may
    14          justly be held responsible for such crimes."
    15                Article 7.3 of the Statute clearly imposes
    16          criminal liability for failure to punish.  The Tribunal
    17          should not hesitate to use this provision in appropriate
    18          circumstances.  The prosecutor submits that the defence
    19          motion should be denied.  Thank you.

    13          particularly as to the conduct of the accused, and the
