     1                                     Wednesday, 29th October, 1997
     2      (12.00 pm)
     3      JUDGE CASSESE:   Good morning.  May I ask the Registrar to
     4          call the case, please?
     5      THE CLERK OF THE COURT: Case number 95-14-AR-108 bis,
     6          Prosecutor versus Blaskic.
     7      MS ARBOUR:   Good morning, I am Louise Arbour, I am the
     8          Prosecutor, and with me is Mr Mark Hayman.
     9      MR MULJACIC:   Good morning, I am the Croatian Ambassador
    10          here in the Kingdom of the Netherlands.  To my left is,
    11          Orsat Miljenic, First Secretary.
    12      JUDGE CASSESE:   I have been informed by counsel for General
    13          Blaskic that they have waived their right to attend the
    14          delivery of the judgement of the Appeals Chamber and that
    15          he appears unrepresented on this occasion.  May I ask
    16          General Blaskic whether he can hear me?
    17      THE DEFENDANT:   Good morning, Messrs Presidents, I can hear
    18          you well and I can understand you well.
    19      JUDGE CASSESE:   Thank you very much indeed.  You can be
    20          seated.
    21      THE DEFENDANT:   Thank you.
    22      JUDGE CASSESE:   I should like to say that Judge Li and
    23          Judge Vohrah I am afraid are unable to attend this
    24          hearing and have asked me to extend their apologies.
    25                You may remember that at the close of the hearing
     1          of the 22nd September I promised to deliver this
     2          judgement within three weeks.  Unfortunately this has
     3          proved impossible.  It was too great a task, as the
     4          issues with which we have grappled are difficult and
     5          complex.  I therefore apologise for not keeping my
     6          word.  There is, however, another reason for our failure
     7          to deliver on time, one reason which some may feel
     8          excuses this delay.  My colleagues and I are pleased to
     9          be able to render this judgement of the Appeals Chamber
    10          both in English and French, the two official languages
    11          of the International Tribunal.  This significant
    12          achievement I should note has been made possible by the
    13          dedication and competence of our translators who have
    14          provided an excellent French translation.  May I add
    15          that this is the first time that the judgement, a
    16          judgement of the International Criminal Tribunal is
    17          delivered simultaneously in both languages.
    18                I will now read slowly a short summary of our
    19          judgement.  As you know, this matter has come before the
    20          Appeals Chamber by way of an appeal by the Republic of
    21          Croatia against the decision of Trial Chamber II on 18th
    22          July, 1997, upholding the issuance to Croatia and its
    23          Minister of Defence of a subpoena duces tecum and this
    24          subpoena duces tecum was issued by Judge McDonald on
    25          15th January, 1997, in connection with the case of
     1          Prosecutor versus Blaskic which is currently being heard
     2          by Trial Chamber I.  In oral submissions to the Appeals
     3          Chamber on the 22nd September, and in its written
     4          pleadings, the Republic of Croatia has questioned the
     5          power of our International Tribunal to issue orders
     6          designated as subpoena duces tecum to Croatia, or to any
     7          other sovereign State, and also to high state
     8          officials.  After careful deliberation the Appeals
     9          Chamber has unanimously reached a decision on this
    10          matter, and has issued a judgement which I shall now
    11          briefly summarise.
    12                The Appeals Chamber has first of all dealt with
    13          two preliminary issues, namely the legal meaning of the
    14          term "subpoena", and whether the question of legal
    15          remedies in case of non-compliance with orders or
    16          subpoenas was ripe for consideration by the Appeals
    17          Chamber.
    18                As for the first issue, namely the legal meaning
    19          of the term "subpoena", the Appeals Chamber holds that
    20          this term should be construed as referring only and
    21          exclusively to binding orders addressed by the
    22          International Tribunal under threat of penalty to
    23          individuals, acting in their private capacity.  With
    24          respect to the other issue, namely whether the question
    25          of legal remedies is ripe for consideration, the Appeals
     1          Chamber has found that it is inappropriate to resort to
     2          the doctrine of ripeness in these proceedings.  Whatever
     3          the merits of this domestic law doctrine, a doctrine
     4          upheld, as you know, in the USA, it appears to the
     5          Appeals Chamber to be inapposite to transpose it into
     6          international criminal proceedings.  Furthermore, it is
     7          wrong to suggest that the issue of legal remedy is not
     8          ripe for judicial consideration given the extent to
     9          which it has been debated by the parties before the
    10          Appeals Chamber.
    11                After consideration of these preliminary issues,
    12          the Appeals Chamber has reached an unanimous decision on
    13          all of the four matters which it has identified as the
    14          core issues in this dispute.
    15                The first question is whether the International
    16          Tribunal is empowered to issue subpoena and binding
    17          orders to States, to sovereign States.  Here the Appeals
    18          Chamber holds that subpoenae, in the sense of injunctions
    19          accompanied by threat of penalty, cannot, cannot be
    20          issued to States.  The International Tribunal does not
    21          possess any power to take enforcement measures against
    22          sovereign States.  Such powers cannot be regarded as
    23          inherent to the functions of an international judicial
    24          body such as our Tribunal.
    25                Under current international law, States can only
     1          be the subject of countermeasures taken by other States
     2          or of sanctions visited upon them by the international
     3          organised community, that is to say the United Nations
     4          or other intergovernmental organisations.
     5                Had the drafters of our Statute intended to vest
     6          the International Tribunal with such a power contrary to
     7          current international law, they would have expressly
     8          provided for it.
     9                By contrast, binding orders or requests can be
    10          addressed to States.  Article 29 of our Statute requires
    11          all States to cooperate and lend judicial assistance to
    12          the International Tribunal.  This obligation is
    13          reiterated in paragraph 4 of Security Council resolution
    14          827(1993).  Its binding force thus derives from the
    15          provisions of Chapter VII and Article 25 of the United
    16          Nations Charter, and from the Security Council
    17          resolution adopted pursuant to those provisions of the
    18          UN Charter.
    19                This exceptional legal basis accounts for the
    20          novel and indeed unique power granted to the
    21          International Tribunal to issue orders to sovereign
    22          States.  Article 29 of our Statute sets out a clear
    23          obligation which is incumbent on every Member State of
    24          the United Nations, every member State of United
    25          Nations.  Article 29 also posits a community interest in
     1          its observance.
     2                As for the question of legal remedies available in
     3          case of non-compliance by a State with a binding order
     4          or request, the Appeals Chamber holds that it is for the
     5          International Tribunal's parent body, the Security
     6          Council, to impose sanctions, if any, against a
     7          recalcitrant State, under the conditions provided for in
     8          Chapter VII of the United Nations Charter.
     9                However, our Tribunal is endowed with the inherent
    10          power to make a judicial finding concerning a State's
    11          failure to observe the provisions of the Statute or the
    12          Rules.  It also has the power to report this judicial
    13          finding to the Security Council.  Where such a report is
    14          made, the Security Council will decide whether or not to
    15          take any action against the recalcitrant State.
    16                In addition, subject to certain conditions, each
    17          Member State of the United Nations may act upon the
    18          communal legal interest in the observance of this
    19          international obligation laid down in Article 29.  A
    20          collective response through other intergovernmental
    21          organisations may also be envisaged, again subject to
    22          certain strict conditions.
    23                The second question the Appeals Chamber has
    24          considered is the question of whether the International
    25          Tribunal is empowered to issue subpoenae and/or binding
     1          orders to States or officials.  The Appeals Chamber
     2          holds that the International Tribunal may not address
     3          subpoena to state officials acting in their official
     4          capacity.  Such officials are mere instruments of a
     5          State, and their official action can only be attributed
     6          to the State.  It is a well-established rule of
     7          customary international law that they cannot be the
     8          subject of sanctions or penalties for conduct that is
     9          not private but undertaken on behalf of that State.
    10                The Appeals Chamber also finds that both under
    11          general international law and our Statute itself Judges
    12          or Trial Chambers cannot address binding orders to
    13          States or officials.  It follows that if a Judge or a
    14          Chamber intends to order the production of documents,
    15          the seizure of evidence, the arrest of suspects, et
    16          cetera, being acts involving action by a State, its
    17          organs or officials, the Judge or the Chamber must turn
    18          to the relevant State for assistance.
    19                The third question tackled by the Appeals Chamber
    20          is that of whether the International Tribunal may issue
    21          binding orders to individuals acting in their private
    22          capacity.  The Appeals Chamber finds that the
    23          International Tribunal can issue binding orders in the
    24          form of subpoenae, that is under threat of penalty to
    25          individuals acting in their private capacity.  This
     1          power derives from the general object and purpose of the
     2          Statute, as well as the role that the International
     3          Tribunal is called upon to play thereunder.
     4                These factors, as well as the relevant provisions,
     5          confer on the International Tribunal an incidental or
     6          ancillary jurisdiction over individuals other than those
     7          whom the Tribunal may prosecute and try under its
     8          primary jurisdiction.  These are individuals who may be
     9          of assistance in the task of dispensing criminal justice
    10          entrusted to our Tribunal.
    11                As for the legal remedies for non-compliance by
    12          individuals, the Appeals Chamber is of the opinion that
    13          the Tribunal should normally turn to the relevant
    14          national authorities to seek remedies or sanctions for
    15          non-compliance by an individual with an order or
    16          subpoenae issued by a Judge or a Trial Chamber.  Legal
    17          remedies or sanctions put in place by the national
    18          authorities themselves are indeed more likely to work
    19          effectively and expeditiously.
    20                However, allowance should be made for cases where
    21          resort to national remedies or sanctions is not
    22          feasible.  In such cases the remedies available to the
    23          International Tribunal should be utilised.  These
    24          remedies range from a general power to hold individuals
    25          in contempt of the International Tribunal, utilising the
     1          inherent contempt power rightly mentioned by the Trial
     2          Chamber to the specific contempt power provided for in
     3          Rule 77 of our Rules of Procedure and Evidence.
     4                The fourth and last question considered by the
     5          Appeals Chamber is that of national security concerns.
     6          The Appeals Chamber recognises that customary
     7          international rules do protect the national security of
     8          States by prohibiting every State from interfering with,
     9          or intruding into, the domestic jurisdiction, including
    10          national security matters of other States.  However, the
    11          Statute imposes upon all States the obligation among
    12          other things to provide to the International Tribunal
    13          documents germane to the conduct of trials.  This is an
    14          extremely and innovative sweeping obligation which
    15          clearly derogates from general international law.  Had
    16          the founding fathers intended to place restrictions upon
    17          these powers of the International Tribunal, they would
    18          have done so expressly.
    19                This Chamber has been persuaded by the submissions
    20          of the Prosecutor that to grant States a blanket right
    21          to withhold documents necessary for a trial might
    22          jeopardise the very function of the International
    23          Tribunal, and, as she put it, defeat its essential
    24          object and purpose.  Recognition of such a right to use
    25          national security concerns to withhold documents could
     1          lead to the stultification of international criminal
     2          proceedings as they might prove crucial to the
     3          determination of innocence or guilt.  The very raison
     4          d’être of the International Tribunal would be
     5          undermined.
     6                Nevertheless, the Appeals Chamber emphasises that
     7          the International Tribunal should be alert to legitimate
     8          national security concerns of States.  The Trial Chamber
     9          rightly indicated that the best way of reconciling the
    10          conflicting interests of the International Tribunal and
    11          the State would be to hold in camera ex parte
    12          proceedings in order to scrutinise the validity of its
    13          national security claims, of States' national security
    14          claims.
    15                The Appeals Chamber, whilst in broad agreement with
    16          the Trial Chamber, suggests in addition several
    17          practical methods and procedures to guide this process
    18          of scrutiny.  These methods and procedures include
    19          consideration of the degree of bona fide co-operation
    20          and assistance lent by the relevant States to our
    21          Tribunal, as well as the general attitude of the State
    22          vis-à-vis the International Tribunal, whether it is
    23          opposed to the fulfilment of its functions or instead
    24          consistently supports and assists our Tribunal.
    25                The majority of the Appeals Chamber also suggests
     1          that the relevant documents could be submitted through
     2          the scrutiny of one Judge, one Judge of the Trial
     3          Chamber, designated by the Trial Chamber itself.  Judge
     4          Karibi-Whyte has appended a separate opinion on this
     5          question of the scrutiny of the national security
     6          concerns by the International Tribunal.  It is his view
     7          that such scrutiny should be undertaken by the whole
     8          Trial Chamber and not by a single Judge.
     9                Of course it will be for the relevant Trial
    10          Chamber to decide whether to adopt any of the methods or
    11          procedures suggested by our Appeals Chamber, or to
    12          provide for other practical arrangements or protective
    13          measures if need be, in consultation with the interested
    14          States.
    15                I shall now read the disposition of our judgement.
    16          It reads as follows:
    17                "The Appeals Chamber unanimously finds:
    18                "(1) That the International Tribunal is empowered
    19          to issue binding orders and requests to States which are
    20          obliged to comply with them pursuant to Article 29 of
    21          the Statute, and that in case of non-compliance a Trial
    22          Chamber may make a specific judicial finding to this
    23          effect and request the President of the International
    24          Tribunal to transmit it to the United Nations Security
    25          Council.
     1                "(2) The Appeals Chamber unanimously finds that
     2          the International Tribunal may not address binding
     3          orders under Article 29 to state officials acting in
     4          their official capacity.
     5                "(3) The Appeals Chamber unanimously finds that
     6          the International Tribunal may summon, subpoena or
     7          address other binding orders to individuals acting in
     8          their private capacity, and that in case of
     9          non-compliance either the enforcement measures provided
    10          for in the relevant State may be taken or the
    11          International Tribunal may instigate contempt
    12          proceedings.
    13                "(4) The Appeals Chamber unanimously finds that
    14          States are not allowed, are not allowed on the claim of
    15          national security interests to withhold documents and
    16          other evidential material requested by the International
    17          Tribunal.  However, practical arrangements may be
    18          adopted by a Trial Chamber to make allowance for
    19          legitimate and bona fide concerns of States.
    20                "(5) The Appeals Chamber unanimously decides to
    21          quash the subpoena duces tecum issued by Judge McDonald
    22          and reinstated it by Trial Chamber II addressed to Croatia
    23          and the Croatian Defence Minister Mr Gojko Susak it
    24          being understood that the Prosecutor is at liberty to
    25          submit to the appropriate Chamber, being Trial Chamber
     1          I, a request for a binding order addressed to Croatia
     2          alone."
     3                That is all.  The hearing is therefore adjourned.
