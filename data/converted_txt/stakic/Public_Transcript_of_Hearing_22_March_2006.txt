10            As the registrar announced, the case on our agenda is Prosecutor
11    versus Milomir Stakic.  In accordance with the Scheduling Order issued on
12    16 March 2006, today the Appeals Chamber will deliver its judgement.  As
13    stated in that order, this hearing for the delivery of the judgement is
14    taking place pursuant to Rule 15 bis (A) of the Tribunal's Rules of
15    Procedure and Evidence, in the absence of one of the Judges of the Appeals
16    Chamber, Judge Andresia Vaz, who is unavailable for reasons of authorised
17    Tribunal business.
18            Following the practice of the International Tribunal, I will not
19    read out the text of the judgement except for the disposition.  Instead, I
20    will summarise the issues on this appeal and the findings of the Appeals
21    Chamber.  I emphasise that this summary is not part of the written
22    judgement, which is the only authoritative account of the Appeals
23    Chamber's rulings and reasons.  Copies of the written judgement will be
24    made available to the parties at the conclusion of this hearing.
25            This case concerns the appellant's role in the events that
 1    occurred in the municipality of Prijedor, Bosnia and Herzegovina, from
 2    30 April 1992 until 30 September 1992.  These events, which are set out in
 3    detail in the trial judgement, followed the 30 April 1992 Serbian
 4    take-over of Prijedor and include atrocities related to the Omarska,
 5    Keraterm, and Trnopolje camps.
 6            The trial began an 16 April 2002 and the judgement was rendered on
 7    13 July 2003.  The Trial Chamber found the appellant not guilty of the
 8    crime of genocide, Count 1; complicity in genocide, Count 2; and other
 9    inhumane acts, forcible transfer, as a crime against humanity, Count 8.
10    The Trial Chamber found the appellant guilty of extermination as a crime
11    against humanity, Count 4; murder, as a violation of the laws and customs
12    of war, Count 5; and persecutions as a crime against humanity, Count 6;
13    incorporating the crimes of murder as a crime against humanity, Count 3;
14    and deportation as a crime against humanity, Count 7.  The Trial Chamber
15    imposed on Milomir Stakic the sentence of life imprisonment qualified,
16    however, by mandatory review after 20 years.  Both the appellant and the
17    Prosecution appealed against this judgement on 1st September 2003.  The
18    hearing on appeal took place on 4, 5, and 6 October 2005.
19            I will now briefly address the grounds of appeal in turn, starting
20    with three of the Prosecution's grounds of appeal, followed by five of the
21    seven of the appellant's grounds of appeal.  The Prosecution's appeal on
22    cumulative convictions will be considered together with the appellant's
23    appeal on cumulative convictions.  Lastly, I will consider the appellant's
24    ground of appeal on sentencing.
25            Now turning to the Prosecution's respective grounds of appeal.
 1            Adopting the order followed in the judgement, I will start with
 2    the Prosecution's third ground of appeal.  The Prosecution argues that the
 3    Trial Chamber erred in law when in the process of determining whether the
 4    appellant committed genocide, it declined to define the target group as
 5    all the non-Serbs in Prijedor municipality, and instead required the
 6    Prosecution to establish genocide with respect to both Bosnian Croats and
 7    Bosnian Muslims.  The Prosecution further argues in the alternative that
 8    the Trial Chamber erred in fact when it found that there was insufficient
 9    evidence to show that the Bosnian Croat group was separately targeted by
10    acts potentially amounting to the actus reus for genocide.
11            The Appeals Chamber, Judge Shahabuddeen dissenting, finds that the
12    Trial Chamber did not err in concluding that the elements of genocide must
13    be separately considered in relation to Bosnian Muslims and Bosnian
14    Croats.  Support for the view that a group targeted for genocide must be
15    positively defined is found in the etymology of the term "genocide," the
16    drafting history of the Genocide Convention, subsequent discussion by
17    experts, and Article 4 of the Statute.  The arguments suggested by the
18    Prosecution to the effect that a group can be subjectively defined are
19    immaterial in reaching this conclusion.  The subground of appeal is thus
20    dismissed.
21            The Appeals Chamber also rejects the Prosecution's alternative
22    argument that the Trial Chamber erred in fact when it found that there was
23    insufficient evidence to show that the Bosnian Croats were a targeted
24    group.  It is true that the Trial Chamber identified a number of
25    individual violent acts, the victims of which were members of the Bosnian
 1    Croat group.  However, the fact that some Bosnian Croats, some Bosnian
 2    Croat properties, and some sites of importance to Bosnian Croats were
 3    victimised does not necessarily compel the conclusion that the Bosnian
 4    Croat group as such was targeted by acts that could constitute the
 5    actus reus for genocide.  In light of the totality of the evidence
 6    concerning crimes against Bosnian Croats, it was not unreasonable for the
 7    Trial Chamber to have found that it could not conclude that the Bosnian
 8    Croat group was separately targeted.  This ground of appeal is therefore
 9    dismissed.
10            Next, turning to the Prosecution's first and second grounds of its
11    appeal, the Prosecution raises six challenges to the Trial Chamber's
12    conclusion that the appellant lacked the requisite dolus specialis for
13    genocide.  The Appeals Chamber will address each of these challenges in
14    turn.
15            First, the Prosecutor contends that the Trial Chamber erred in
16    considering the mens rea of others, namely, the direct perpetrators of the
17    crimes in Prijedor, rather than focussing on the appellant's mental state
18    alone.  The Appeals Chamber finds that it is clear that the Trial Chamber
19    did not suggest that genocidal intent on the part of others was a
20    prerequisite to convicting the appellant for genocide.  Rather, it simply
21    considered whether the apparent intentions of others could provide the
22    indirect evidence of the appellant's own intentions when he agreed with
23    those others to undertake criminal plans.  The Trial Chamber also
24    considered the direct evidence of the appellant's mental state, including
25    his statements, and found it insufficient to establish genocidal intent.
 1    The Appeals Chamber sees no error in this approach.
 2            Second, the Prosecution argues that the Trial Chamber improperly
 3    required it to prove an intent to kill all Bosnian Muslims in the region.
 4    The Appeals Chamber is not persuaded by this argument.  In the very
 5    paragraph cited by the Prosecution to support its argument, the Trial
 6    Chamber specifically found that the Prosecution had not proven that the
 7    appellant sought to destroy in part the Muslim group.  The Trial Chamber's
 8    reference to structures being in place to kill all Muslims merely
 9    constitutes evidence that the appellant did not seek to destroy the
10    Bosnian Muslim group in whole or in part.  The Appeals Chamber considers
11    that the Trial Chamber did not err under the circumstances in this case in
12    concluding that the appellant lacked dolus specialis for genocide.
13            Third, the Prosecution argues that the Trial Chamber confused
14    motive with intent, erroneously concluding that because the appellant's
15    ultimate motive was simply to remove the Bosnian Muslims from Prijedor, he
16    did not intend to destroy that group as a means to that end.  The Appeals
17    Chamber finds, however, that the Trial Chamber expressly distinguished
18    between the goal of the operation, that is motive, and the methods that
19    the appellant intended to employ in order to bring the goal about.  With
20    respect to the latter, the Trial Chamber found insufficient evidence of an
21    intention to achieve the goal by destroying in part the Muslim group.  The
22    Trial Chamber specifically considered whether the appellant intended to
23    achieve his goal through particular actions, including killing and the
24    imposition of inhumane conditions of life, which could, if proven, amount
25    to genocide.  The Appeals Chamber finds no error in this approach.
 1            Fourth, the Prosecution argues that the Trial Chamber erroneously
 2    failed to consider the appellant's intent to inflict conditions of life
 3    calculated to bring about destruction, focussing exclusively on acts of
 4    deportation.  The Appeals Chamber finds that, while the Trial Chamber did
 5    not specifically discuss whether the conditions that prevailed in
 6    detention camps and deportation convoys constituted evidence of this
 7    intent, a Trial Chamber need not spell out every step of its analysis.
 8    Rather than repeating itself unnecessarily, the Trial Chamber referred
 9    back to its analysis in previous paragraphs in relation to the appellant's
10    mental state; for instance, its conclusion that the appellant's public
11    statements suggested that his intention was only to displace the Bosnian
12    Muslim population and not to destroy it.  This analysis was equally
13    applicable to all of the alleged genocidal acts, including the imposition
14    of intolerable living conditions pointed to by the Prosecution.  The Trial
15    Chamber's own factual findings elsewhere in the judgement illustrate that
16    it was well aware of the evidence demonstrating the terrible conditions in
17    the camps and on deportation buses, but it nonetheless concluded that it
18    was insufficient to prove the mens rea for genocide.  The Appeals Chamber
19    sees no error in this approach.
20            Fifth, the Prosecution contends that the Trial Chamber failed to
21    draw proper inferences from the appellant's utterances.  While the Appeals
22    Chamber agrees that utterances might constitute evidence of genocidal
23    intent, even if they fall short of express calls for a group's physical
24    destruction, it finds on the facts of this case that the Trial Chamber
25    adequately and reasonably considered the appellant's derogatory statements
 1    and propaganda in paragraph 554 of the trial judgement.  The Prosecution
 2    has, therefore, not demonstrated that no reasonable Trial Chamber could
 3    fail to conclude that the appellant's utterances demonstrated his
 4    genocidal intent beyond reasonable doubt.
 5            Finally, the Prosecution contends that the Trial Chamber
 6    improperly compartmentalised its inquiry, considering the mens rea
 7    evidence separately with respect to the various genocidal acts alleged
 8    rather than taking into account the totality of the evidence, and that it
 9    ignored or gave insufficient weight to several categories of relevant
10    evidence bearing on the appellant's mens rea.  The Appeals Chamber agrees
11    with the Prosecution that the Trial Chamber's compartmentalised mode of
12    analysis obscured the proper inquiry.  Rather than considering separately
13    whether the appellant intended to destroy the group through each of the
14    genocidal acts specified by Article 4(1)(a), (b), and (c), the Trial
15    Chamber should expressly have considered whether all of the evidence,
16    taken together, demonstrated a genocidal mental state.  Nonetheless, it
17    does not appear that the Trial Chamber's piecemeal approach had any effect
18    on its conclusion.  The reasons it gave with respect to Article 4(1)(b)
19    and (c) simply cross-referenced its analysis of mental state with respect
20    to Article 4(1)(a), in which it concluded that there simply was no
21    evidence in the record that proved that the appellant sought to destroy
22    the Muslim population.  In reaching this conclusion, it must be assumed
23    the Trial Chamber was area of its own factual findings but found them
24    insufficient to establish intent beyond a reasonable doubt.
25            Further, when the Prosecution appeals from factual findings
 1    against it, it bears a heavy burden of persuasion.  The Appeals Chamber
 2    cannot conclude that the evidence in this case is so unambiguous that a
 3    reasonable Trial Chamber was obliged to infer that intent was established
 4    beyond a reasonable doubt.  To the contrary, the evidence could reasonably
 5    be seen as consistent with the conclusion the Trial Chamber did draw:
 6    That the appellant merely intended to displace but not to destroy the
 7    Bosnian Muslim group.  In short, the first three Prosecution grounds of
 8    appeal are dismissed.
 9            Next, we turn to the mode of liability in this case.  In its
10    analysis of the appellant's responsibility, the Trial Chamber specifically
11    rejected the application of joint criminal enterprise as a mode of
12    liability despite the fact that it had been pleaded by the Prosecution,
13    both in the indictment and at trial.  In lieu of joint criminal
14    enterprise, the Trial Chamber applied a mode of liability which is
15    termed "co-perpetratorship," which is new to the jurisprudence of this
16    Tribunal.  Although neither party has appealed the Trial Chamber's
17    application of this mode of liability, the general importance of this
18    issue to the application of the law warrants the scrutiny of the Appeals
19    Chamber proprio motu.  The introduction of new modes of liability into the
20    jurisprudence of the Tribunal may generate uncertainty, if not confusion,
21    in the determination of the law by parties to cases before the Tribunal as
22    well as in the application of the law by Trial Chambers.  To avoid such
23    uncertainty and ensure respect for the values of consistency and coherence
24    in the application of law, the Appeals Chamber must intervene to assess
25    whether the mode of liability applied by the Trial Chamber is consistent
 1    with the jurisprudence of this Tribunal.
 2            The Appeals Chamber finds that the mode of liability of
 3    co-perpetratorship, as defined and applied by the Trial Chamber, does not
 4    have support in customary international law or in the settled
 5    jurisprudence of this Tribunal.  As the Trial Chamber erred in employing a
 6    mode of liability which is not valid law within the jurisprudence of this
 7    Tribunal, its decision as to the mode of liability it employed in the
 8    trial judgement is invalidated.  To remedy this error, the Appeals Chamber
 9    applies the correct legal framework - that of joint criminal enterprise -
10    to the factual conclusions of the Trial Chamber to determine whether they
11    support liability for the crimes charged.
12            Although the indictment does not expressly refer to categories of
13    joint criminal enterprise liability, the allegations therein nonetheless
14    made it clear that the Prosecution intended to rely on both the first and
15    third categories of joint criminal enterprise.  Upon a close review of the
16    trial judgement, the Appeals Chamber holds that the Trial Chamber's
17    factual findings support the conclusion that the appellant participated in
18    a joint criminal enterprise, the common purpose of which amounted to the
19    commission of the crimes against humanity of persecutions, deportation,
20    and other inhumane acts, forcible transfer, punishable under
21    Articles 5(h), 5(d), and 5(i) of the Statute, against the Bosnian Muslim
22    and Bosnian Croat populations of Prijedor.  The appellant shared the
23    intent to further this common purpose and had the intent to commit the
24    underlying crimes.  Further, the Appeals Chamber holds that the factual
25    findings of the Trial Chamber demonstrate that a natural and foreseeable
 1    consequence of the implementation of this common criminal purpose was the
 2    commission of the crimes of extermination and murder.  The appellant, who
 3    participated in the implementation of the common purpose, reconciled
 4    himself with the likely commission of these crimes.
 5            In light of the foregoing, the Appeals Chamber concludes that the
 6    Trial Chamber's factual findings support the appellant's liability for the
 7    crimes against humanity, persecutions, deportation, and inhumane acts,
 8    forcible transfer, pursuant to the first category of joint criminal
 9    enterprise; and for the crimes against humanity of extermination and
10    murder and the war crime of murder pursuant to the third category of joint
11    criminal enterprise.
12            In various sections of his appeal brief, the appellant has raised
13    a number of arguments challenging the Trial Chamber's use of
14    dolus eventualis as a form of mens rea, submitting that the Trial Chamber
15    impermissibly enlarged the mens rea required for the crimes against
16    humanity of murder, extermination, and persecutions, as well as the war
17    crime of murder, and that by doing so the Trial Chamber violated the
18    principles of nullum crimen sine lege and in dubio pro reo.  Since the
19    Appeals Chamber has established the appellant's responsibility via the
20    mode of liability of joint criminal enterprise, the Appeals Chamber has
21    addressed these arguments in the context of this mode of liability.  The
22    Appeals Chamber notes that the mode of liability of joint criminal
23    enterprise was recognised as a mode of liability in customary law as early
24    as 1992 and that it has been clarified in the jurisprudence of the Appeals
25    Chamber that the use of dolus eventualis within the context of the third
 1    category of joint criminal enterprise does not violate the principles of
 2    nullum crimen sine lege and in dubio pro reo.  On this basis, the
 3    appellant's challenges must fail.
 4            Next, we turn to the appellant's first ground of appeal, which
 5    contain three subgrounds concerning the alleged expansion of the
 6    indictment by the Trial Chamber.  In his first two subgrounds, the
 7    appellant alleges that the Trial Chamber erred by relying on acts
 8    originating outside the time period of the indictment and by preventing
 9    him from contesting the alleged error during the proceedings at trial.
10    The Appeals Chamber finds that some of the acts referred to by the
11    appellant constitute material facts which must be pleaded, while others
12    amount to evidence which need not be pleaded.  The material facts referred
13    to by the appellant, including his authority and role in the joint
14    criminal enterprise during the indictment period, were properly pleaded.
15    The remainder of the acts referred to by the appellant in fact constituted
16    evidence and therefore did not need to be pleaded in the indictment.
17    Further, the appellant has not shown by reference to either the trial
18    record or the trial judgement that the Trial Chamber prevented him from
19    introducing relevant evidence.  Therefore, the appellant's first two
20    subgrounds of appeal are dismissed.
21            In his third subground of appeal, the appellant submits that the
22    indictment did not sufficiently inform him as to how his status as
23    commander could be used as an aggravating circumstance in determining his
24    sentence and that he was, therefore, prevented from contesting this matter
25    at trial.  The Appeals Chamber finds that this submission is inconsistent
 1    with the jurisprudence of this Tribunal.  The Appeals Chamber has
 2    previously found that as a matter of principle there is no requirement
 3    that the Prosecution plead aggravating factors in an indictment.  This
 4    ground of appeal is accordingly dismissed.
 5            In the second and third ground of appeal, the appellant alleges a
 6    number of factual and legal errors which he argues denied him a fair trial
 7    occasioning a miscarriage of justice.  The appellant submits that the
 8    Trial Chamber erred in denying his request to obtain certain expert
 9    witnesses, in not admitting nine witness statements pursuant to
10    Rule 92 bis, in denying his motion for a mistrial based on violations of
11    Rule 68, in admitting certain of the Prosecution's Rule 92 bis evidence,
12    in issuing numerous Rule 91 warnings to Defence witnesses, and in
13    admitting unreliable and untrustworthy evidence.  The Appeals Chamber has
14    addressed each of these specific challenges in detail in the text of the
15    judgement and has dismissed them, and the appellant's second ground of
16    appeal on the basis that the Trial Chamber did not commit any errors.
17            The appellant further argues that the Trial Chamber drew
18    impermissible inferences from circumstantial evidence, regarding his state
19    of mind and degree of knowledge of the crimes being committed in the
20    prison camps, on the battle-field, and in the municipality in general,
21    errors which he alleges invalidate all of his convictions.  The Appeals
22    Chamber clarifies that a Trial Chamber may only find an accused guilty of
23    a crime if the Prosecution has proved each element of both that crime and
24    the related mode of liability beyond a reasonable doubt.  This standard
25    applies whether the evidence evaluated is direct or circumstantial.  Where
 1    the challenge on appeal is to an inference drawn to establish a fact on
 2    which the conviction relies, the standard is only satisfied if the
 3    inference drawn was the only reasonable one that could be drawn from the
 4    evidence presented.  In light of this standard, the Appeals Chamber in the
 5    judgement has addressed in detail the alternative inferences suggested by
 6    the appellant and found that the Trial Chamber did not err in drawing the
 7    inferences it did.  In view of the foregoing, the Appeals Chamber
 8    dismisses the appellant's third ground of appeal.
 9            In its fourth ground of appeal, the appellant submits that the
10    Trial Chamber erred in law and in fact in the application of various
11    elements of Article 5 of the Statute.  In his first subground of appeal,
12    the appellant denies that the attacks in Prijedor were widespread or
13    systematic, as required by Article 5.  The appellant has failed to
14    demonstrate to the Appeals Chamber how the Trial Chamber's findings of the
15    existence of a systematic attack were unreasonable in light of all the
16    evidence.  Therefore, the Appeals Chamber finds that there is no basis on
17    which to overturn the finding by the Trial Chamber that the attack was
18    systematic.  Having found that the Trial Chamber did not err in concluding
19    that the systematic attack occurred, the Appeals Chamber finds that for
20    reasons of judicial economy it is not necessary to address whether such an
21    attack is also widespread.  The related submissions are accordingly
22    dismissed.
23            In his second subground of appeal, the appellant argues that the
24    Trial Chamber erred in law in its treatment of extermination as a crime
25    against humanity.  He submits that extermination requires both that a vast
 1    scheme of collective murder be established and that the accused person
 2    have knowledge of such a scheme.  He further submits that the mens rea for
 3    extermination requires an intent to kill a large number of individuals,
 4    the number of which should be in the thousands in order to meet the
 5    threshold of severity and gravity of the crime.  Finally, he submits that
 6    dolus eventualis is not sufficient to establish the mens rea for
 7    extermination.
 8            The Appeals Chamber finds that the jurisprudence of the Tribunal
 9    does not support requirements for the crime of extermination of a vast
10    scheme of collective murder, knowledge of such a scheme, or a numerical
11    minimum of victims required for extermination.  The Appeals Chamber also
12    finds that dolus eventualis is sufficient to meet the mens rea
13    requirements for liability pursuant to the third category of joint
14    criminal enterprise.  As the appellant has not shown that the findings of
15    the Trial Chamber on which the Appeals Chamber relied on reaching this
16    conclusion are in error, this subground of appeal is dismissed.
17            Next, we consider the subground of appeal on deportation.  The
18    appellant submits that the Trial Chamber erred in its application of the
19    law on deportation by not requiring forcible displacement across a
20    national border and by inferring that he had the intent to deport the
21    non-Serb population permanently.  He further submits that the Trial
22    Chamber overlooked evidence demonstrating that persons left Prijedor
23    voluntarily and erred when it concluded that departures organised by
24    international humanitarian organisations are not permitted under
25    international law.
 1            The Appeals Chamber holds that the actus reus of deportation is
 2    the forced displacement of persons by expulsion or other forms of coercion
 3    from the area in which they are lawfully present across a de jure state
 4    border or, in certain circumstances, a de facto border, without grounds
 5    permitted under international law.  The Appeals Chamber has found no
 6    evidence that demonstrates that transfers across constantly changing front
 7    lines may amount to deportation under summary international law.  It
 8    therefore concludes, Judge Shahabuddeen dissenting, that the Trial
 9    Chamber's finding in this respect in fact expands criminal responsibility
10    by giving greater scope to the crime of deportation than exists under
11    customary international law, and thus violates the principle of
12    nullum crimen sine lege.
13            The Appeals Chamber considers that the mens rea of deportation
14    does not require an intent that deportees should not return.  The Trial
15    Chamber therefore erred when it reached a contrary conclusion on the basis
16    of the ICRC commentary.  However, the Trial Chamber's error proved
17    harmless in this case.
18            With respect to the factual basis for the Trial Chamber's finding
19    on deportation, the appellant has not demonstrated how the Trial Chamber's
20    conclusions about the coercive atmosphere pervading the municipality of
21    Prijedor are such that no reasonable trier of fact could have made them.
22    Consequently, the Appeals Chamber finds that the Trial Chamber did not
23    err, either as a matter of law or fact, in finding that the departures
24    were involuntary and, therefore, unlawful.  In this respect, the Appeals
25    Chamber considers that the participation of an NGO in facilitating
 1    displacements does not in and of itself render an otherwise unlawful
 2    transfer lawful.  Further, although displacement for humanitarian reasons
 3    is justifiable in certain situations, the Appeals Chamber agrees with the
 4    Prosecution that it is not justifiable where the humanitarian crisis that
 5    caused the displacement is itself the result of the accused's own unlawful
 6    activity.  In the instant case, the evidence supports only one reason why
 7    it might arguably have been safer for Bosnian Muslims in Prijedor to be
 8    displaced:  The dangers posed to them by the criminal scheme of
 9    persecutions undertaken by the appellant and his co-perpetrators.
10            The Trial Chamber's error with respect to the nature of the
11    cross-border transfer requirement for the crime of deportation
12    necessitates that the Appeals Chamber apply the correct legal definition
13    of the crime to the factual findings of the Trial Chamber.  Before doing
14    so, the Appeals Chamber finds proprio motu that the notion of other
15    inhumane acts contained in Article 5(i) of the Statute cannot be regarded
16    as a violation of the principle of nullum crimen sine lege as it forms
17    part of customary international law.  Accordingly, the Appeals Chamber
18    finds that the Trial Chamber erred in finding that a conviction based on
19    Article 5(i) of the Statute for acts of forcible transfer could not be
20    entered.
21            On the basis of the correct definition of deportation, the Appeals
22    Chamber is satisfied that the forcible displacement of Witness Cehajic,
23    who was transported by convoy from Prijedor on 5 September 1992 and
24    arrived in Karlovac, Croatia, one day later, amounts to deportation.
25    However, the Appeals Chamber finds proprio motu that a few of the
 1    incidents in respect of which the Trial Chamber found deportation, most
 2    notably the transfer of 1.561 people from the Trnopolje camp in the
 3    municipality of Prijedor to Karlovac cannot with certainty be placed
 4    within the indictment period.  Other findings in the trial judgement
 5    related to forcible displacements across front lines between the parties
 6    to the conflict as well as between locations under Serb control are not
 7    sufficient to ground a conviction for deportation.  Instead, the Trial
 8    Chamber should have entered a conviction for other inhumane acts under
 9    Article 5(i) of the Statute for these incidents, detailed in the appeal
10    judgement, which amount to acts of forcible transfer.
11            With respect to the fourth subground of appeal concerning
12    Article 5, the appellant submits that the Trial Chamber lowered the
13    threshold of proof required for persecutions by accepting dolus eventualis
14    as sufficient to prove the mens rea for acts underlying persecutions.  He
15    further submits that the Trial Chamber provided inadequate analysis of how
16    the dolus specialis requirement for persecutions was met.  The Appeals
17    Chamber finds that the Trial Chamber carefully considered evidence of the
18    appellant's personal discriminatory intent; such intent was neither
19    presumed nor transferred from the direct perpetrators.  It further
20    concludes that the Trial Chamber did not err in its consideration of the
21    evidence on the appellant's mens rea for persecutions.  Accordingly, the
22    arguments of the appellant are dismissed.
23            Under his fifth ground of appeal, the appellant challenges his
24    conviction on Count 5 for murder as a violation of the laws and customs of
25    war.  He claims that the Trial Chamber erred in its consideration of the
 1    evidence establishing a nexus between the acts of the appellant and the
 2    armed conflict, as required by Article 3 of the Statute, because it did
 3    not specifically analyse the required nexus with respect to each alleged
 4    act.  Contrary to this submission, the Appeals Chamber finds that when the
 5    trial judgement is considered as a whole, it is clear that the requisite
 6    nexus analysis was indeed undertaken.  For each of the three categories of
 7    killings considered - the camp killings, the convoy killings, and the
 8    municipality killings - the Trial Chamber sufficiently demonstrated that
 9    the appellant's Article 3 crimes were linked to the armed conflict.  This
10    ground of appeal is accordingly dismissed.
11            Now, we will consider the Prosecution's fourth ground of appeal
12    and the appellant's seventh ground of appeal on cumulative convictions.
13            The Prosecution submits that the Trial Chamber erred when it
14    declined to enter convictions for the crimes against humanity of murder
15    and deportation in light of the conviction for the crime against humanity
16    of persecutions based on, inter alia, the same underlying acts, reasoning
17    that persecutions most accurately captured the nature of the appellant's
18    criminal conduct taken as a whole.  The appellant submits that the Trial
19    Chamber erred in convicting him cumulatively for both persecutions and
20    extermination based on the same conduct.
21            The Appeals Chamber considers that the test to be applied with
22    respect to cumulative convictions was clearly established in the Celibici
23    appeal judgement and refined in the Kordic appeal judgement.  In the view
24    of the Appeals Chamber, the Trial Chamber erred in law in exercising
25    discretion to convict only in relation to the crime that it considered to
 1    most closely and most comprehensively reflect the totality of the
 2    accused's criminal conduct.  When the evidence supports convictions under
 3    multiple counts for the same underlying acts, the test as set forth in the
 4    Celibici and Kordic does not permit the Trial Chamber discretion to enter
 5    one or more of the appropriate convictions, unless the two crimes do not
 6    possess materially distinct elements.
 7            The Appeals Chamber, Judge Guney dissenting, considers that a
 8    proper application of the test for cumulative convictions in this case
 9    demonstrates that convictions are permissible for the following crimes
10    against humanity:  Persecutions under Article 5(h); deportation under
11    Article 5(d); other inhumane acts under Article 5(i); and extermination
12    under Article 5(b).  Only a conviction for murder under Article 5(a) is
13    not permissible, as that crime does not require any material elements to
14    be proven over and above those required for the crime of extermination.
15            The Appeals Chambers thus dismisses the appellant's appeal
16    relating to his cumulative convictions for persecution and extermination.
17    The Prosecution appeal is granted, but the Appeals Chamber finds proprio
18    motu that it is not permissible to enter a conviction for murder as a
19    crime against humanity because it is impermissibly cumulative with the
20    conviction for extermination.
21            In his sixth ground of appeal, the appellant submits that the
22    Trial Chamber erred in imposing a life sentence.  A number of the
23    appellant's submissions in this regard have been considered by the
24    Appeals Chamber and dismissed for lack of merit.  However, the Appeals
25    Chamber finds that the Trial Chamber committed three discernible errors in
 1    imposing the sentence.  The Appeals Chamber addresses these errors in
 2    turn.
 3            First, the appellant submits that the Trial Chamber erred by
 4    imposing conditions of the review of the sentence when such authority is
 5    reserved to the relevant Host State and by usurping the competence vested
 6    in the President of the Tribunal to ultimately decide such matters.  The
 7    Appeals Chamber notes that the disposition appears to impose a 20-year
 8    review obligation on the Host State.  This obligation is inconsistent with
 9    the regime set forth in the Statute and Rules.  The Statute, Rules,
10    relevant Practice Direction, and Model Agreement for enforcing sentences
11    each provide that eligibility of a convicted person for pardon, early
12    release, or commutation of sentence is determined by the law of the state
13    in which the convicted person is serving his sentence.  These instruments
14    also define the precise nature of the supervisory role of the Tribunal in
15    this situation, granting the President of the Tribunal the power to make a
16    final determination in each case.  Imposing a 20-year review obligation on
17    the courts of the Host State is contrary to these provisions as it imposes
18    on the Host State both the date of review and the relevant considerations
19    when conducting the review, therefore supplanting applicable municipal
20    laws.  Further, by vesting the courts of the Host State with the power to
21    suspend the sentence, the Trial Chamber effectively removes the power from
22    the President of the Tribunal to make the final determination regarding
23    the sentence.  The Appeals Chamber finds that the Trial Chamber acted
24    ultra vires in imposing a review obligation on the Host State and
25    therefore committed a discernible error.
 1            Second, the appellant contends that the Trial Chamber erred in
 2    concluding that his professional background as a physician was an
 3    aggravating factor for sentencing purposes.  For the conclusion that the
 4    appellant's medical background could be cited as an aggravating factor,
 5    the Trial Chamber relied on only the Kayishema and Ruzindana and
 6    Ntakirutimana trial judgements.  The Appeals Chamber does not find these
 7    precedents persuasive.  In Kayishema and Ruzindana, the Trial Chamber
 8    simply stated that as a medical doctor Kayishema owed a duty to his
 9    community and that this constituted an aggravating factor but said little
10    as to the legal basis for its conclusion.  In Ntakirutimana, the Trial
11    Chamber's reference to the duty of a medical doctor appears to have been
12    made in a context which is completely different from that of the case
13    before this Appeals Chamber.  For these reasons, the Appeals Chamber thus
14    finds that the Trial Chamber committed a discernible error in identifying
15    the professional background of the appellant as an aggravating factor.
16            Third, the appellant argues that the Trial Chamber erred as a
17    matter of law and abused its discretion in concluding that a long phase of
18    preparation and planning was an aggravating factor.  The Appeals Chamber
19    does not dispute that, as noted by the Prosecution, a long phase of
20    planning and preparation can be an aggravating factor.  Although the Trial
21    Chamber is not clear in this regard, the Appeals Chamber notes that this
22    long phase of planning and preparation appears to have ended with the
23    take-over of Prijedor, 30 April 1992.  The Appeals Chamber considers it
24    unfair to consider for aggravation purposes findings concerning events
25    that are temporarily outside the scope of the indictment, without
 1    providing a reasoned opinion as to why doing so would be appropriate in
 2    the circumstances of the case.  For this reason, the Appeals Chamber finds
 3    that the Trial Chamber committed a discernible error.
 4            The Appeals Chamber has considered the errors made by the Trial
 5    Chamber and comes to the conclusion that their impact on the sentence has
 6    to be regarded as very limited.  It takes note, however, that one of the
 7    errors concerns the sentence itself.  Therefore, in view of the fact that
 8    the imposition of a fixed-term sentence must be revised, the Appeals
 9    Chamber will impose an appropriate sentence, properly reflecting both the
10    criminality of the appellant and the substance of the sentence imposed by
11    the Trial Chamber.
