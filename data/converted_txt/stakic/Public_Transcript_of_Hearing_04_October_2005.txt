20            JUDGE MERON:  Please be seated.  We shall now start the hearing on
21    the merits of the appeals.  Before we begin, let me briefly recall the
22    grounds of appeal raised by the parties.
23            The first, second, and third grounds of appeal by the Prosecution
24    concern genocide, a crime of which Mr. Stakic was acquitted.
25            In its first ground of appeal, the Prosecution argues that the
 1    Trial Chamber erred in finding that Mr. Stakic did not have the requisite
 2    intent for genocide.
 3            In its second ground of appeal, the Prosecution argues that, in
 4    determining that Mr. Stakic had not committed acts specified in Article 4
 5    of the Statute, was genocidal intent, the Trial Chamber improperly failed
 6    to consider conditions at certain camps set up by the authorities in
 7    Prijedor, conditions under which deportations and expulsions were carried
 8    out and measured that deprived Bosnian and Croats of work and housing in
 9    Prijedor.
10            In its third ground of appeal, the Prosecution argues that the
11    Trial Chamber should have defined the group victimised by the alleged
12    genocide as "non-Serbs" and that in any event, the Trial Chamber should
13    have found that Bosnian Croats, like Bosnian Muslims, were targeted for
14    crimes in Prijedor because of their ethnicity.
15            The Prosecution's fourth ground of appeal relates to cumulative
16    convictions.  The Prosecution argues that the Trial Chamber erred in law
17    by failing to enter independent convictions against the appellant for
18    persecutions, murder, and deportations as crimes against humanity, and
19    instead only entering a conviction for persecutions as a crime against
20    humanity.
21            I turn now to the Defence's grounds of appeal.
22            The first relates to the alleged expulsion of the indictment.  The
23    Defence argues here, inter alia, that the Trial Chamber erred by relying
24    on acts that Mr. Stakic allegedly took before or after the period
25    specified in the indictment.
 1            In its second ground of appeal, the Defence argue that the Trial
 2    Chamber committed numerous errors of law and fact and thus denied
 3    Mr. Stakic a fair trial.  Specifically, the Defence challenges the Trial
 4    Chamber's refusal to admit expert evidence on various topics.  The Trial
 5    Chamber's denial of Mr. Stakic's motion for a mistrial based on the
 6    Prosecution's breach of its Rule 68 disclosure obligations; the Trial
 7    Chamber's refusal to let the Defence introduce certain written statements
 8    pursuant to Rule 92 bis; the Trial Chamber's decision to admit certain
 9    written statements offered by the Prosecution; the Trial Chamber's
10    decision not to warn certain Defence witnesses about the obligation to
11    tell the truth; and the Trial Chamber's admission of certain evidence the
12    Defence deems untrustworthy.
13            In its third ground of appeal, the Defence submits that the Trial
14    Chamber drew impermissible circumstances from circumstantial evidence
15    regarding Mr. Stakic's state of mind and the extent to which he knew about
16    the crimes being committed and thereby caused a miscarriage of justice.
17            In its fourth ground of appeal, the Defence contends that the
18    Trial Chamber erred in law and in fact in its application of various
19    elements of Article 5 of the Statute; in particular, the Defence
20    challenges both the Trial Chamber's finding that there was a widespread
21    and systematic attack on civilian populations in Prijedor municipality and
22    its findings that Mr. Stakic committed the offences of extermination,
23    persecutions, and deportation.
24            The Defence's fifth ground of appeal relates to Article 3 of the
25    Statute.  The Defence argues that the Trial Chamber erred in finding a
 1    nexus between Mr. Stakic's acts and the armed conflict, a requirement for
 2    Article 3, liability.  The Trial Chamber erred in convicting him of murder
 3    under Article 3 of the Statute.
 4            In its sixth ground of appeal, the Defence argues that the Trial
 5    Chamber erred in imposing a life sentence.
 6            In its seventh ground of appeal, the Defence argues that the Trial
 7    Chamber erred in convicting Mr. Stakic for both persecutions and
 8    exterminations as crimes against humanity.  According to the Defence,
 9    Mr. Stakic's conviction for the crime of extermination is impermissibly
10    cumulative with his conviction for the crime of persecutions.
11            Now I would like to remind the parties that an appeal is not a
12    trial de novo.  The Appeals Chamber will consider alleged errors of law
13    which would invalidate the judgement, and in exceptional circumstances it
14    will consider other questions of law of importance to the Tribunal's
15    jurisprudence.
16            The Tribunal will only consider alleged errors of fact that would
17    result in a miscarriage of justice.  As the parties should be well aware,
18    the Appeals Chamber considers questions of law de novo, but requires an
19    appellant who alleges errors of fact to demonstrate that no reasonable
20    trier of fact could have reached the conclusion being challenged.  Parties
21    should be as clear and precise as possible in the presentation of their
22    arguments.

 1    US Senate Foreign Relations Committee, US Secretary of State Colin Powell
 2    described the ongoing events in the Darfur region of Sudan as "genocide."
 3    According to Secretary Powell, the target group of this genocide was
 4    non-Arab individuals and villagers.
 5            In the context of the Sudan, where it has been reported that there
 6    are more than 500 ethnic groups, this is the only designation that made
 7    sense of the facts on the ground.
 8            Your Honours, 12 years before Secretary Powell's declaration about
 9    the Sudan, a UN Commission of Experts, looking at the situation in Bosnia,
10    reached a similar conclusion.  In their final report, the Commission of
11    Experts concluded that, if there are several or more than one victim group
12    and each group, as such, is protected, it may be within the spirit and
13    purpose of the convention to consider all the victim groups as a larger
14    entity.  The commission described a situation where an A group has defined
15    a pluralistic, non-A group using national, ethnical, racial, or religious
16    criterion for the definition.  The Commission concluded that it could be
17    relevant to analyse the fate of the non-A group along similar lines as if
18    the non-A group had been homogenous.
19            Given the fact that the Commission of Experts focussed much of
20    their investigation on what happened in Prijedor, it should come as no
21    surprise that the example they give matches the facts in this case so
22    well.  The approach suggested by the Commission of Experts has been
23    accepted into the jurisprudence of this Tribunal in the Jelisic case.  In
24    that case, the Trial Chamber concluded that a group could be stigmatised
25    by way of positive or negative criteria.  A positive approach
 1    distinguishes a group by the characteristics which they deem to be
 2    particular to a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group.  A
 3    negative approach consists of identifying individuals as not being part of
 4    the group to which the perpetrators of the crime consider that they
 5    themselves belong and which, to them, display specific national, ethnical,
 6    racial, or religious characteristics.
 7            All individuals thus rejected would, by exclusion, make up a
 8    distinct group.
 9            The Jelisic Trial Chamber agreed that it was consonant with the
10    object and the purpose of the genocide convention to consider that its
11    provisions also protect groups defined by exclusion where they have been
12    stigmatised by the perpetrators in that way.  In the view of the Jelisic
13    Trial Chamber, thus, you correctly define a target group from the
14    perspective, the subjective perspective, of the perpetrator, and it will
15    depend on the facts of the case.
16            Now, keeping in mind the caveat expressed by the Commission of
17    Experts that the separate victim groups so combined must, as such, each
18    already be protected by the genocide convention, we submit that this
19    approach makes sense of the evidentiary burden placed on the Prosecution.
20    The Prosecution must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the perpetrator
21    had the specific intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national,
22    ethnical, racial, or religious group as such.  To determine the specific
23    intent of the perpetrator, however, you must first identify what it is he
24    is attempting to destroy, what he is trying to destroy.  This means that
25    the focus must be on the subjective view of the perpetrator.
 1            Let's say, for example, that the perpetrator is a member of a
 2    group A and he is trying to destroy a group B because of specific
 3    characteristics of group B and group C because of different specific
 4    characteristics of group C.  On the facts of this case, it could make
 5    sense to analyse the acts against group B and C as if they were separate
 6    intended genocides.  But what if the perpetrator is only going against
 7    groups B and C because neither one is group A, to which he belongs?  In
 8    that case, from the point of view of the perpetrator, there are not two
 9    attempted genocides but only one.  And in the latter case, the perpetrator
10    himself has defined the target group by negative criterion.
11            When analysing the perpetrator's intent, we submit, the trier of
12    fact should analyse the intent he actually has and not some artificial
13    construct based on a division of the targeted groups, which doesn't
14    reflect the way the perpetrator allegedly viewed the situation.  In the
15    present case, the Trial Chamber erred because it concluded that it could
16    not legally do that.
17            Now, the approach accepted in the Jelisic Trial Chamber is
18    consistent with other cases from this Tribunal and the Rwanda Tribunal
19    that have defined the targeted group from the subjective perspective of
20    the perpetrator.  Citing Jelisic, for example, the Krstic Trial Chamber
21    defined the relevant group by using as a criterion the stigmatisation of
22    the group, notably by the perpetrators of the crime, on the basis of its
23    perceived national, ethnical, racial, or religious characteristics.
24            In Rutaganda, from Rwanda, the Trial Chamber found that membership
25    of a group is, in essence, a subjective rather than an objective concept.
 1    The victim is perceived by the perpetrator of the genocide as belonging to
 2    a group slated for destruction.  The Rutaganda case had followed a
 3    subjective approach used by the Trial Chambers in Kayishema, and this was
 4    later followed by the Trial Chamber in Bagilishema, which concluded that
 5    the perpetrators of genocide may characterise the target groups in ways
 6    that do not fully correspond to conceptions of the group shared generally
 7    or even by other segments of society.  In such a case, the Chamber was of
 8    the opinion that on the evidence, if a victim was perceived by a
 9    perpetrator as belonging to a protected group, the victim could be
10    considered by the Chamber as a member of the protected group for the
11    purposes of genocide.
12            Now let's come to what happened in the present case.  The Trial
13    Chamber in this case concluded that when more than one group is targeted,
14    it is not appropriate to define the group in general terms as non-Serbs,
15    thus rejecting the negative approach adopted in Jelisic and the subjective
16    approach adopted in most of the remaining cases from the ICTR and ICTY. In
17    so doing, the Trial Chamber ignored its own findings that the target of
18    Dr. Stakic's genocidal acts was actually non-Serbs.
19            I have several pages of examples that I'm not going to go through

22            First of all, the word "non-Serb" or "non-Serbs" appears in the

 1            Before moving on to grounds 1 and 2, I'd like to deal here with
 2    two questions that were posed in advance of this hearing by the Chamber.
 3    The first is question number 4 that asks that we address the legal basis
 4    for defining a group protected under Article 4 as non-Serbs.
 5            I would just like to add this to what I've already said on that
 6    subject.  I think there are two ways where the definition of the group is
 7    important under the genocide convention and under the Statute.  First, we
 8    must be sure that the victim group is one that enjoys the protection of
 9    the genocide convention.
10            For example, this must not be a case where the victim group is a
11    political construct which is generally accepted as being beyond the reach
12    of the protection of the genocide convention, which does not protect
13    political groups.
14            And second, we must analyse the specific intent of an accused to
15    determine whether he had the intent to eliminate a protected group as
16    such.  So first is ensuring that there are protections given to the people
17    that are targeted, and secondly, ensuring that we examine the intent of
18    the accused.  And I think I've already demonstrated how in this case
19    defining the group in a negative fashion as non-Serbs is the best way to
20    analyse the evidence of Dr. Stakic's intent in this case.
21            So turning to the first issue, this is not a case where the
22    Prosecution is seeking to expand the protection of the genocide convention

25    extended to any stable and permanent group, as was done in the Akayesu
 1    case, for example.  I think there is no doubt that Bosnian Muslims, or at
 2    least no dispute in this case, that Bosnian Muslims and Bosnian Croats
 3    were each separately protected groups under the genocide convention.
 4            Thus, every person defined as members of the larger group,
 5    non-Serb, is also a member of the smaller, protected group defined by
 6    ethnicity.  The groups are merely being combined to analyse the intent of
 7    the accused.  Thus, I submit, this meets the concerns expressed by the
 8    Commission of Experts that the groups combined in this fashion must, as
 9    such, be protected.
10            Moreover, we would refer to the language of the convention which
11    defines the protections as extending to groups defined by ethnicity,
12    nationality, religion, or race.  In a broader group, in a broader sense,
13    an ethnic group is any group defined by ethnicity.  If you have a group of
14    people, say everyone in this room, from which you exclude everyone who is
15    of one nationality, the remainder, by definition, is a group defined by
16    ethnicity, the ethnicity they don't have.  The genocide convention
17    protects ethnic groups, not homogenous ethnic groups.

 1    cannot be relied upon as an independent source of legal authority.
 2    However, policy considerations can be used as an aid in interpreting the
 3    Statute, particularly when, as here, the statutory language has been
 4    lifted directly from an international treaty, the 1948 Convention on the
 5    Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide.
 6            The legal basis for using policy considerations in interpreting
 7    Article 4 is Articles 31 and 32 of the Vienna Convention on Treaties.  The
 8    Appeal Chamber for the ICTR recognised in an interlocutory appeal in the
 9    Bagosora case that Article 31 of the Vienna Convention applied to the
10    Statute of the ICTR.  That's the decision of 8th of June, 1998,
11    paragraphs 28 and 29.
12            Here at the ICTY, Judges McDonald and Vohrah for the Tribunal
13    recognised the application of the rules of treaty interpretation in the
14    Vienna Convention in the Tadic jurisdictional appeal, paragraph 18.  And I
15    think that you will see from an analysis of that decision that what I'm
16    going to say was used as a way to analyse the intent.
17            Under Article 31 of the Vienna Convention, terms in a treaty are
18    interpreted in context and in light of the treaty's object and purpose.
19    The reference to the treaty's context and its object and purpose points
20    towards a teleological or purposive standard of interpretation, which
21    means, in our view, considering the mischief the treaty was designed to
22    remedy.  In that context, what we meant by policy considerations in 4.9 of
23    our brief is that the Statute must be interpreted by considering which
24    interpretation would be most effective in getting at the mischief
25    criminalised by the genocide convention.  At the same time, the Trial
 1    Chamber should have avoided an interpretation that doesn't reflect how the
 2    perpetrators of genocide actually view their targeted groups.
