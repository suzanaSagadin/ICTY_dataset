18            Last February, I pointed out that this trial was going to put
19    international humanitarian law and its practice to the test.  The test has
20    obviously been conclusive in many respects.  The procedural feasibility of
21    such a trial has been established.  The Prosecution presented its case for

 1            However, a trial before an international forum is inconceivable
 2    without the cooperation of states.  It is in this field that the fate of
 3    the trial will ultimately be played out.  And unlike domestic
 4    jurisdictions, international justice has no means of enforcement.  Yet
 5    state cooperation, in particular cooperation from the Federal Republic of
 6    Yugoslavia, is far from being exhaustive.  Admittedly, during the initial
 7    stage of these proceedings Yugoslavia made it possible to hear several
 8    deposition witnesses and facilitated access to some sources of evidence
 9    material. Cooperation remains, however, fractious, difficult,
10    unpredictable.  More importantly and much to my regret, it does not appear
11    to be guided by the will or the interest to actively participate in
12    establishing the truth.
13            With regard to the segment of the trial opening today, Yugoslavia
14    continues to hold the key to irreplaceable evidence, such as potential
15    witnesses who will need state authorisation to travel and testify, or
16    civilian and military archives, access to which is still denied to date.
17            I have given up the hope of seeing Belgrade encourage potential
18    witnesses to come and testify.  The only thing I ask is that they allow
19    them to come while lifting the threat of criminal prosecution for
20    violation of state or military secrets.  As you can see, a great deal will
21    depend on the extent to which third parties, including but not limited to
22    Yugoslavia, will comply with their legal obligations before the Tribunal.
23            Allow me to underscore that cooperation should not be limited to
24    strict compliance with our requests.  States and international
25    organisations should, in my view, contribute actively to ascertaining the
 1    facts by submitting to the Trial Chamber all the evidence they know to be
 2    relevant to the case.
 3            Furthermore, administration of justice must be transparent.
 4    Public hearings must remain the rule, closed sessions the exception,
 5    justified only by the prevailing need to protect the identity of witnesses
 6    in danger.  This very much coincides with the views of the Trial Chamber.
 7            A transparent administration of justice is a fundamental component
 8    of the rule of law.  We therefore call upon all countries in a position to
 9    make witnesses available to us, to acknowledge and apply to international
10    justice the principles they hold dear in their own country.  In this
11    respect, we should recall that the Trial Chamber expressed time and time
12    again its willingness to take into consideration legitimate state
13    interests by approving closed sessions as required to protect confidential
14    and classified evidence.

16    large extent to the cooperation and support of the Victims and Witnesses

19    Trial Chamber heard witnesses without interruption, with the exception of

21    imponderables and factors beyond our control.  I want to pay tribute to
