25             As to the law of interference, Your Honour, I want to begin by
 1     drawing your attention to the first minute of this trial, my opening
 2     argument, and I began my opening argument by saying that this is a case
 3     about witness interference.  And if you could turn your computers on to
 4     computer evidence now so that you could see what we are broadcasting.  We
 5     are going to be showing some slides.  I had indicated at the beginning of
 6     my opening argument that this is a case about interference with
 7     witnesses; not a case about threats, not a case about witness
 8     intimidation, and as such I'm going to focus my discussion on the law of
 9     interference with witnesses.
10             The most recent case to discuss witness interference came out of
11     the Brdjanin case which was the Maglov contempt case.  And in that case
12     the Trial Chamber defined otherwise interfering with a witness as
13     contempt of court as requiring proof of two elements:  first, that the
14     accused is engaged in conduct that is likely to deter a witness or a
15     potential witness from giving evidence, or engaged in conduct that is
16     likely to influence the nature of witnesses' evidence.  That's element
17     number 1.  One of those two alternatives; and the second element is that
18     the accused acted knowingly and wilfully.
19             Now, having studied this I think there is a loophole in this and
20     I think there is a better construction that this Court ought to consider.
21     The "likely to deter" I think is problematic and I want to give you an
22     example of where I think this will cause problems, not in this case but
23     in other cases.  If you have, for example, a case where the Microsoft
24     Corporation is being litigated and Bill Gates, who is one of the richest
25     men in America is being sued and Bill Gates walks up to the court house
 1     and he has a witness badge on and somebody who is intent on interfering
 2     with Bill Gates approaches him and says, "I will give you 50.000 dollars
 3     if you withdraw your testimony right now."  Well, Bill Gates is going to
 4     continue walking and he's going to say, "I don't need the money.  It's
 5     far too small amount to money for me to even consider."  And under the
 6     Maglov decision, that behaviour of that person would not be arguably
 7     considered contemptuous because it was not likely to deter Bill Gates.
 8     And I think this Court actually is going to want to regulate the
 9     behaviour of anybody who approaches a witness even if it's not likely to
10     deter.  So I think the standard here, the construction as espoused in
11     Maglov is problematic.
12             And I want to turn your attention to a case from England that I
13     think is very similar to our case.  It's called the Toney case, and I
14     have some documents which I can tender to the Court.
15             I would refer the Court to the head note on page 1 of the
16     decision.
17             Does Defence counsel also have a copy of this?  Okay, good.
18             In this case the appellant's brother was due to be tried on a
19     charge of robbery and two days before trial, the appellant called to see
20     a potential witness with whom he had been at school.  The appellant spoke
21     to the witness and told him that his brother had not been at the scene of
22     the robbery.  That was simply a factual inaccuracy and that as was all
23     that that person did.  He was charged with interfering with a witness.
24     And that court held "the offence of perverting the course of justice by
25     interfering with a potential witness could be committed where there is no
 1     evidence of any bribe, threat, undue pressure or other unlawful means
 2     since, notwithstanding the fact that the great majority of cases, the
 3     actus reus would be accompanied by such unlawful means and the use of
 4     unlawful means was not an essential ingredients of the offence.  It
 5     followed that, since there was an ample evidence on which the jury could
 6     find that the appellant's intention was to pervert the course of justice
 7     by persuading the witness to change his evidence, the appeal would be
 8     dismissed."
 9             I would ask this Court to take a look at the definition as
10     espoused by Maglov, because I think that a better construction would be
11     to focus on the intent of the actor.  The "likely to deter" seems to me
12     to be focussed on the success as it's associated with the victim and if
13     you have somebody like Bill Gates, I think you're going to have actions
14     that would not be covered by your own contempt statute that you are going
15     to want to have covered.
16             And there is another thing about Maglov which -- I think there is
17     an inconsistency, because Maglov says that you don't need to succeed in
18     interference, and I think that conflicts with this definition, that it's
19     likely to deter.  On the one hand you don't need to succeed in your
20     interference in order to commit contempt; but on the other hand it has to
21     be likely to deter.  So I think it's problematic.
22             That being said, I'm going to argue Maglov for the purpose of
23     this hearing because that is the latest definition that we have, and
24     frankly, it's one of the only definitions of interference with witnesses,
25     and also because in this case, it doesn't really matter because this is a
 1     case about suborning witness testimony, suborning perjury.  Either way,

 3     standard, both of them clearly qualify as proof beyond a reasonable doubt
