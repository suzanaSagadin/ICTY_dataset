20             Before I move on to look in more detail at each of these two
21     legal arguments, I want to take a few moments to just emphasize the key
22     concern underlying this ground of appeal and why we've brought this
23     matter to the attention of Your Honours.
24             As we've explained in our appeal brief and our reply brief, by
25     1991 when the crimes in this case commenced, the crimes against humanity
 1     doctrine had moved far beyond its origins in the Nuremberg charter.  It
 2     had developed into a broad-based human rights protection with the
 3     objective of protecting humanity dignity in all circumstances, whether in
 4     peacetime or during war.
 5             So the crimes against humanity doctrine by this time was no
 6     longer intended merely as a vehicle to supplement war crimes provisions,
 7     to cover that conduct which would not otherwise be caught within the
 8     reach of war crimes provisions; but rather, it had become a specialised
 9     vehicle for expressing the international community's condemnation of
10     crimes that were so widespread or systematic that the international
11     community as a whole was affected and not just the individual victim.
12             And it's against that background that I ask you to view Article 5
13     of our Statute.  Article 5 is, of course, one of the key avenues in our
14     Statute for responding to the ethnic cleansing that took place in the
15     former Yugoslavia, the practice that had so shocked the international
16     community and had led in large part to the establishment of this
17     Tribunal.  And the present case, of course, is a prime example of such an
18     ethnic cleansing campaign.  It would frustrate the object and purpose of
19     Article 5 to artificially extract from the victim group people who were
20     targeted as part of a single over-arching ethnic cleansing campaign
21     simply because they happened to be members of the armed forces.  In fact,
22     due to the system of territorial defence employed in the former
23     Yugoslavia, such an approach might in some cases lead to a situation
24     where a large part of the male population of the former Yugoslavia was
25     excluded from the reach of Article 5 even if they were targeted along
 1     with all the other members of their ethnic group.  And we submit that
 2     could not have been the Security Council's intention in Article 5.
 3             And overwhelmingly, Your Honours, the concluded cases before this
 4     Tribunal have rejected such an approach.  I'll touch on them only very
 5     briefly because they are referred to in our appeal brief and reply brief,
 6     but the Kordic Appeals Chamber in three separate instances included hors
 7     de combat victims amongst crimes against humanity convictions; the
 8     Naletilic Trial Chamber included forcing prisoners of war to perform work
 9     in dangerous conditions as part of the Article 5 convictions in that
10     case; the Krstic and Blagojevic, the Srebrenica cases, provide further
11     examples, where crimes against humanity convictions were entered in part
12     in respect of crimes committed against the Bosnian Muslim 28th Division,
13     which of course formed part of the column of men that was escaping
14     Srebrenica and was ultimately captured and executed.  And I can also add
15     to this list the Fofana appeal judgement rendered by the Special Court of
16     Sierra Leone, where crimes against humanity convictions were entered on
17     appeal in that case and included crimes against captured enemy
18     combatants, and I can refer Your Honours, for example, to paragraph 266,
19     321, and footnote 658 of that judgement and cross-referenced with the
20     trial judgement.
21             So, Your Honours, I submit that the result is clear.  The
22     question is:  What is the reasoning that leads to this conclusion?
23             And this brings me to the first part of my presentation today,
24     which is our chapeau argument.  We submit that individual victims of
25     crimes against humanity need not be civilians as long as they are
 1     illegally targeted as part of a widespread or systematic attack that is
 2     directed against civilians.  In essence, it's the widespread or
 3     systematic attack and not the individual acts of the accused that must
 4     target civilians.
 5             We accept, of course, that it's also necessary to determine that
 6     non-civilians have been harmed in a manner that is not lawful under
 7     international humanitarian law.  We certainly do not advocate, for
 8     example, that lawful combat deaths under IHL could somehow be made
 9     unlawful under crimes against humanity.
10             In your question in the Scheduling Order for today's hearing,
11     you've asked the parties to address the Prosecution's chapeau argument
12     specifically with reference to the wording of the chapeau in Article 5
13     and also with respect to the relevant jurisprudence.  I'll start with the
14     wording of the chapeau.  And, Your Honours, I'll ask our case manager to
15     bring this up on the screen for you because it's -- I think it's useful
16     to see the wording of the Statute as we're going through this.  And just
17     as a general comment, Your Honours, I will be using quite a few slides
18     during my presentation today, so you may wish to leave your monitors on,
19     as that will help me go through it in a quicker manner.
20             So, Your Honours, the wording of Article 5, the chapeau, is:
21     "The International Tribunal shall have power to prosecute persons
22     responsible for the following crimes when committed in armed conflict,
23     whether international or internal in character, and directed against any
24     civilian population ..."
25             Do these words "and directed against any civilian population"
 1     mean that the underlying acts of the accused, the murder, torture, rape,
 2     and so on must be directed at civilian victims?  We submit that the
 3     drafters of the Statute did not intend for Article 5 to be interpreted in
 4     that way, and I refer Your Honours to the report of the Secretary-General
 5     on the Statute of the Tribunal, and paragraph number 48, which will also
 6     appear on your screen, where the Secretary-General was commenting on
 7     Article 5, and he said in relevant part that:  "Crimes against humanity
 8     refer to inhumane acts of a very serious nature, such as wilful killing,
 9     torture, or rape, committed as part of a widespread or systematic attack
10     against any civilian population ..."
11             So the wording that the Secretary-General used here clarifies
12     that the wording of the Statute was intended to convey that the attack
13     rather than the individual acts of the accused must be directed against
14     civilians.  And we submit that this interpretation is confirmed in the
15     wording of Article 3 of the ICTR Statute, which followed subsequently,
16     and Article 3 states that:  "The International Tribunal for Rwanda shall
17     have power to prosecute persons responsible for the following crimes when
18     committed as part of a widespread or systematic attack against the
19     civilian population ..."
20             So this wording expressly reflects the interpretation of Article
21     5 that the Secretary-General gave in his report on this Tribunal's
22     Statute.  And we submit that this conclusion is also evident in the
23     Secretary-General's report on the ICTR Statute because the
24     Secretary-General commented on some of the differences in the wording
25     between Article 5 of our Statute and Article 3 of the ICTR Statute, but
 1     he made no reference to this issue, and we submit that's because the
 2     wording in Article 3 was considered to be synonymous with the words
 3     "directed against any civilian population" in our Statute.
 4             The Appeals Chamber has confirmed that the phrase "directed
 5     against any civilian population" requires that the crimes be committed as
 6     part of a widespread or systematic attack against civilians.  For
 7     example, the Tadic appeal judgement at paragraph 248 stated in relevant
 8     part that: " ...  it may be inferred from the words 'directed against any
 9     civilian population' in Article 5 of the Statute that the acts of the
10     accused must comprise part of a pattern of widespread or systematic
11     crimes directed against a civilian population ..."
12             And similarly in the Kunarac appeal judgement at paragraph 85,
13     the Appeals Chamber affirmed that the chapeau requirement for Article 5
14     requires that the attack be directed against the civilian population.  It
15     did not specify that the individual acts of the accused must target
16     civilians.  Certainly, the Kunarac Appeals Chamber emphasized that in
17     deciding whether an attack is directed primarily against civilians, it
18     will of course be relevant to look at the status of the victims and their
19     number, but there is nothing in the Kunarac appeal judgement that compels
20     the conclusion that acts targeting non-civilians must be necessarily
21     excluded.
22             Can Article 5 be interpreted to mean that both the attack and the
23     acts of the accused must be directed against civilians?  And again, I'll
24     ask Ms. Galicia to put up the wording of the Statute.
25             We submit that the answer to that question must be no in light of
 1     the Appeals Chamber's case law.  Your Honours, if the term "directed
 2     against any civilian population" was intended to qualify the acts of the
 3     accused, then it would be necessary to show not only that the acts of the
 4     accused were directed against civilians but also that they were directed
 5     against a 'population' in the sense of being part of a widespread or
 6     systematic -- in the sense of being a widespread or systematic course of
 7     events.
 8             For example, the Tadic trial judgement at paragraph 644 states
 9     that "the population element was included in Article 5 to imply crimes of
10     a collective nature and so exclude isolated or single acts."
11             But importantly, the Appeals Chamber has rejected the proposition
12     that the acts of the accused must be widespread or systematic and has
13     confirmed that a single act by an accused person can qualify as a crime
14     against humanity if it is committed in the context of the broader attack.
15     That's the Kunarac appeal judgement at paragraph 96 and the Blaskic
16     appeal judgement at paragraph 101.
17             So the point is really this, Your Honours:  If the acts of the
18     accused can be directed against a single victim, as long as they form
19     part of the broader attack, then it logically follows that the acts of
20     the accused need not necessarily target civilians as long as they form
21     part of an attack directed against civilians.
22             More generally, the Appeals Chamber's case law before both this
23     Tribunal and the ICTR confirms that the victim acts of the accused need
24     not share the required features of the broader attack.  For example, the
25     Appeals Chamber in the Kunarac case ruled that it does not matter whether
 1     the accused intended to target the civilian population as such or only
 2     his individual victim.  In particular, in confirming the irrelevance of
 3     person motives in assessing the acts of the accused, the Kunarac Appeals
 4     Chamber stated at paragraph 103:  "It is the attack, not the acts of the
 5     accused, which must be directed against the target population, and the
 6     accused need only know that his acts are part thereof."
 7             The case law of the ICTR Appeals Chamber provides a further
 8     example.  In Article 3 of the ICTR Statute, there is an additional
 9     requirement that the widespread and systematic attack against the
10     civilian population must also be committed on discriminatory grounds.  In
11     Akayesu, the Appeals Chamber ruled that the individual acts of the
12     accused need not be committed on discriminatory grounds as long as they
13     form part of a broader attack that is discriminatory.  That's at
14     paragraph 467 of the Akayesu appeal judgement.
15             So as the Kayishema Trial Chamber put it at paragraph 135:  "The
16     crimes themselves need not contain the three elements of the broader
17     attack under the ICTR Statute (that is, they need not be widespread or
18     systematic, they need not be directed against civilians, and they need
19     not be committed on discriminatory grounds, as long as they form part of
20     an attack that meets all those requirements."
21             Your Honours, we submit that the Appeals Chamber's application of
22     Article 5 to the facts that have arisen in cases before it confirms that
23     individual victims need not be civilians.
24             In particular, in affirming Kordic's conviction for crimes
25     against humanity in Santici, the Kordic Appeals Chamber stated at
 1     paragraph 480 that it included the killing of three identified victims,
 2     and it specifically stated that whether or not these individual victims
 3     were civilians was "beside the point."  This must mean that individual
 4     victims of crimes against humanity needn't be civilians.
 5             The conclusion on the Santici analysis is confirmed in the Kordic
 6     Appeals Chamber's approach to crimes in Rotilj.  The Appeals Chamber
 7     concluded that both to members and civilians were detained in Rotilj but
 8     that the majority were civilians and that there was an unlawful attack
 9     directed against civilians in that village, paragraphs 538 to 539 of the
10     judgement.  In convicting Kordic for murder as a crime against humanity,
11     the Appeals Chamber noted that seven individuals had been killed in the
12     village, but the Appeals Chamber importantly did not discuss their
13     individual status as civilians.  As the Appeals Chamber found at
14     paragraph 543:  "Taking into account that the killings occurred in the
15     context of an unlawful attack on civilians, the Appeals Chamber considers
16     that a reasonable trier of fact could have found that the elements of
17     murder" as a crime against humanity "were established."
18             The Martic Trial Chamber's conclusion, erroneous conclusion we
19     would submit, that individual victims must be civilians was based on a
20     misreading of the Blaskic appeal judgement.  At paragraph 107 of that
21     judgement, the Blaskic Appeals Chamber had stated in relevant part:  "The
22     Appeals Chamber considers that both the status of the victim as a
23     civilian and the scale on which it is committed or the level of
24     organization ... characterize a crime against humanity."
25             Your Honours, this passage must be read in context.  Here, the
 1     Appeals Chamber was discussing the chapeau requirement of Article 5 that
 2     the attack must be directed against civilians.  The Appeals Chamber here,
 3     we submit, was simply saying that both the widespread or systematic
 4     character of the attack and the fact that the attack must be directed
 5     against a civilian population characterize the chapeau element.
 6             Your Honours, I also refer you to the Tadic appeal judgement at
 7     paragraph 286, and in this passage the Appeals Chamber was commenting on
 8     the relationship between Article 3 of the Statute and Article 5 of the
 9     Statute, and it said in relevant part that: " ... those who drafted the
10     Statute deliberately included both classes of crimes," that is, war
11     crimes and crimes against humanity, "thereby illustrating their intention
12     that those war crimes which, in addition to targeting civilians as
13     victims, present special features such as the fact of being part of a
14     widespread or systematic practice, must be classified as crimes against
15     humanity and deserve to be punished accordingly."
16             To the extent that this could be read as suggesting individual
17     victims must be civilians, we submit that it is inconsistent with later
18     Appeals Chamber case law and that it should not be followed.
19             I must also acknowledge Trial Chamber authorities before both
20     this Tribunal and the ICTR that either expressly state or implicitly
21     assume individual victims must be civilians.  We have already cited a
22     number of these in our reply brief in footnote 2, and to these I should
23     add the following ICTR authorities:  The Rutuganda trial judgement at
24     paragraph 79 and the Akayesu trial judgement at paragraph 578, 582, and
25     589.  We submit that these Trial Chamber authorities are inconsistent
 1     with the Appeals Chamber case law that I've described and which support
 2     the Prosecution's chapeau argument.
 3             A final question might arise for Your Honours concerning the
 4     chapeau argument.  If crimes against civilians and non-civilians alike
 5     can qualify as crimes against humanity, what was the purpose of including
 6     the civilian requirement in Article 5 at all?  Why insist on an attack
 7     against a civilian population if the individual victims can either be
 8     civilian or non-civilian?
 9             We submit that the requirement of an attack directed against a
10     civilian population serves to rule out legitimate combat action.
11     Although under customary international law crimes against humanity can of
12     course occur in peacetime or in war, the fact remains that in Article 5
13     of our Statute we are applying it in or to the context of an armed
14     conflict.  To respect IHL's lex specialis status, we must ensure that
15     lawful combat under IHL is not caught within the reach of Article 5; and
16     that, we submit, is the purpose of including the civilian population
17     requirement.
18             Your Honours, this brings me to my second argument, the civilian
19     definition argument.  If, as we advocate under the chapeau argument, the
20     civilian population requirement serves to rule out legitimate combat
21     action, then it logically follows that the term "civilian" should be
22     interpreted to mean people who are not lawful targets of attack under
23     IHL, people whom it is not lawful to direct combat action against.  As I
24     said at the beginning of my submissions, the chapeau argument and the
25     civilian definition argument are really mutually reinforcing arguments.
 1             And we submit that in this context the relevant IHL principle to
 2     take into Article 5 is that anyone who is not directly participating in
 3     the hostilities is protected from attack, and this is reflected in Common
 4     Article 3 to the Geneva Conventions and, of course, the principle of
 5     distinction, which underpins so much of international humanitarian law.
 6     These people covered by Common Article 3 are properly regarded as
 7     civilians in the specific sense in which that term is used in Article 5.
 8             Let me make it clear at the outset that when I say individuals
 9     not participating in the hostilities, I don't mean members of the armed
10     forces who simply happen not to be engaged in combat or happen not to be
11     armed at a particular point in time.  We fully accept that as the Blaskic
12     Appeals Chamber rightly found at paragraph 114, members of the armed
13     forces remain lawful targets even when they are not engaged in combat or
14     happen not to be armed.  We mean non-combatants in the sense of Common
15     Article 3, people who are protected from attack, including persons hors
16     de combat because they've been rendered incapable of combat.
17             By contrast, the Trial Chamber in this case defined civilians
18     purely by reference to non-membership of the armed forces.  And for
19     reasons that I'll explain, we submit that was the wrong bench-mark to use
20     in the context of Article 5, that it conflicts with the principle of
21     distinction, and that it's contrary to the object and purpose of Article
22     5 as I've outlined in my submissions.
23             We fully accept that IHL is an important bench-mark and an
24     important reference point for defining the term "civilian" in Article 5.
25     But we submit - and this is really the key point that I want to convey to
 1     Your Honours today - that the issue is more nuanced than simply
 2     identifying a corresponding label in IHL and transposing that into the
 3     distinctive Article 5 context.  Instead, it's important to examine and to
 4     fully understand the concepts underpinning the labels in IHL and the
 5     purpose that they play in that particular regime and then to assess how
 6     appropriate it is to transfer them into the context of Article 5.
 7             It's a complex point, Your Honours, and I'm going to try and
 8     attempt to illustrate it with the aid of some diagrams because I think
 9     this is a case where pictures really do assist more than words.  So what
10     I have is a series of four charts that I'm going to put up on the
11     monitors for Your Honours, and the first one is on your screens now, and
12     I've called this:  Article 5:  Who Is a Civilian?  And you can see there,
13     Your Honours, that there are four boxes, four blue boxes, each numbered.
14     And these boxes represent the various categories of people that we are
15     concerned with in answering the question before you.
16             So in box number 1, we have non-members of the armed forces not
17     directly participating in hostilities; in the second box, we have
18     non-members of the armed forces who are directly participating in the
19     hostilities; in box 3, we have members of the armed forces not directly
20     participating in the hostilities, and here we include persons hors de
21     combat for the reasons I've explained; and in box 4, finally, we have
22     members of the armed forces directly participating in the hostilities.
23             So how should we classify each of these groups for Article 5
24     purposes?  Well, box number 1 as demonstrated on the chart is not
25     difficult.  That's the easy part of this question.  As the down arrow on
 1     the chart from box number 1 shows, these people are always non-combatants
 2     in IHL, and they are clearly civilians for Article 5 purposes.  On the
 3     other side of the diagram in box number 4, members of the armed forces
 4     directly participating in the hostilities.  To the extent that these
 5     people are enemy combatants who've been lawfully targeted in accordance
 6     with IHL, they are not civilians for Article 5 purposes.  So for our
 7     purposes today, we can also exclude these from consideration.  But as the
 8     chart shows with box number 2 and box 3, there is a question as to how to
 9     properly classify these other two groups of people.
10             Let's consider what the Trial Chamber did in the present case,
11     and this brings me to the second chart, which I've called:  IHL's
12     Technical Definition of Non-Combatant and Civilian.
13             Again, we have the same four boxes from the previous chart
14     representing our categories of people, and we can see that the
15     Trial Chamber's approach was to classify as civilians the people in box
16     number 1 and box 2.  These are non-combatants or civilians in the
17     technical meaning of Article 50 of Protocol I.  It's a definition that
18     reflects the fact that they are non-members of the armed forces who are
19     not entitled to participate in the hostilities.  So let me just go over
20     some of the salient points that emerge from this chart.
21             Why have I called it a technical definition?  Well, it's
22     technical because it reflects the particular characteristics of
23     international armed conflicts, where combatant status, that term
24     combatant, carries with it certain so-called privileges such as the right
25     to use violent force without being subjected to punishment.  States have
 1     clearly stipulated that only members of the armed forces are entitled to
 2     benefit from this privilege.  And we see this reflected, for example, in
 3     Article 43(2) of Protocol I, which states that members of the armed
 4     forces of a party to the conflict ... are combatants, that is to say,
 5     they have the right to participate directly in hostilities."
 6             So it's a definition tied up with entitlement to participate,
 7     regardless of whether these people actually do participate or not.
 8             We fully accept that persons hors de combat remain as members of
 9     the armed forces combatants in this technical sense of Article 50.  By
10     contrast, non-members of the armed forces do not have these privileges,
11     and they can be punished if they participate in the hostilities.
12             So I submit, Your Honours, that the Trial Chamber overlooked the
13     purpose underlying the Article 50 civilian definition.  It is not a
14     definition that is synonymous with protected status under IHL.  In
15     particular, if non-members of the armed forces, the people in box number
16     2, do participate in the hostilities, under Article 50 they retain their
17     civilian status but they lose their protection from attack as set out,
18     for example, in Article 51(3) of Additional Protocol I.  So as this chart
19     shows, in adopting the Article 50 approach the Trial Chamber has included
20     in the definition of "civilian" the people in box number 2 who are
21     actually lawful targets of attack under IHL.
22             The Trial Chamber also overlooked the fact - and this is the
23     second problem that we see with the Trial Chamber's approach - that there
24     is another concept of non-combatant in IHL regulating both international
25     and internal armed conflicts, and we can see this reflected in the next
 1     slide which I've called:  IHL's Generic Definition of
 2     Non-Combatant/Civilian.  Again, we have the same four boxes representing
 3     the categories of people at issue, but here what we see is that box
 4     number 1 and box 3 make up non-combatants in the generic sense in which
 5     that term is used.  And I use the word "generic" because it's a term that
 6     has been used in this context in the ICRC customary law study, and I'll
 7     explain a little bit more about that in a moment.
 8             This concept of non-combatant is reflected in Common Article 3 of
 9     the Geneva Conventions, and it conveys the important fact for Article 5
10     purposes that these are people who are protected from attack under IHL.
11             Persons hors de combat in box number 3 are regarded as
12     non-combatants in this generic sense.  They are protected from attack as
13     set out, for example, in Article 41 of Protocol I.  In this context, even
14     in IHL, persons hors de combat are assimilated with civilians who do not
15     participate in the hostilities and are protected from attack.  So, for
16     example, the ICRC commentary to Article 41 of Protocol I at paragraph
17     1605 states that:  "It is a fundamental principle of the law of war that
18     those who do not participate in the hostilities shall not be attacked.
19     In this respect, harmless civilians and soldiers hors de combat are a
20     priori on the same footing."
21             Other sources also underscore that persons hors de combat are
22     non-combatants in this generic sense of the term.  For example, as we've
23     referred to in our appeal brief at paragraph 31, persons hors de combat
24     are referred to as non-combatants in both the drafting history and the
25     commentary to Article 41 of Protocol I.
 1             Military manuals also reflect this conclusion.  For example, the
 2     US Law on Naval Operations, 1995, at section 11.1 states that:
 3     "Non-combatants are non-members of the armed forces who do not
 4     participate in the hostilities," so our box number 1, people, "and
 5     members of the armed forces who enjoy special protected status, including
 6     persons who have been rendered incapable of combat by wounds, sickness,
 7     shipwreck, or capture."  In other words, persons hors de combat are
 8     people in box number 3.
 9             And as I've said, the best articulation of this generic concept
