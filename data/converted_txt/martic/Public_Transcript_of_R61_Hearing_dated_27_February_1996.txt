  8             Some outlining facts might serve the purpose to
  9       facilitate the understanding.  By the late 1980s, ethnic
 10       tensions emerged in many parts of the former Yugoslavia,
 11       not least in Croatia, where Croat Serbs protest actions
 12       against alleged Croat repressions took place.  The
 13       multi-party elections in the republics of Yugoslavia
 14       brought strong nationalist parties, which accelerated
 15       disintegration of the Federal Republic.  As Macedonia
 16       and Slovenia had done, Croatia followed in the
 17       referendum and declared its independence in June 1991.
 18             The Serbian minority of Croatia, approximately
 19       half a million people, boycotted the referendum.  They
 20       already had had one of their own, forming the Serbian
 21       autonomous region of Krajina, and declared on
 22       1st October 1990 their autonomy and thus independence
 23       from Croatia.  The Krajina Serbs mobilised to protect
 24       themselves, and in response Croatia did the same, and in
 25       October/November 1990 an armed conflict erupted between
  1       the parties in many parts of Croatia.
  2             After the declaration of independence of Croatia
  3       in June 1991, fighting between the Croatian armed forces
  4       and the army of Krajina Serbs intensified.  The Krajina
  5       Serbs, since February 1991 the Serbian republic of
  6       Krajina, were extensively supported by the Federal
  7       Republic of Yugoslavia and its army.  Serbian
  8       paramilitary groupings with close links to that army
  9       also assisted the Krajina Serbs.
 10             The armed conflict continued for the duration of
 11       1991 and resulted in the Serbs seizing approximately 25
 12       per cent of the territory of Croatia.
 13             During this time, the Hague Peace Conference had
 14       failed to achieve peace, and the situation had
 15       deteriorated to such an extent that an arms embargo was
 16       imposed on Yugoslavia by the Security Council on 25th
 17       September 1991.  At the beginning of October 1991,
 18       nevertheless, the siege of Dubrovnik commenced and
 19       attacks were launched on Vukovar by the Yugoslavian
 20       National Army.

  4       deployed into four United Nations Protection Areas,

  7             Despite the Vance Plan, numerous violations of the
  8       January 1992 ceasefire occurred as hostilities between
  9       the Croatians and the armed forces of the Republic of
 10       Serbian Krajina sporadically erupted during 1992 and

 12       various agreements were reached.  The ceasefire

 23             I will now proceed by addressing some legal issues
 24       pertaining to the indictment.
 25             Milan Martic is charged with being responsible for
  1       unlawful attacks against the civilian population and
  2       individual civilians of Zagreb, contrary to Article 3 of
  3       the Statute of our Tribunal, which permits the Tribunal
  4       to prosecute persons committing violations of the laws
  5       and customs of war.  In the decision of the Appeals
  6       Chamber of this Tribunal on 2nd October 1995 on the
  7       Tadic jurisdictional motion, the Appeals Chamber
  8       addressed the scope of Article 3 at some length,
  9       particularly in paragraphs 94, 126, and 143 of the
 10       majority judgment.
 11             In brief, the Chamber indicated that Article 3 was
 12       a general or residual clause covering all violations of
 13       humanitarian law not falling within Articles 2, 4 or 5.
 14       It indicated that, where violations of humanitarian law
 15       were embodied in customary international law, some
 16       customary law rules applied exclusively to international
 17       conflicts, some to internal conflicts and some to all
 18       conflicts.  Further, it indicated that, where violations
 19       of humanitarian law were referred to in treaties, it
 20       must be established that the treaties were binding on
 21       the parties at the time of the alleged offence.
 22             It is the submission of the Prosecution that the
 23       existence of an armed conflict involving the armed
 24       forces of Croatia and the armed forces of the
 25       self-proclaimed Republic of Serbian Krajina is
  1       indisputable.  The only question which might be raised
  2       is:  is the conflict internal or international?
  3       Whether, on the basis of the evidence we are going to
  4       present during this hearing, the Trial Chamber will
  5       conclude that the conflict is internal or international,
  6       the offences remain the same.
  7             If the conflict is international, Article 85 of
  8       Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Convention prohibits
  9       making the civilian population or individual civilians
 10       the object of attack in international armed conflicts,
 11       and Additional Protocol I was in force as a result of
 12       ratification by the Socialist Federal Republic of
 13       Yugoslavia in 1979 and accession by Croatia on 11th May
 14       1992.
 15             If the conflict is internal, Article 13 of
 16       Additional Protocol II to the Geneva Convention
 17       prohibits making the civilian population or individual
 18       civilians the object of attack in internal conflicts,
 19       and Additional Protocol II was in force as a result.
 20       Accession by Croatia, also, on 11th May 1992.
 21             Further, if it is not considered practicable to
 22       classify the conflict as international or internal, the
 23       Appeals Chamber has indicated, in paragraphs 110, 111
 24       and 112 of the majority judgment of the Tadic
 25       jurisdictional motion, that the principles of the UN
  1       General Assembly Resolutions 2444 of 19th December 1968,
  2       and 2675 of 9th December 1970, are part of customary law
  3       and applicable to all conflicts regardless of
  4       classification.  Both of these resolutions indicate that
  5       it is prohibited to launch attacks against the civilian
  6       population as such.  It is the submission of the
  7       Prosecution that the charges alleged against the accused
  8       can be committed in both international and internal
  9       conflicts, although the underlying legal basis for the
 10       charges varies depending on the nature of the conflict.
 11             I will now, as the concluding part of my opening
 12       statement, turn to the doctrine of reprisals.  I have
 13       just stated that it is the submission of the Prosecution
 14       that it is prohibited to launch attacks against the
 15       civilian populations as such.
 16             It is also the submission of the Prosecution that
 17       reprisals against civilian populations or civilians are
 18       prohibited under international law, whether in
 19       international or internal armed conflicts.  In the
 20       alternative, it is submitted that, to the extent that
 21       such reprisals may not be prohibited, they constitute an
 22       exception to the general rule of humanitarian protection
 23       and, consequently, their lawfulness is conditioned on
 24       the satisfaction of certain very strict criteria.
 25             In 1970, the General Assembly of the United
  1       Nations unanimously adopted resolution 2675 on:  "Basic
  2       principles for the protection of civilian populations in
  3       armed conflicts", provided expressly that, "civilian
  4       populations, or individual members thereof should not be
  5       the object of reprisals, forcible transfers, or other
  6       assaults on their integrity."
  7             Although resolutions of the General Assembly are
  8       not considered legally binding when they are concerned
  9       with principles of international law, a unanimous or
 10       majority vote constitutes an important expression of
 11       opinio juris.  Thus, the Appeals Chamber of the Tribunal
 12       considers resolution 2675 as, I quote the decision on
 13       jurisdiction on the Tadic case, page 61, paragraph 112:
 14             "Declaratory of the principles of customary
 15       international law regarding the protection of civilian
 16       populations and property in armed conflict of any
 17       kind".
 18             With respect to the minimum humanitarian standards
 19       under customary law, the dichotomy between international
 20       and internal armed conflicts is increasingly untenable.
 21       I quote again from the same decision of the Appeals
 22       Chamber, page 54, paragraph 97:
 23             "A state-sovereignty-oriented approach has been
 24       gradually supplanted by a human-being-oriented
 25       approach... It follows that, in the area of armed
  1       conflict, the distinction between interstate wars and
  2       civil wars is losing its value as far as human beings
  3       are concerned.  Why protect civilians from belligerent
  4       violence, or ban rape, torture or the wanton destruction
  5       of hospitals, churches, museums, or private property, as
  6       well as proscribe weapons causing unnecessary suffering
  7       when two sovereign States are engaged in war, and yet
  8       refrain from enacting the same bans or providing the
  9       same protection when armed violence has erupted 'only'
 10       within the territory of a sovereign State?  If
 11       international law, while of course duly safeguarding the
 12       legitimate interests of States, must gradually turn to
 13       the protection of human beings, it is only natural that
 14       the aforementioned dichotomy should gradually lose its
 15       weight."
 16             The 1977 Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions of
 17       1949 provides in categorical terms that in an
 18       international armed conflict, "Attacks against the
 19       civilian population or civilians by way of reprisals are
 20       prohibited".  Article 52, paragraph 1, of the same
 21       Protocol, provides that, "Civilian objects shall not be
 22       the object of attack or of reprisals."
 23             The former Yugoslavia and the Republic of Croatia
 24       ratified Protocol I in 1979 and 1992 respectively.
 25       Consequently, Protocol I was applicable for the entire
  1       duration of the armed conflict throughout the territory
  2       of the former Yugoslavia, including Croatia, and may
  3       thus be considered as part of the "laws or customs of
  4       war" within the meaning of Article 3 of our Statute.
  5             The 1977 Protocol II, also to the Geneva
  6       Conventions, defines the "fundamental guarantees" in
  7       Article 4, paragraph 1, that:
  8             "Persons who do take a direct part or who have
  9       ceased to take part in hostilities... shall in all
 10       circumstances be treated humanely", and in Article 13,
 11       paragraph 1, it goes on:
 12             "The civilian population, and individual
 13       civilians shall enjoy the general protection against the
 14       dangers arising from military operations", and then it
 15       goes on again: "The civilian population as such, as well
 16       as individual civilians, shall not be the object of
 17       attack. Acts or threats of violence, where the primary
 18       purpose of it is to spread terror among the civilian
 19       populations, are prohibited."
 20             It is significant that the International Committee
 21       of the Red Cross as the "guardian" of international
 22       humanitarian law has adopted the position that the
 23       prohibitions on inhumane treatment under Article 4 of
 24       Protocol II, "are explicit and do not allow for any
 25       exception", because, "They are absolute obligations"
  1       from which "no derogation is allowed", and consequently,
  2       "there is, in fact, no room left at all for carrying
  3       out 'reprisals' against protected persons".  All this is
  4       quoted from the ICRC Commentary to Protocols, for these
  5       Protocols of the Geneva Convention.
  6             The ICRC observes that the absence of an express
  7       reference to "reprisals" in Protocol II is because of
  8       "reasons of legal and political nature" and that in the
  9       absence of such  provisions, reprisals against civilians
 10       are "implicitly prohibited".
 11             I stressed  that the lawfulness of reprisals was
 12       subject to strict criteria.  A concise statement of
 13       these criteria or general rules, including those
 14       incorporated in most national law of war manuals, is
 15       contained in Chapter G of the ICRC Handbook on the Law
 16       of War for Armed Forces, generally called the "ICRC
 17       Handbook", which is extensively quoted in the brief on
 18       applicable law which we have submitted to the Trial
 19       Chamber.
 20             It is the submission of the Prosecution that, to
 21       the extent that reprisals against civilians may not be
 22       prohibited in internal armed conflicts, reprisals
 23       constitute a narrow exception to the general rule that
 24       civilians should be protected against inhumane acts and
 25       attacks under all circumstances.  Consequently, the
  1       lawfulness of reprisals against civilians is conditioned
  2       on the strict satisfaction of certain criteria, such
  3       that a breach of any of the conditions would render the
  4       action unlawful.  It is the submission of the
  5       Prosecution that reprisals against civilians constitute
  6       such a significant deviation from the elementary norms
  7       of international humanitarian law, that if serious or
  8       deliberate attacks on civilians are established beyond a
  9       reasonable doubt, there is a presumption that such
 10       action was unlawful in the absence of proof to the
 11       contrary.
 12             This issue is, in more detail, argued in a brief
 13       on applicable law submitted to the Trial Chamber
 14       yesterday.  I conclude my opening statement by saying:
 15       the shelling of Zagreb was not a reprisal.  It was a
 16       terror retaliation and it was unlawful.

  1       law, your Honours can conclude that reprisals against
  2       civilians are forbidden, be it an international or an
  3       internal conflict.
  4             Clearly, on Protocol I reprisals against the
  5       civilian population in international conflicts are
  6       forbidden per Article 51.  It is specifically forbidden,
  7       so we need not even discuss it.
  8             Under Protocol II, which refers to internal
  9       conflicts, the guiding principle, the ideological
 10       approach of Protocol II is the safeguarding of
 11       civilians.  While there is no specific delineation
 12       saying that reprisals against civilians are forbidden in
 13       Protocol II, there is (1) clearly an attitude among the
 14       signees of Protocol II to protect civilians and (2)
 15       clearly there was no intent, when Protocol II was being
 16       ratified and negotiated with individuals, to allow for
 17       reprisals against civilians.  In fact, if one looks at
 18       the legislative history behind Protocol II, you examine
 19       that the failure to specifically preclude reprisals did
 20       not in any way, shape or form mean that reprisals
 21       against civilians in the internal context were allowed.
 22             There were several reasons, when you examine the
 23       legislative history for that particular provision to be
 24       excluded, some political, some legal.  Some individuals

  1       no one during those proceedings that came forward to

  5       now attitude of humanitarian law.

  8       of the leading eyes on humanitarian law throughout the
  9       world, quite clearly in the commentaries of Protocol II,

 11       Protocol II are forbidden.
 12             Any way we look at it, your Honours, be it in the
 13       international context or internal conflict, it makes
 14       simply no difference.  At this stage of humanitarian
 15       law, and where we are in customary law and conventional
 16       law, it is the position of the Prosecutor that reprisals
 17       and attacks on the civilian population in any way, shape
