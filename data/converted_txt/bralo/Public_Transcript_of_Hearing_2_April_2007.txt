25            As the Registry has announced the proceedings concern the case,
 1    the Prosecutor versus Miroslav Bralo.  As indicated in the Scheduling
 2    Order of 15 March 2007, the Appeals Chamber is sitting today to pronounce
 3    the judgement in this case.  This hearing is being held pursuant to Rule
 4    117(D) of the Rules of Procedure and Evidence of the Tribunal.
 5            In accordance with Tribunal practice, I will not read out the text
 6    of the judgement, except for the disposition.  After recalling the main
 7    issues raised in the appellate proceedings, I will give the conclusions of
 8    the Appeals Chamber.
 9            I would like to point out that the summary which follows is not an
10    integral part of the judgement.  The authoritative version of the
11    conclusions and rationale of the Appeals Chamber may be found only in the
12    written judgement, copies of which will be made available to the parties
13    at the conclusion of this hearing.
14            The facts giving rise to the present appeal took place from April
15    to July 1993 in Central Bosnia, in the villages of Ahmici, Nadioci and
16    surrounding areas.  Miroslav Bralo was born on 13 October 1967, and was a
17    member of the Jokeri, a Croatian Defence Council (HVO) 4th Military Police
18    Battalion anti-terrorist unit.  On the 6th April 1993, he participated in
19    an attack on Ahmici, the objective of which was to ethnically cleanse the
20    village, to kill the Muslim men of weapons-bearing age, to burn all of the
21    houses belonging to Muslims, and to expel all of the Muslim inhabitants
22    out of the village.
23            In the sentencing judgement of 7 December 2005, the Trial Chamber
24    sentenced Miroslav Bralo to a term of imprisonment of 20 years after
25    accepting his guilty plea of 19 July 2005 regarding eight counts against
 1    him, specifically:  One count of persecution on political, racial and
 2    religious grounds pursuant to Articles 5(h) and 7(1) of the statute; four
 3    counts of grave breaches of the Geneva Conventions of 1949, including acts
 4    of torture or inhuman treatment and unlawful confinement, punishable under
 5    Articles 2(b), 2(g) and 7(1) of the statute; three counts of violations of
 6    the laws or customs of war punishable under Articles 3 and 7(1) of the
 7    statute, including murder, torture and outrages upon personal dignity,
 8    including rape.
 9            Mr. Bralo appealed the sentencing judgement on 5th January 2006,
10    and filed his appeal brief on 30th March 2006.  I would recall that during
11    the appellate proceedings the Appeals Chamber admitted three additional
12    exhibits in accordance with Rule 115 of the Rules of Procedure and
13    Evidence.  We also received two additional briefs from the Appellant in
14    support of his second and third grounds of appeal.  Finally, it should be
15    noted that the prosecution requests that all of the Appellant's grounds of
16    appeal be dismissed.
17            The appellant argues in general that the Trial Chamber erred in
18    law and abused its discretion by imposing an excessive sentence.  He
19    asserts that the sentence imposed on him resulted in a miscarriage of
20    justice since it fails to appropriately take into account the significant
21    mitigating circumstances of the case.  In support of his appeal, he
22    submits in particular that:  First, the Trial Chamber was wrong to
23    consider that certain factors were "not relevant to the sentence" and find
24    that they did not constitute a mitigating circumstance; second, the Trial
25    Chamber did not attach sufficient weight to the factors it accepted; and
 1    finally, the Trial Chamber should have pronounced a lighter sentence,
 2    considering the number and significance of the mitigating circumstances.
 3            The Appeals Chamber has examined each of these claims as separate
 4    grounds of appeal.  Before addressing them, it is appropriate to recall
 5    the applicable criteria for alleged errors of fact and of law in appeal.
 6            Appeals against sentencing judgements, like those against any
 7    judgement, are appeals stricto sensu; they are of a "corrective nature"
 8    and do not result in trials de novo.  The Appeals Chamber will, in
 9    principle take into account only the following factual evidence:  Evidence
10    referred to by the Trial Chamber; evidence contained in the trial record
11    and referred to by the parties; and additional evidence admitted on
12    appeal.  The Appeals Chamber notes that whenever it is seized of an error
13    of fact or of law with respect to mitigating circumstances, the conclusion
14    that a fact represents a mitigating circumstance is arrived at on the
15    balance of probabilities.
16            Under Article 25 of the statute, the role of the Appeals Chamber
17    is limited to correcting errors of law invalidating a decision and errors
18    of fact which have occasioned a miscarriage of justice.  Trial Chambers
19    are vested with a broad discretion in determining an appropriate
20    sentence.  To a large extent, this stems from their obligation to
21    individualise the penalties to fit the individual circumstances of the
22    accused and the gravity of the crime.  Accordingly, as a general rule, the
23    Appeals Chamber will not revise a sentence unless the Trial Chamber has
24    committed a discernible error in exercising its discretion or has departed
25    from the applicable law.  It is for the Appellant to demonstrate how the
 1    Trial Chamber ventured outside its large discretionary framework in
 2    imposing the sentence.
 3            We will now examine the first ground of appeal regarding the
 4    factors which, according to the Appellant, the Trial Chamber failed to
 5    consider as mitigating.  More specifically, the Appellant refers to the
 6    following:  One, the deteriorating military and political situation in the
 7    Lasva Valley in 1992 and 1993; second, the attack against the Appellant
 8    and his wife in their home in February 1993; thirdly, the fact that the
 9    Appellant was released from prison in exchange for his participation in
10    the attack against the village of Ahmici; fourthly, the way in which he
11    was used by his superiors.
12            Generally the Appeals Chamber observes that even if the Appellant
13    alleges that the Trial Chamber erred in law by failing to take certain
14    factors into account, the issue before it is not to determine whether the
15    factors mentioned by the Appellant constitute mitigating circumstances in
16    law, but whether the Trial Chamber improperly exercised its discretion by
17    deciding which factors to consider.  Moreover, the only mitigating
18    circumstance that Trial Chambers are required to consider by law, in
19    accordance with Rule 101(B)(i) of the Rules of Procedure and Evidence, is
20    the cooperation with the Prosecution by the Accused.
21            As regards the deteriorating military and political situation in
22    Vitez, the Appeals Chamber notes, as the Appellant acknowledges, that
23    Trial Chamber did, in fact, take into account the arguments put forward in
24    his sentencing brief and during the sentencing hearing.  The Trial Chamber
25    did not therefore disregard the Appellant's arguments, but rather declined
 1    to consider that a chaotic context was a mitigating circumstance.  The
 2    Appellant simply repeats once again his submissions to the Trial Chamber
 3    and fails to demonstrate that the Trial Chamber committed a discernible
 4    error.  In any case, the Appeals Chamber has already concluded that the
 5    chaotic context of a conflict may not be considered as a mitigating
 6    circumstance.  For these reasons, this part of the first ground of appeal
 7    raised by the Appellant is dismissed.
 8            With regard to the attack against the Appellant and his wife in
 9    their home in February 1993, the Appeals Chamber notes that the Trial
10    Chamber examined the Appellant's submissions in light of the military and
11    political situation in Vitez municipality.  The Appeals Chamber considers
12    that the Trial Chamber had no need to rely on specific examples.  Whether
13    the chaotic context is borne in mind or whether specific examples of
14    attacks against houses are considered, the reasoning is the same:  Any
15    participants in a conflict have an obligation to reduce that chaos and
16    respect international humanitarian law.  An Accused may not therefore rely
17    solely on the fact that his home was attacked to request a reduction of
18    the sentence imposed on him for crimes he committed subsequently.
19    Consequently, this part of the Appellant's first ground of appeal is
20    dismissed.
21            Finally, regarding the arguments related to duress and the orders
22    given by his superiors, the Appeals Chamber notes that the Appellant
23    acknowledged that he "always refused to plead duress or that he was acting
24    under superior orders," and from that the Chamber infers that he does not
25    intend to raise these defences for the first time on appeal.  The Appeals
 1    Chamber notes that at the time the sentence was determined, the Appellant
 2    only indicated that his superiors had used him.  The Trial Chamber
 3    specifically had these arguments in mind and recognised that the Appellant
 4    had been released from prison in exchange for his participation in the
 5    attack against Ahmici and that, to a certain extent, he had been pressured
 6    to rejoin the ranks of the Jokeri and to actively participate in HVO
 7    combat operations.
 8            The Appeals Chamber stresses the fact that the Appellant, who does
 9    not dispute the Trial Chamber's conclusions does not claim that the
10    pressures exerted on him to join the Jokeri was stronger than the Trial
11    Chamber considered it was.  The Appeals Chamber considers that the
12    Appellant's arguments relate to his feeling that he was obliged to obey
13    illegal orders.  Nevertheless, the Appeals Chamber agrees with the
14    Prosecution and finds that the Appellant has failed to demonstrate that he
15    obeyed illegal orders under "duress."  The Trial Chamber rightly concluded
16    that it is the duty of any person involved in an armed conflict to comply
17    fully with the relevant norms of international humanitarian law and, while
18    Bralo may have been pressured to participate in combat activities, he
19    remained legally and morally obliged to conduct himself in accordance with
20    those norms.  Moreover, the Appeals Chamber considers that the Appellant
21    not only failed to oppose the illegal orders but acted on them
22    enthusiastically and willingly, as shown by the Trial Chamber's
23    conclusions regarding his desire to humiliate his victims.  Consequently
24    the Appeals Chamber concludes that the Appellant has failed to demonstrate
25    that the Trial Chamber committed a discernible error when it found that
 1    the pressure exerted on him did not constitute a mitigating circumstance.
 2            Accordingly the first ground of appeal is dismissed in its
 3    entirety.
 4            In his second ground of appeal, the Appellant argues that the
 5    Trial Chamber erred by failing to give sufficient weight to his personal
 6    situation and to the "very positive effect of his cooperation with the
 7    Prosecutor, the Tribunal and the residents of Ahmici."
 8            As regards the arguments whereby the Trial Chamber only took into
 9    account evidence of the Appellant's "prior good character" and "family
10    situation" in its assessment of his personal situation, the Appeals
11    Chamber recalls that the Trial Chamber is not required to examine the
12    arguments of the Appellant in any given part of the judgement.  In this
13    case, the Trial Chamber took into account all of the circumstances of the
14    case and did not attach any weight to them.
15            With regard to the Trial Chamber's alleged failure to attach
16    sufficient weight to the fact that the Appellant had lost his job as a
17    result of the war, the Appeals Chamber considers that the Appellant has
18    failed to demonstrate how the loss of his job, in the difficult economic
19    conditions which prevailed after the disintegration of Yugoslavia, could
20    have placed him in a more difficult situation compared to that of other
21    inhabitants of the region.
22            With regard to the Appellant's argument that the home in which he
23    was born and raised was located on the front-line, the Appeals Chamber
24    notes that it was not put forth at the time the sentence was determined
25    and, as such, the Appeals Chamber has no information to assess it.
 1    Consequently, the Appeals Chamber will not entertain the argument.
 2            Regarding the objection that the Trial Chamber failed to take into
 3    account the fact that the HVO detained the Appellant for nearly two years
 4    after his attempted surrender in 1997, the Appeals Chamber notes that it
 5    is, in fact, not certain whether the Trial Chamber specifically took this
 6    factor into account.  That being the case, even if that factor was not
 7    taken into account, the Appeals Chamber considers that the Appellant has
 8    failed to establish what impact it could have had on his sentence,
 9    especially since he failed to explain why he remained in HVO custody.  The
10    Appeals Chamber concludes that the Appellant has failed to establish that
11    the Trial Chamber committed a discernible error in exercising its
12    discretion by failing to take this factor into account.
13            As regards the balance between the seriousness of the crimes
14    committed by the Appellant and his personal situation, the Appeals Chamber
15    recalls its established case law whereby a Trial Chamber is open "to weigh
16    the mitigating circumstances against other factors, such as, the gravity
17    of the crime, the particular circumstances of the case and the form and
18    degree of the participation of the accused in the crime."
19            The Appeals Chamber notes that in this case, when the Trial
20    Chamber assessed the factors to take into account in sentencing, it
21    considered that the crimes committed by the Appellant were "extremely
22    serious."  Beyond the "inherently shocking nature of these crimes," the
23    Trial Chamber particularly took into account the manner in which they were
24    committed and the impact they had on the victims.  The Appeals Chamber
25    considers that therefore by assessing the specific circumstances of the
 1    Appellant, the Trial Chamber also rightly took into account the
 2    seriousness of the crimes.  This part of the second ground of appeal
 3    raised by the Appellant is therefore dismissed.
 4            Regarding the argument whereby the Trial Chamber failed to take
 5    into account his conciliatory attitude prior to the guilty plea, the
 6    Appeals Chamber considers that by failing to explain how the Trial Chamber
 7    made a discernible error, the Appellant failed to discharge the applicable
 8    burden in his appeal.  His arguments are therefore dismissed because they
 9    are evidently unfounded.
10            As regards the importance of the guilty plea and the summary of
11    facts, the Appeals Chamber recalls that the plea agreement must only be
12    founded pursuant to Rule 62 bis (iv), be founded on a sufficient factual
13    basis agreed by the parties, which is assessed during the evaluation of
14    the guilty plea.  Nevertheless, this does not preclude the Trial Chamber
15    from considering any other additional information provided by the Accused,
16    going beyond the "sufficient factual basis" necessary for the acceptance
17    of the plea.  The Appeals Chamber concludes that by taking into account
18    the fact that the Appellant had provide information about other crimes
19    when it assessed the value of his guilty plea and considered that it
20    constituted a "significant factor in mitigation," the Trial Chamber
21    committed no discernible error by not attaching any particular weight to
22    that factor, beyond the significant weight already given to his guilty
23    plea.
24            With respect to the Appellant's argument that he helped
25    to "establish a record of the past," the Appeals Chamber considers that
 1    while the Trial Chamber did not specifically mention this contribution, it
 2    is by its very nature taken into account in the weight attached to the
 3    guilty plea.  The contribution of an Accused to the establishment of the
 4    truth is indeed one of the reasons given in the case law of this Tribunal
 5    and the Tribunal for Rwanda for considering guilty pleas as mitigating
 6    factors.  As such, that contribution had an influence on the Trial
 7    Chamber's decision to attach significant weight to the Appellant's guilty
 8    plea.
 9            With respect to the Appellant's argument that particular weight
10    should be attached to the fact that he was the first accused in a series
11    of cases relating to the Lasva Valley to have pleaded guilty prior to the
12    trial, the Appeals Chamber notes that the Trial Chamber specifically took
13    this factor into account when it assessed the value of the guilty plea and
14    the Appellant's remorse.  Consequently, the Appeals Chamber considers that
15    the Appellant has failed to establish that the Trial Chamber erred in
16    exercising its discretion.
17            Finally, with regard to the appellant's assertion that his guilty
18    plea "resulted in major savings of time, money and other resources," the
19    Appeals Chamber notes that this issue was carefully examined by the Trial
20    Chamber which concluded that the avoidance of a lengthy trial was
21    admittedly a factor to be taken into account in sentencing, but that it
22    should not be given undue weight.  The Appeals Chamber considers that in
23    this case, the appellant has failed to establish that the Trial Chamber
24    committed a discernible error in assessing the value of his guilty plea.
25            The Appeals Chamber therefore concludes that the Appellant has
 1    failed to establish how these factors, which were given substantial weight
 2    in mitigation of the sentence, should have been taken into account
 3    separately within the context of "cooperation" and why they deserved extra
 4    weight.  This part of the second ground of appeal raised by the Appellant
 5    is dismissed.
 6            The Appellant then submits that the Trial Chamber should have
 7    considered his cooperation with the Prosecution as "substantial" and given
 8    it weight as such, while the Trial Chamber considered the
 9    cooperation "moderate."
10            Although cooperation with the Prosecution is the only mitigating
11    factor referred to expressly in the Rules, the Appeals Chamber notes
12    that "substantial cooperation" is not defined, and it is for the Trial
13    Chamber to assess the scope and nature of the cooperation provided by the
14    Appellant.  The assessment of whether or not the cooperation
15    is "substantial" for the most part depends on the specific circumstances
16    of the case.  That assessment cannot be based on one single act of an
17    Accused, but rather on the overall efforts he has made in this regard.
18    The characterisation of an Accused's cooperation with the Prosecution
19    as "substantial" will hinge in particular on the quantity and quality of
20    the information provided.
21            The Appeals Chamber considers that this quality is measured
22    essentially by the "precise content" of that information.  In this
23    connection, the disclosure of new information, presented for the first
24    time to the Tribunal, must be considered as a particularly significant
25    factor.  Accordingly, the content of the information provided is not the
 1    only factor to be taken into account to assess the quality of that
 2    information, since quality also depends, on the seriousness with which the
 3    Accused provided the information.  Finally the Appeals Chamber considers
 4    that the Prosecution's effective use of this information in other cases
 5    before the Tribunal is not sufficient to prove its quality.
 6            In view of these conclusions, the Appeals Chamber first determined
 7    whether the Trial Chamber correctly took into account all the evidence in
 8    assessing the Appellant's cooperation with the Prosecution.  With regard
 9    to the documents provided by the Appellant in 1997, the Appeals Chamber
10    notes that the Trial Chamber took into account that they were used in
11    other cases, and therefore bore in mind that the Appellant had provided
12    them when assessing his cooperation with the Prosecution.
13            As regards the Trial Chamber's assessment of the value of the
14    factual basis upon which the guilty plea rests, the Appeals Chamber, while
15    concurring with the Trial Chamber that the use of that information is not
16    sufficient to prove its quality, notes that the Appellant's references to
17    the proceedings in the request for review in the Blaskic case do suggest
18    that the information is helpful to the Prosecution.  Nevertheless, the
19    Appeals Chamber notes that cooperation must be considered as a whole.
20            The Appeals Chamber notes that particular attention should be paid
21    to the willingness of an Accused to cooperate in the context of this
22    analysis, which motivates his efforts and is exemplified in particular by
23    the seriousness with which he discloses the information to the
24    Prosecution.  In this case the Appeal Chamber notes that the Trial
25    Chamber, while taking into account the fact that the Appellant was willing
 1    to testify in other cases, noted that he refused to meet with the
 2    Prosecution for questioning.  The Appeals Chamber also notes that the plea
 3    agreement presented to the Trial Chamber did not specify, unlike others,
 4    that the Appellant had pledged his cooperation.
 5            In view of these factors, the Appeals Chamber considers that the
 6    Trial Chamber did not err in finding that the Appellant, although not
 7    obstructive, nevertheless made no genuine commitment to cooperate with the
 8    Prosecution.  Consequently the Trial Chamber did not err based on the
 9    evidence before it in characterising the the Appellant's cooperation
10    as "moderate."
11            With regard to additional evidence, the Appeals Chamber notes that
12    the evidence provided by the Appellant was used in the Blaskic and Kordic
13    and Cerkez cases, and as supporting material accompanying the indictment
14    in the Ljubicic case, and during the subsequent transfer of that case from
15    the Prosecution to Bosnia and Herzegovina, in accordance with Article 11
16    bis of the statute.  The Appeals Chamber also notes that this evidence was
17    used during the review proceedings in the Blaskic case, but only as
18    corroborative evidence.  The Appeals Chamber is therefore not convinced
19    that the quality and quantity of the information contained in the
20    additional evidence is of such a character to conclude that the
21    Appellant's cooperation was "substantial."
22            Considering the additional evidence admitted on appeal, taken in
23    conjunction with the evidence presented to the Trial Chamber, the Appeals
24    Chamber concludes that the Appellant has failed to demonstrate that his
25    cooperation was in fact "substantial."  This part of the second ground of
 1    appeal raised by the Appellant is dismissed.
 2            As regards the Appellant's argument that the Trial Chamber failed
 3    to appropriately consider the particular significance of his cooperation
 4    for the inhabitants of Ahmici, the Appeals Chamber observes at the outset
 5    that the Appellant acknowledges that the Trial Chamber took into account
 6    his conduct after the conflict.  The Trial Chamber concluded that the
 7    Appellant's efforts to make amends expressed genuine remorse and agreed
 8    that the fact that he acknowledged his wrongdoing was extremely important
 9    for the entire community and contributed to healing and reconciliation.
10    The Appeals Chamber observes that the Trial Chamber considered that the
11    genuine remorse expressed by the Appellant constituted a factor in
12    mitigation to which it attached significant weight.  The Appellant has
13    failed to demonstrate why the Trial Chamber should have taken these
14    factors into account separately and give them weight as "efforts towards
15    cooperation" even though the Trial Chamber considered them elsewhere as
16    mitigating factors to which it attached significant weight.  The Appeals
17    Chamber considers in particular that the Trial Chamber could perfectly
18    consider the assistance he provided to help locate crime scenes and burial
19    sites, along with his assistance in mine-clearing operations, is evidence
20    of his remorse and of his efforts to make amends for his crimes.  The
21    Trial Chamber was not required to take these factors into account to
22    assess his cooperation.  This part of the second ground of appeal raised
23    by the Appellant is dismissed.
24            Finally, as far as the Appellant's argument whereby the expression
25    of genuine remorse going beyond the simple acknowledgment of his crimes by
 1    pleading guilty is one of the most significant and complete mitigating
 2    factors.  The Appeals Chamber recalls that the Trial Chamber considered
 3    the Appellant's genuine remorse to be a mitigating factor to which it
 4    attached significant weight.  The Appeals Chamber considers that the
 5    Appellant has failed to demonstrate that the Trial Chamber committed a
 6    discernible error in exercise its discretion.  Consequently, this part of
 7    the second ground of appeal raised by the Appellant is dismissed.
 8            For the foregoing reasons, the appellant's second ground of appeal
 9    is dismissed in its entirety.
10            In his third ground of appeal, the appellant submits that after
11    considering that the mitigating circumstances, taken as a whole, should
12    substantially mitigate punishment the Trial Chamber erred in excercising
13    its discretion when it reduced the overall sentence by only five years.
14            The Appeals Chamber notes that after determining the weight to
15    attach to the mitigating circumstances, the Trial Chamber concluded that
16    the fact that Miroslav Bralo pleaded guilty on the date he did, that he
17    expressed remorse, tried to make amends, and voluntarily surrendered to
18    the Tribunal, are factors which, considered as a whole, weigh in favour of
19    a substantial reduction in what would otherwise be an appropriate
20    sentence.
21            The Appellant agrees with this conclusion but asserts that,
22    considering the Tribunal's broader goal to "promote justice, peace and
23    reconciliation in the region," the Trial Chamber in assessing the weight
24    to attach to the mitigating circumstances erred in its excessively
25    restrictive interpretation of the Tribunal's purpose.  The Appeals Chamber
 1    agrees with the Appellant that the work of the Tribunal is "an element of
 2    the process of reconciliation and a factor contributing to the maintenance
 3    of peace and security" in the region, which has been an inherent function
 4    of the Tribunal since its inception.  It considers nonetheless that the
 5    Trial Chamber was perfectly aware of this role and had it specifically in
 6    mind when it assessed the purposes of punishment.  As regards the
 7    Appellant's argument that the Trial Chamber did not give sufficient weight
 8    to that factor, the Appeals Chamber recalls that "although reconciliation
 9    in the region and the restoration and maintenance of peace are important
10    purposes in punishment, they are not the only ones."
11            As the Trial Chamber rightly specified, the purposes of punishment
12    are clearly set out in the Tribunal's case law.  Specifically, the Appeals
13    Chamber recalls that, to determine sentence, the Tribunal attaches special
14    importance to principles of retribution and deterrence.
15            The Appeals Chamber has already ruled that the Trial Chamber
16    closely examined the mitigating and aggravating circumstances in the case
17    and that it gave them appropriate weight.  The Appeals Chamber recalls in
18    particular that the Trial Chamber specifically bore in mind the
19    Appellant's genuine remorse and personal contribution towards
20    reconciliation in the region when it analysed his guilty plea, and that it
21    attached significant weight to these factors.  The Appeals Chamber
22    therefore concludes that the Appellant has failed to demonstrate that the
23    Trial Chamber misapplied the Tribunal's sentencing principles.
24            Regarding the Appellant's argument whereby the Trial Chamber did
25    not reduce his sentence to fully take into account the substantial
 1    reduction which, according to it, was justified, in view of the relevant
 2    mitigating circumstances, the Appeals Chamber recalls that once proven,
 3    mitigating circumstances "do not automatically entitle an appellant to
 4    a 'credit' in the determination of the sentence," they simply require the
 5    Trial Chamber to consider such mitigating circumstances in its final
 6    determination.  In this case the Appeals Chamber notes that the Trial
 7    Chamber did, in fact, take the relevant mitigating circumstances into
 8    account to determine the Appellant's sentence.  The Appeals Chamber notes
 9    that it is up to the Appellant to show that the Trial Chamber improperly
10    exercised its discretion in imposing his sentence.  In this case the
11    Appeals Chamber considers that the Appellant has failed to demonstrate
12    that the Trial Chamber committed a discernible error of law or of fact in
13    excercising its broad discretionary powers to determine an appropriate
14    sentence.
15            Moreover, the Appeals Chamber is of the opinion that the Appellant
16    has failed to demonstrate that the Trial Chamber exhibited a lack of
17    transparency when reducing the sentence.
18            According to the Appeals Chamber, the Trial Chamber clearly
19    weighed the mitigating and aggravating circumstances and the seriousness
20    of the crimes committed by the Appellant.
21            Finally, as regards the calculus of the weight to be attached to
22    circumstances in mitigation of sentence, the Appeals Chamber considers
23    that any changes to it must be considered in light of the overall
24    circumstances of the case, and that it is not a matter of simple
25    subtraction.  As mentioned above, the Trial Chamber appropriately
 1    considered all of the circumstances of the case before determining the
 2    sentence.  The Appeals Chamber may only impose a new sentence when "that
 3    imposed by the Trial Chamber is incompatible with the Tribunal's
 4    principles governing the determination of sentence."
 5            In this case, and considering in particular the seriousness of the
 6    crimes committed and the aggravating circumstances, not disputed by the
 7    Appellant, the Appeals Chamber considers that the Appellant has failed to
 8    demonstrate that the sentence imposed on him was so unreasonable as to
 9    constitute an error in the exercise of the discretion of the Trial
10    Chamber.  He has also failed to establish in what way the sentence imposed
11    is "incompatible with the Tribunal's principles governing the
12    determination of sentence."
13            For the foregoing reasons, the third ground of appeal raised by
14    the Appellant is dismissed in its entirety.
15            Having reached the end of its analysis and having considered all
16    of the particular circumstances of this case, the Appeals Chamber has
17    arrived at the conclusion that a reduction in the sentence would not be
18    justified.  The Appeals Chamber, ruling unanimously, affirms the sentence
19    of 20 years imposed by the Trial Chamber.
20            I shall now read out the disposition of the judgement handed down
21    by the Appeals Chamber.  Mr. Bralo, could you kindly stand up, please.
22            Here is the disposition of the judgement:  For the foregoing
23    reasons, the Appeals Chamber, ruling unanimously, pursuant to Article 25
24    of the statute and Rules 117 and 118 of the Rules of Procedure and
25    Evidence noting the respective written submissions of the parties and the
 1    oral arguments they presented at the hearing of 9 February 2007, sitting
 2    in open session, dismisses the grounds of appeal raised by the Appellant,
 3    affirms the sentence of 20 years' imprisonment imposed by the Trial
 4    Chamber, orders, in accordance with Rule 103 and Rule 107 of the Rules
 5    that the Appellant is to remain in the custody of the International
 6    Tribunal pending the finalisation of arrangements for his transfer to the
 7    state where his sentence will be served.
 8            Mr. Bralo, you may sit down.
 9            Mr. Registrar, kindly hand out copies of the judgement to the
10    parties.
11            Thank you.  I apologise if I read this out a little too fast.  The
12    Court stands adjourned.
