14            Introduction.  By a decision dated 16 May 1995, pursuant to Rule
15    61(A) of the Rules of Procedure and Evidence, the Rules, Judge Odio-Benito
16    ordered that the Prosecutor submit to this Trial Chamber the indictment
17    against Dragan Nikolic alleged to have been, during 1992, the Commander of
18    Susica camp located in the region of Vlasenica in Bosnia-Herzegovina.
19            Pursuant to Rules 46 and 55, the same Judge had confirmed the
20    indictment on 4 November 1994 and issued a warrant of arrest which, to
21    this day, has not been executed.  The Chamber had the material which was
22    initially submitted to the confirming Judge, but it has also heard in the
23    public hearing, during the week of 9 to 13 October 1995, a number of
24    witnesses and alleged victims.  The Chamber is now called on to determine
25    the putative responsibility of Dragan Nikolic, that is, to determine if
 1    there are reasonable grounds for believing that he has committed all or
 2    any of the crimes charged in the indictment, and if so to issue an
 3    international warrant for his arrest.  The execution of the arrest warrant
 4    relies upon States' obligation to cooperate and to render judicial
 5    assistance provided for by Article 29 of the Statute.
 6            In effect, all States in the International Community will be
 7    bound, if the warrant is issued, to cooperate in searching for and
 8    arresting the accused who would, in consequence, become an international
 9    fugitive.  Moreover, should the Chamber consider that the failure to
10    effect service of the indictment and to execute the original warrant of
11    arrest is attributable to a State or, under the terms of the Rules, to a
12    self-proclaimed entity, it may so certify and it may inform the Security
13    Council through the offices of the President of the Tribunal.
14            Before reviewing the crimes allegedly committed by the accused,
15    both the facts and the legal description of those facts, it is appropriate
16    to assess the scope of Rule 61 in the context of its first application.
17    Recourse to Rule 61 means that the Tribunal, which does not have any
18    direct enforcement powers, is not rendered ineffective in the face of the
19    nonappearance of the accused and can proceed with the case despite this
20    deficiency.  The review by a panel of judges sitting in a public hearing
21    of an indictment initially confirmed by a single judge reinforces both the
22    rights of the accused and enhances the solemnity and gravity of the
23    Judge's decision.
24            The Rule 61 procedure, which is initiated by the Prosecutor,
25    cannot be considered a trial in absentia.  It does not culminate in a
 1    verdict, nor does it deprive the accused of the right to contest in person
 2    the charges brought against him before the Tribunal.  The rights of
 3    alleged victims should not, however, be discounted altogether.  Rule 61
 4    will provide them with the opportunity to have their voices heard in a
 5    public hearing and to become part of history.
 6            We are now going to review the crimes which Dragan Nikolic
 7    allegedly committed.
 8            The crimes described hereunder were allegedly perpetrated during
 9    1992 in Eastern Bosnia, in the municipality of Vlasenica and, for the most
10    part, within the Susica camp, a former military installation converted by
11    the Bosnian Serbs into a detention camp, and of which Dragan Nikolic is
12    alleged to have been the commander.  The detainees were held in one of the
13    two main buildings within the camp called the hangar.
14            Now, first the criminal acts.  The Chamber now examines the
15    evidence which has been tendered in support of the indictment in order to
16    determine whether that evidence provides reasonable grounds for believing
17    that the accused has committed any or all of the crimes charged in the
18    indictment.
19            The indictment against Dragan Nikolic consists of 24 counts.  Each
20    count relates to one set of facts for which the Prosecutor has proposed
21    alternative legal descriptions.  For the reasons discussed below, each
22    count, with two exceptions, will here be presented under the most suitable
23    description, that is, crimes against humanity.
24            Murders first.
25                A) The murders of Durmo Handzic and Asim Zildzic.
 1                A number of witness statements, written and oral, of persons
 2    detained in Susica camp reported the murder of Durmo Handzic and Asim
 3    Zildzic.  Those testimonies indicate that one evening in June 1992, Dragan
 4    Nikolic and camp guards went towards the hangar and called out Durmo
 5    Handzic and Asim Zildzic.  The latter, shortly after having stepped from
 6    the hangar, was subjected to major physical assaults inflicted for more
 7    than 45 minutes by Dragan Nikolic and the guards.  They were punched and
 8    kicked and struck with truncheons and rifle butts.  Some prisoners
 9    witnessed some of the assaults as suffered, and others heard the victims
10    cry out, scream, moan, and beg for an end to their torture.  Durmo Handzic
11    and Asim Zildzic were then brought back into the hangar.
12            According to the testimony of prisoners who were there, their
13    bodies were covered with bruises and their clothes were soiled and torn.
14    Asim Zildzic had been beaten so violently that his face was unrecognisable
15    and one eye had come out of its socket.  He died shortly after having been
16    brought back.  Dragan Nikolic ordered his body to be taken away and two
17    prisoners, Hasim and Alija Ferhatovic to bury him.  Dragan Nikolic came
18    into the hangar the next morning and approached Durmo Handzic.  The latter
19    was suffering intensely from the beating the night before, and Nikolic
20    prohibited prisoners from helping him.  Durmo Handzic thereupon begged
21    Nikolic to kill him, to put an end to his suffering.  Nikolic answered
22    that a bullet was worth more than his life, that it would be wasted on him
23    and that he should suffer before dying.  Durmo Handzic died shortly
24    afterwards.  He was buried the same day by Hasim and Alija Ferhatovic.
25            B) Murder of Mevludin Hatunic.
 1            Mevludin Hatunic, his wife, and daughter were turned in the Susica
 2    camp in early July 1992.  Mevludin Hatunic reportedly offered his home to
 3    a Serb guard in return for his family's release.  He was authorised to
 4    accompany the guard to his house for that purpose and then returned to the
 5    camp.  This was between 3 and 7 July 1992.  That same evening, Dragan
 6    Nikolic announced to the camp prisoners that after having handed over his
 7    home, Mevludin Hatunic had made a comment to the effect that he would be
 8    waiting for a chance to get even.  Nikolic then hit Mevludin Hatunic.  The
 9    following morning, Dragan Nikolic again beat him up until he lost
10    consciousness.  Dragan Nikolic returned that evening, saw that Mevludin
11    Hatunic had regained consciousness and beat him savagely for the third
12    time.  Mevludin Hatunic died from his injuries.  His body was put in a
13    plastic bag by one of the Ferhatovic brothers and removed from the
14    hangar.
15            C) Murders of Rasid Ferhatbegovic, Dzevad Saric, Muharem
16    Kolarevic, and Ibrahim Zekic.
17            According to a number of witnesses, probably in the night from 23
18    to 24 June 1992, Rasid Ferhatbegovic, Dzevad Saric, Muharem Kolarevic, and
19    Ibrahim Zekic were taken from the detainees' hangar by Goran Tesic and
20    other guards.  Some detainees saw Dragan Nikolic follow the prisoners out
21    and heard his voice.  Dzevad Saric and Muharem Kolarevic were the first to
22    be taken out.  The hangar doors were closed again immediately.  For about
23    30 minutes, the detainees heard screams of pain.  Thereafter they heard
24    gunshots, following which Goran Tesic asked Hasim Ferhatovic to come out.
25    The latter saw the bodies of Muharem Kolarevic and Dzevad Saric which
 1    seemed to have bullet wounds in the chest.  Dragan Nikolic thereupon
 2    ordered the Ferhatovic brothers to put the bodies on a stretcher and take
 3    them behind the warehouse where they could not be seen from the camp
 4    entrance.  They then came to where Nikolic, Tesic, a guard named Djuro and
 5    Dragan Nikolic's brother were sitting with others.  Goran Tesic told them,
 6    "Hold on a minute.  You're going to get another."  Goran Tesic ordered
 7    Alija Ferhatovic to go and get Ibrahim Zekic.  Goran Tesic had him sit
 8    down on a metal chair and questioned him.  He then asked another guard to
 9    give him his weapon, and once he had it, he shot Ibrahim Zekic twice, who
10    fell from the chair.  Goran Tesic thereupon ordered the Ferhatovic
11    brothers to take Ibrahim Zekic away.  When they returned to where they had
12    left the bodies of Muharem Kolarevic and Dzevad Saric, Muharem Kolarevic's
13    body was no longer there.  They brought this to Goran Tesic's attention.
14    The prisoners who were in the hangar heard the guards yell, "Call the
15    police.  They're escaping."  The police arrived some 15 minutes later and
16    entered the hangar accompanied, according to some detainees, by Dragan
17    Nikolic and Goran Tesic.  Rasid Ferhatbegovic looked up.  One of the
18    police officers pointed at him and asked him if he was the one who had
19    escaped.  Tesic replied yes.  Rasid Ferhatbegovic was taken outside and
20    the prisoners then heard a gunshot.
21            Towards 5.00 a.m., Dragan Nikolic came into the hangar and called
22    Hasim and Alija Ferhatovic.  They went over to the toilets where they
23    found the body of Muharem Kolarevic.  He was slumped over a barbed wire
24    fence.  Traces of blood led from where his body had been left the night
25    before.  Even though he seemed to be dead, Goran Tesic fired another
 1    bullet into the body.  Hasim and Alija Ferhatovic carried the body to
 2    where they had left the other bodies the previous evening.  There they
 3    found Rasid Ferhatbegovic with a bullet in the middle of the forehead.
 4    Hasim and Alija Ferhatovic and Redjo Cakisic buried the bodies of those
 5    prisoners on 24 June 1992.
 6            The murder of Ismet Dedic.
 7            Around 6 July 1992, Dragan Nikolic had Ismet Dedic come out of the
 8    detainees' hangar.  He closed the door and the detainees thereafter heard
 9    Ismet Dedic scream.  Shortly after, Dragan Nikolic opened the hangar door
10    and ordered two detainees to drag Ismet Dedic inside.  The latter was
11    seriously wounded and died a little while later.  His body was put in a
12    plastic bag and carried away by other detainees.
13            That concludes what is characterised as murders.
14            Inhumane acts.  First, inhumane acts committed against Galib
15    Music.  Dragan Nikolic committed serious physical assaults against Galib
16    Music in Susica camp over a seven-day period.  Dragan Nikolic allegedly
17    kicked him and beat him with a metal bar.  Galib Music was beaten
18    unconscious each time.  The beatings appeared to become more severe every
19    day.  Shortly before the last beating, Galib Music regained consciousness
20    and asked for water.  He was not given any water, food, or
21    medical attention during the entire time he was tortured.  Galib Music
22    died as a result.
23            B) Inhuman acts against Sead Ambeskovic [phoen].
24            Dragan Nikolic and other guards committed physical assaults
25    against Sead Ambeskovic in Susica camp using axe handles, iron bars,
 1    wooden bats, and rifle butts.  According to Sead Ambeskovic's testimony,
 2    as a result of the beatings, "The back of my head was cut, four teeth on
 3    the left side of my mouth were knocked out, and three ribs were broken."
 4            C) Inhumane acts committed against Redjo Cakisic.
 5            When he arrived in Susica camp, Redjo Cakisic was told by Nikolic
 6    and the camp guards, "Look how you ended up by voting for Alija
 7    Izetbegovic and the SDA party."  According to his testimony at the
 8    hearing, Redjo Cakisic was called out by Nikolic one night.  Nikolic told
 9    the two men waiting outside, "Here, I brought you something for dinner."
10    The two men beat him in the back with rifle butts and kicked him in the
11    stomach as Nikolic was leaving.
12            D) Inhuman acts committed against Hasna Cakisic.
13            On three occasions, the guards called out Hasna Cakisic for
14    interrogation.  According to the record, the 68-year-old victim was
15    slapped and beaten on her hands with a truncheon to force her to reveal
16    the whereabouts of her son.  Dragan Nikolic participated in this
17    ill-treatment.
18            Generally speaking, in Susica camp, civilians were detained under
19    inhumane conditions, especially as regards hygiene, health, safety, and
20    nutrition.  Included in the approximately 500 civilians were women,
21    children, and elderly people.  The detainees were crammed into a hangar
22    with practically no ventilation and had to sleep on the concrete floor.
23    They were given only one daily ration of food, and even that was usually
24    spoiled.  They were infrequently allowed to use the toilet outside and had
25    to use a single bucket in the hangar for their bodily functions.  No
 1    medical care was provided.  Certain prisoners were specifically appointed
 2    to control the hangar.  The detainees lived in constant fear for their
 3    lives, especially at night.  I quote, "At night everybody waited for the
 4    day to come because that meant going out to work, not seeing what was
 5    happening.  The torture and suffering were less severe, whereas the nights
 6    were nights of horror.  Old people were not spared this inhuman
 7    treatment."  As the witness described, "A 75- to 80-year-old woman, she
 8    wanted to go to the toilet but was not allowed to.  Dragan Nikolic ordered
 9    her to be tied up, so they tied her hands and feet and she had to urinate
10    the room we were in."
11            In addition to the four victims mentioned above, the indictment
12    alleges that Mevludin Hatunic, Ismet Dedic, and Fikret Cice Arnaut were
13    victims of inhumane acts, Counts 3(6), 8(6), 11(3) [as interpreted].
14            As Nikolic is also charged with the murder of Hatunic and Dedic,
15    the relevant acts are described under murders.  As Nikolic is also charged
16    with torturing Fikret Cice Arnaut, the relevant acts are described under
17    torture.
18            We will now go on to the paragraph on torture.
19            Torture of Fikret Cice Arnaut.
20            Fikret Cice Arnaut was allegedly assaulted by Nikolic on numerous
21    occasions.  Witnesses related how, on one occasion, Nikolic came into the
22    hangar and while shouting to the women, "You are not here because of me
23    but because of him.  He wanted to rape my mother and now we will rape
24    you."  He forced Arnaut, Fikret Cice Arnaut, to put his hands behind his
25    back and kneel on the floor, spreading his knees open.  Dragan Nikolic
 1    kicked Fikret Cice Arnaut in the stomach and lower back.  He forced him to
 2    tilt his head back and he put a bayonet into his mouth.  Witnesses saw
 3    blood on Dragan Nikolic's bayonet.  Later, Fikret Cice Arnaut was spitting
 4    and vomiting blood.  He was heard begging, "Nikolic, don't beat me to
 5    death.  Kill me with a bullet," to which Dragan Nikolic answered, "No.  A
 6    bullet costs five German marks and you're not worth half a cigarette."
 7    Fikret Cice Arnaut was also taken outside and beaten by Dragan Nikolic who
 8    was wearing brass knuckles.  According to one eyewitness, at one point,
 9    Fikret Cice Arnaut could no longer stand.  He was covered with blood and
10    his face was swollen.  It appeared as though many of Arnaut's bones had
11    been broken.
12            Torture of Mubin Music.
13            Mubin Music was mistreated while he was in Susica camp.  While he
14    was outside the hangar, Nikolic put a bayonet into his mouth, insulting
15    and questioning him all the while.
16            Torture of Suad Mahmutovic.
17            Suad Mahmutovic was allegedly beaten repeated by Dragan Nikolic.
18    Several witnesses, including the victim, asserted that during one beating,
19    seven of his ribs were broken.  Another time, Dragan Nikolic kicked him in
20    the face with his boots and hitting him with a baton, gashing open his
21    face.  According to the witness, "One time in the hangar, Nikolic put a
22    cocked pistol in my mouth and told me to admit my neighbour had a weapon.
23    I feared for the safety of others and could not lie.  He pulled the
24    trigger.  It was then that I realised the gun was not loaded."
25            The Chamber notes that it appears that several other victims of
 1    inhumane acts or torture committed by Dragan Nikolic in Susica camp have
 2    not been identified as such in the indictment.
 3            Now we will proceed with the imprisonment of civilians.
 4            According to the written testimony as well as the oral testimony
 5    presented to the Chamber, a great number of people were detained at Susica
 6    camp during the period between 1st of June, 1992 and the 30th of
 7    September, 1992.  The regular population of the camp is said to have been
 8    about 500.  The majority of the detainees were men, but women and children
 9    are said to have also been part of that regular population.  Over the
10    entire period in question, 8.000 people in all are said to have been
11    detained in Susica camp.  All the witnesses who testified to having been
12    detained at Susica camp emphasised that at the time of their arrest they
13    had not been participating in a resistance movement against the
14    authorities who had seized power in Vlasenica and who were responsible for
15    the camp.  It does not seem that such a movement could have taken shape in
16    the Vlasenica region where the detainees were locals during the period in
17    which the camp was operational.  The arrests seem to have taken place only
18    after the population had been totally disarmed.  Thus, it would appear
19    that the establishment of Susica camp was aimed at detaining a defenceless
20    civilian population which was not organised into a resistance movement.
21            Now we will proceed with the persecution on religious grounds.
22            In the light of the relevant parts of the record, the imprisonment
23    of civilians under particularly inhumane conditions might constitute an
24    act of persecution since it appears to be motivated solely by a
25    discriminatory intent based principally, if not exclusively, on the
 1    religious characterisation of the targeted population.  The oral testimony
 2    at the hearing indicates that the camp population seemed to have been
 3    exclusively Muslim.
 4            Now we will talk about the appropriation of property and plunder.
 5            Many witnesses have provided evidence of a system of unlawful
 6    appropriation or plunder of property at Susica camp.  The evidence is that
 7    on arrival at the camp, each detainee was forced to hand over items of
 8    private property, especially valuable items such as gold or jewelry.
 9    Dragan Nikolic is alleged to have personally supervised the confiscation
10    of property while he was in a small building which he used for
11    interrogations.  There is also evidence that before women were deported
12    from Susica camp, they had to sign a document stating that they were
13    leaving the area voluntarily and that they were giving up their
14    possessions.
15            The Chamber considers that there are reasonable grounds for
16    believing that the appropriations were not justified by military necessity
17    and were carried out unlawfully and wantonly.  The Chamber further
18    considers that the acts described above could also be considered as
19    characterising persecution on religious grounds and so be covered by
20    Article 5 of the Statute.
21            Unlawful transfer of civilians.  Under the supervision and on the
22    orders of the accused Dragan Nikolic, a large number of detainees were
23    said to have been unlawfully transferred from Susica camp to Batkovic camp
24    during the summer of 1992.  Dragan Nikolic is said to have organised the
25    transfers, calling out detainees from a list of names and telling them
 1    that they were to be exchanged for Serbian prisoners.  In actual fact, the
 2    detainees were transferred to Batkovic camp.  They were forced to travel
 3    by bus, with their heads down, their hands behind their heads.  They were
 4    beaten and forced to sing patriotic Serbian songs.  At Batkovic camp,
 5    conditions were similar to those at Susica camp, if not worse.
 6            As submitted by the Prosecutor, the Chamber considers that Dragan
 7    Nikolic may have committed grave breaches of the Geneva Conventions of
 8    1949, in particular, of the Fourth Convention, which comes under -- which
 9    come under the Tribunal's jurisdiction, pursuant to Article 2 of the
10    Statute.  The Chamber, however, also considers that the same set of facts
11    could be characterised as deportation and accordingly come under Article 5
12    of the Statute.
13            Crimes against humanity.  We're now going to look into what type
14    of responsibility arose out of Nikolic's position at the Susica camp.
15            The relevant part of the record provides reasonable grounds for
16    believing that Dragan Nikolic held the position of camp commander at
17    Susica camp.  The witnesses base their conclusions upon an analysis of the
18    distribution of tasks within the camp.  The guards were subjugated to
19    Nikolic's order.  Nothing apparently could be carried out without his
20    assent.  The witnesses also referred to declarations by Nikolic himself
21    proclaiming his sovereign power within the camp.  According to
22    corroborated testimony, he would publicly state, "I am here.  I am the
23    commander, God, stick, and the law."
24            The indictment and supporting material showed that Nikolic's
25    responsibility for the crimes against identified persons could arise not
 1    only as a result of his direct involvement in committing such crimes,
 2    Article 7(1) of the Statute, the one who directly took responsibility, but
 3    also by his position of authority when the evidence does not suggest such
 4    direct involvement, which is the case for Counts 4, 5, 6, 7, and 18.
 5            This latter principle of individual responsibility for admission,
 6    that is, that he did not take all steps that he should have, long
 7    recognised under international criminal law, is reaffirmed by Article 7(3)
 8    of the Statute of the Tribunal which provides the fact that any of the
 9    acts referred to in Articles 2 to 5 of the present Statute was committed
10    by a subordinate does not relieve a superior of criminal responsibility if
11    he knew or had reason to know that the subordinate was about to commit
12    such acts or had done so and the superior failed to take the necessary and
13    reasonable measures to prevent such acts or to punish the perpetrators
14    thereof.
15            However, with respect to the counts of the indictment concerning
16    crimes against a group of persons, Counts 20 to 24, it should be noted
17    that the reference to Article 7(3) is less pertinent.  Dragan Nikolic's
18    position of authority in the Susica camp makes him responsible not through
19    his subordinates, but on his account where imprisonment, appropriation,
20    deportation, persecution, and inhumane acts related to the very conditions
21    of detention are concerned.
22            The Chamber finished the review of all of the crimes of which
23    Dragan Nikolic is suspected and now will move into the third part, the
24    appraisal of the general context in which the alleged crimes are alleged
25    to have been committed and will now say what it thinks the
 1    characterisation should be in priority.
 2            The Chamber has noted the alternative presentation in the
 3    indictment which the Prosecutor presented of the legal characterisation of
 4    the crimes.  On the basis of relevant parts of the record, the Tribunal's
 5    jurisdiction may conceivably be founded on Articles 2 or 3 of the
 6    Statute.  However, without prejudicing the appraisal of the Judges hearing
 7    the case, who one day, if Nikolic is arrested, will have to take the
 8    trial, feel that there are reasons to believe that the crimes are more
 9    suitably characterised as crimes against humanity, and the Chamber will
10    now give its reasons for that.
11            First it will look into the proof which will allow us to set the
12    priority of those crimes which fall into the context of crimes against
13    humanity, and we'll look at the context in which those crimes must be
14    committed in order to be crimes against humanity.  And then it will point
15    out, through this definition, the various elements of the record that are
16    the most relevant and that will be cited.
17            First of all, the context in which crimes must be committed to be
18    characterised as crimes against humanity.  If I can remind you that first,
19    Article 5 of the Statute reads:  "The International Tribunal shall have
20    the power to prosecute persons for the following crimes when committed in
21    armed conflict, whether international or internal, and directed against
22    any civilian population:  Murder, extermination, enslavement, deportation,
23    imprisonment, torture, rape, persecutions on political, racial and
24    religious grounds, other inhumane acts."
25            The definition specifies the type of crimes which, if committed
 1    under certain circumstances, constitute crimes against humanity and
 2    moreover describes those circumstances.  The first circumstance referred
 3    to in the text is that of armed conflict.  It is, in fact, common to
 4    Articles 2, 3, and 5 of the Statute, dealt with alternatively or
 5    cumulatively in the indictment, which is why it will be addressed below.
 6            The Appeals Chamber thus confirmed the findings of the Trial
 7    Chamber and considered that by requiring demonstration of the existence of
 8    an armed conflict, the Statute had narrowed the customary concept of
 9    crimes against humanity, a decision of 2 October 1995, case number
10    IT-94-1-A-72, paragraph 141.  That concept, since the Nuremberg judgement,
11    had taken on a certain autonomy as there was no longer any need to
12    determine a link with a crime against peace or a war crime.
13            The second circumstance, whereby crimes must be directed against
14    any civilian population, is specific to crimes against humanity.  Set
15    forth in broad terms in the Statute, it apparently covers three distinct
16    components according to the three prevailing opinions.  First, the crimes
17    must be directed at a civilian population specifically identified as a
18    group by the perpetrators of those acts.  Secondly, the crimes must be
19    part of an organised and systematic approach; although they need not be
20    related to a policy established at the level of the state in the
21    conventional sense of the term, they cannot be the work of isolated
22    individuals alone.  Lastly, the crimes considered as a whole must be
23    perpetrated on a broad scale and be very serious.
24            Then having moved to this investigation of the first objective of
25    the test, we'll now raise the question as to know whether Dragan Nikolic
 1    participated during 1992 in a systematic, serious, and widespread policy
 2    directed against a civilian population specifically identified as a
 3    group.
 4            The evidence produced by the Prosecutor tends to show that in the
 5    spring of 1992, in the Vlasenica region, there was an authoritarian
 6    takeover by the Serbs which seems to have been largely facilitated by the
 7    intervention of elements of the JNA, the Yugoslav People's Army, and in
 8    particular the Novi Sad Corps, which at that time was under the command of
 9    the government of Belgrade.
10            The eyewitnesses generally confirmed the existence of a new
11    authoritarian power structure at Vlasenica and were unanimous in stating
12    that discriminatory measures were directed against them as early as
13    March/April 1992.  Some banking institutions imposed restrictions on the
14    accounts of individuals of Muslim faith.  Laissez-passer were issued in
15    order to control movement both within and outside the city.  A witness
16    said at the hearing, "I never received a laissez-passer to leave the
17    municipality of Vlasenica, but I was issued a laissez-passer to travel
18    between my home and my land and my farm."
19            Lastly, the population was required to hand in any and all
20    weapons.
21            The conclusion from all of the case material is that the civilian
22    population subjected to the above-mentioned discrimination was identified
23    by the perpetrators of the discriminatory measures principally by its
24    religious characteristics.  The testimony is consistent on this point.
25    The Muslim population was specifically, if not exclusively, targeted.  The
 1    initial discriminatory measures seem to have been followed by still more
 2    radical ones such as summary arrests, detentions, and torture at the
 3    police station, massive transfers of civilians to Susica and then to the
 4    Batkovic camp.
 5            On the basis of all the testimony, in September 1992, there
 6    remained only a few traces of the population of Muslim faith of the
 7    opstina of Vlasenica which, according to the 1991 census, the last census
 8    prior to the said events, amounted to 55.3 per cent of the total
 9    population.
10            The implementation of that discriminatory policy, commonly
11    referred to as ethnic cleansing, over the region of Vlasenica alone seems
12    to have been some extensive that the Tribunal might be deemed competent
13    under Article 5.  However, the Chamber notes that the serious
14    discriminatory acts do not seem to have been limited to the Vlasenica
15    region.  Camps like the one in Susica were set up in a large part of the
16    territory under Serb control in Bosnia.  This policy of ethnic cleansing
17    covered other parts of Bosnia from the spring of 1992 on.
18            According to the expert witness, the conduct of the spring 1992
19    attacks, including the manner in which the JNA and paramilitary groups
20    deployed and treated the non-Serb population was similar throughout
21    Bosnia.  The deposition of this witness shows the extensiveness of the
22    criminal acts which were committed, as well as the fact that they were
23    organised at the highest level.  According to the witness, the speed and
24    high level of coordination that these attacks required made clear that
25    they were centrally coordinated and planned.  And the witness finished by
 1    saying, "In conjunction with Serbian political leaders and Serbian
 2    irregular units, the JNA conceived, planned, prepared, and implemented an
 3    armed campaign in Bosnia that involved a systematic use of terror to
 4    establish the borders of a new Yugoslavia."
 5            According to all the witnesses, Dragan Nikolic was the commander
 6    of the Susica camp at Vlasenica from the end of May 1992 to late September
 7    1992.  On that basis and in light of all the above, the Chamber considers
 8    that there are reasonable grounds for believing that he participated in
 9    such a policy and committed crimes against humanity pursuant to Article 5
10    of the Statute.
11            Nonetheless, the Chamber has continued to review, in order to give
12    the most complete answer to the request of the Prosecutor, and looking
13    into the conditions for the applications of Article 2 and 3 of the
14    Statute, that is, grave breaches of the Geneva Conventions and violation
15    of the laws or customs of war respectively.  Let us begin with the issue
16    of the existence of an armed conflict and then the specific conditions for
17    application.
18            In certain cases, a condition for the Tribunal's
19    competence ratio materi [phoen] is a state of armed conflict.
20    Accordingly, this Chamber shall examine whether such a state of affairs
21    prevailed at Vlasenica at the time that the crimes were committed.
22            The Appeals Chamber of the Tribunal in the Tadic case said
23    that, "An armed conflict exists whenever there is a resort to armed force
24    between States or protected armed violence between governmental
25    authorities and organised groups or between such groups within a State."
 1            According to the deposition of Mr. James Gall [phoen], expert
 2    witness, the armed conflicts in the former Yugoslavia began in the summer
 3    of 1991 and continued out peaceful settlement to the present day.  In this
 4    case, witnesses have provided testimony regarding the armed takeover of
 5    the town of Vlasenica both by the Bosnian Serbs and the JNA.  Thus, the
 6    Chamber accordingly considers that the crimes of which Dragan Nikolic is
 7    accused were committed in armed conflict.
 8            Now we will look into the specific requirements for the
 9    application of Article 2 of the Statute having to do with serious
10    violations of the provisions of the Geneva Convention.  For it to be
11    applied, the victims must be persons who are persons protected under the
12    provisions of the relevant Geneva Convention.
13            The Muslim population of Vlasenica was systematically disarmed,
14    and it does not appear that there was any resistance movement in the
15    region.  The Chamber considers that all the detainees at Susica camp were
16    civilians and therefore protected persons within the meaning of Article 4
17    of the Fourth Geneva Convention of 1949.
18            The Appeals Chamber further stipulated in the said case of Tadic
19    that the Tribunal has jurisdiction under Article 2 of the Statute only in
20    the context of an international armed conflict.  The relevant parts of the
21    record tend to show that troops of the JNA from Novi Sad, under the
22    command of the government in Belgrade, participated in the occupation of
23    Vlasenica after Bosnia-Herzegovina had been recognised as an independent
24    state.
25            The evidence of the expert witness Mr. Gall suggests moreover that
 1    the armed conflict in the territory of the former Yugoslavia may be viewed
 2    in its entirety as one major armed conflict that reportedly began in the
 3    autumn of 1991, with its aim to establish a new state, and would have
 4    involved several states.
 5            The Chamber considers, on the basis of the foregoing, that the
 6    armed conflict was international in character and Article 2 may,
 7    therefore, be applicable.
 8            Now the Chamber will review the specific requirements for the
 9    application of Article 3 of the Statute.
10            In its decision on the jurisdiction of the Tribunal in the Tadic
11    case, the Appeals Chamber affirmed that the Tribunal is competent under
12    Article 3 of the Statute to prosecute as violations of the laws or customs
13    of war, and I quote, "All violations of international humanitarian law not
14    falling under Article 2 or covered by Articles 4 or 5, more specifically,
15    crimes against humanity and genocide, more specifically:  1, violations of
16    The Hague Law on International Conflict; 2, infringements of provisions of
17    the Geneva Conventions other than those classified as grave breaches by
18    those conventions; 3, violations of Common Article 3 of the Geneva
19    Conventions and other customary rules on internal armed conflict; 4,
20    violations of the agreements binding upon the parties to the conflict
21    considered qua treaty law."
22            The indictment against Nikolic contains 20 charges of violating
23    the laws or customs of war under Article 3 of the Statute.  All but one of
24    those charges concerns violations of Article 3 common to the four Geneva
25    Conventions.  In light of the Appeals Chamber with respect to that
 1    Article, and in light of our finding that the detainees at Susica camp
 2    were civilians and therefore persons taking no active part in the
 3    hostilities, the Chamber considers that Article 3 of the Statute may apply
 4    in this case.  The only charge not coming under Common Article 3 of the
 5    Geneva Conventions concerns the prohibition on plunder of private
 6    property, but that is specifically mentioned in Article 3(e) of the
 7    Statute.
 8            After we have described all the acts that were allegedly
 9    committed, after we have legally analysed, now we would ask the Prosecutor
10    to complete the indictment.  Based on this review of the indictment, and
11    in the light of all materials submitted by the Prosecutor, the Chamber
12    would like to draw the Prosecutor's special attention to two points which
13    it deems to be particularly important.
14            Having regard to the Rules of Procedure and Evidence, it is the
15    prerogative of the Prosecutor, not the Chamber, to amend the indictment
16    according to Rule 50 of the Rules of Procedure and Evidence.  Under these
17    circumstances, the Chamber can only express a conclusion and invite the
18    Prosecutor to amend the indictment accordingly should he share such
19    conclusion.  This concerns the rape and sexual assault and, on the other
20    hand, ethnic cleansing and genocide.
21            Let's first have a look into the rape and sexual assault.  From
22    multiple testimonies and victim and witness statements submitted by the
23    Prosecutor to this Trial Chamber, it appears that women and girls were
24    subjected to rape and other forms of sexual assault during their detention
25    at Susica camp.  Dragan Nikolic was allegedly directly involved in some of
 1    these rapes or sexual assaults.  These allegations do not seem to concern
 2    isolated instances.
 3            The Trial Chamber feels that the Prosecutor may be well advised to
 4    review these statements carefully with a view to ascertaining whether to
 5    charge Dragan Nikolic with rape and other forms of sexual assault as a
 6    crime against humanity or as grave breaches or war crimes.  Without
 7    prejudice to the appraisal of the Trial Judges, and in view of the special
 8    provisions on this subject contained in the Rules of Procedure and
 9    Evidence, the Chamber would concur with the Prosecutor's proposition that
10    rape and other forms of sexual assault inflicted on women in circumstances
11    such as those described by the witnesses are acts of torture.
12            After rape and sexual assault, the Chamber has deemed it extremely
13    important to look into ethnic cleansing and genocide.  It emerged on the
14    basis of the evidence that the policy of discrimination implemented at
15    Vlasenica, of which Dragan Nikolic's acts formed a part, was specifically
16    aimed at cleansing the region of its Muslim population.  In this instance,
17    this policy of ethnic cleansing took the form of discriminatory acts of
18    extreme seriousness which tend to show its genocidal character; for
19    instance, the Chamber notes the statements by some witnesses which point,
20    among other crimes, to mass murders being committed in the region.  More
21    specifically, the specific intent inherent to the crime of genocide may be
22    deduced from the very gravity of those discriminatory acts.
23            That intent, according to some of the eyewitnesses at the hearing,
24    was expressly stated by the accused.  Dragan Nikolic reportedly said, I
25    quote, "You Muslims never existed," or "You will never exist.  I will
 1    eradicate you.  I will slit your throats.  I will butcher you all."
 2            The Chamber considers that the Tribunal might possibly have
 3    jurisdiction in this case under Article 4 of the Statute.  It would
 4    therefore invite the Prosecutor to pursue his investigations, if feasible
 5    and advisable, with a view to indicting Dragan Nikolic for complicity in
 6    genocide or acts of genocide.
 7            In order to respect the disposition of Rule 61, the Chamber has
 8    now to see whether everything was done in order to serve the indictment,
 9    and with regard to this, our decision is as follows:
10            The Chamber notes the efforts by the Prosecutor to effect service
11    of the original indictment and the subsequent warrants of arrest.
12            On the 4th of November, 1994, the day the indictment against
13    Dragan Nikolic was confirmed, two warrants for his arrest were issued; one
14    addressed to the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina, the other addressed
15    to the Bosnian Serb administration in Pale, in accordance with Rules 2(A)
16    and 55 of the Rules of Procedure and Evidence.
17            On the 7th of November, 1994, the Registrar of the Tribunal
18    forwarded the warrant addressed to the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina
19    to the relevant authorities in Sarajevo.  On the 15th of November, 1994,
20    the Registrar received official notification that the Republic of Bosnia
21    and Herzegovina was unable to execute the arrest warrant due to the fact
22    that Dragan Nikolic, the accused, resides in the town of Vlasenica, which
23    was stated to be "temporarily occupied territory controlled by
24    aggressors."
25            On the 17th of November, 1994, the warrant addressed to the
 1    Bosnian Serb authorities was physically handed by an official of the
 2    Tribunal to the members of the Bosnian Serb administration in Pale,
 3    including Mr. Koljevic, the declared Vice-President of the Bosnian Serb
 4    administration.
 5            On the 2nd of March, 1995, the Prosecutor sought to advertise the
 6    indictment in newspapers having wide circulation in the territory, as
 7    provided for in Rule 60.  Accordingly, on the 13th of March, 1995, the
 8    Registrar forwarded to the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina a request
 9    for publication.  A similar request was also forwarded the same day to the
10    Bosnian Serb administration in Pale.
11            An article announcing an indictment against Dragan Nikolic was
12    published in the newspaper Oslobodenje, the largest daily newspaper
13    circulated in the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina, on the 8th of April,
14    1995.  In addition, the details of the indictment were broadcast
15    repeatedly on the radio and television of Bosnia and Herzegovina on the
16    7th of April, 1995.  There has been no response from the Bosnian Serb
17    administration concerning its willingness or ability to execute the
18    warrant of arrest issued against Dragan Nikolic.
19            At the end of this analysis, on the basis of the elements of the
20    file, of the record, the Chamber takes the following disposition:
21            For the foregoing reasons, and pursuant to Rule 61 of the Rules of
22    Procedure and Evidence, pursuant to the confirmation of the indictment by
23    Judge Odio-Benito, dated 4 November 1994, pursuant to the decision of 16
24    May 1995 by which the same Judge ordered the Prosecutor to submit the case
25    to the Trial Chamber, and following the hearings of 9 to 13 October 1995
 1    held at the seat of this Tribunal, the Trial Chamber unanimously
 2    determines that there are reasonable grounds for believing that Dragan
 3    Nikolic committed the offences with which he is charged in the indictment
 4    issued against him by the Prosecutor and dated 1 November 1994; confirms,
 5    therefore, this entire indictment comprised of 24 counts as described
 6    above; issues an international arrest warrant against Dragan Nikolic and
 7    states that such warrant shall be transmitted to all States; takes formal
 8    note of the efforts by the Prosecutor to effect service of the indictment
 9    and states that the failure to effect service is due wholly to the failure
10    or refusal of the Bosnian Serb administration in Pale to cooperate;
11    certifies this failure and invites the President of the Tribunal to notify
12    the Security Council of the United Nations accordingly.  Dated this 20th
13    day of October, 1995, at the International Criminal Tribunal for the
14    former Yugoslavia, in The Hague, the Netherlands.
15            This session is closed.
