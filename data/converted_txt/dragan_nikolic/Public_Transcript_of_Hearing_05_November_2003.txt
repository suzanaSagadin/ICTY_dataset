 3       A.   Your Honours, with respect to the last question, I would like to
 4    say that the Max Planck Institute is an independent research institute.
 5    Its specific feature is first that it comprises both legal aspects and
 6    criminological aspects.  And its second specific feature is that it has a
 7    department dealing with specific legal orders.  So whenever a question
 8    comes up as the question of the court, what is the situation in a specific
 9    country, we can in many cases refer to specialists in our house.  We also
10    have a network of contributors which we can ask if there are questions
11    which cannot be covered by our own staff.
12            In the following, I would like to illustrate you this with respect
13    to the research which we have done for the Court.  I would like to first
14    give a short introduction with respect to the aim and the scope of the
15    research.  In a second part, I would like to turn to the research method
16    which we have used for this research, illustrating concretely the work of
17    the Max Planck Institute.  And in the third and main part of this report,
18    I would like to present you the results of our study.
19            Let me start with the scheduling order of the Trial Chamber of the
20    ICTY.  We have been requested two types of information.  One is sentencing
21    law and the other one is sentencing practice; sentencing law referring to
22    normative aspects, which we can find in the books and court decisions, and
23    which was dealt with in the Max Planck Institute, especially by its
24    criminal law section; and the other type of information concerned
25    sentencing practice, which requires empirical information, which mostly
 1    you cannot find in the books but for which you have to do criminological
 2    research.  And this part of the study was primarily conducted by the
 3    criminological department of the Max Planck Institute.
 4            The scheduling order of the ICTY - and I refer to the computer
 5    presentation, so I would like to switch on the computer evidence mode in
 6    order to follow it - the second request concerned two regions to be
 7    covered:  On the one hand side, the territory of the former Yugoslavia; on
 8    the other-hand side, other countries, especially of the Council of Europe.
 9            We have interpreted the scheduling order on the background which
10    Your Honour has just explained.  It's Article 24 of the ICTY Statute which
11    provides that "The Trial Chamber shall had recourse to the general
12    practice regarding prison sentences in the courts of the former
13    Yugoslavia."  Similarly, as laid down in Article 101 of the Rules of
14    Procedure.
15            Interpreting the scheduling order on the background of these rules
16    raised some questions.  The first question was:  Which point in time
17    should be covered by the question.  Should we deal with the situation of
18    1992, when the acts were committed?  Should we deal with the situation in
19    2003?  Or should we deal with the situation in 1992 as mitigated by
20    subsequent amendments applying the lex mitior principle.
21            Similar questions arose with respect to the relevant practice.
22    The question arose, should we consider the courts of the specific place of
23    the acts, which means especially Bosnia and Herzegovina and especially the
24    Serbian entity?  Or should we consider a kind of average of the courts in
25    all republics?
 1            An additional question concerns the issue, how should we weight
 2    the significance of the law on the territory of the former Yugoslavia and
 3    the significance of the law of the other countries?
 4            We have decided that it is up to the Court to give answers to
 5    these questions and that we will try to present the information in a way
 6    so that you can refer to each of the specific questions so that you find
 7    information about the different points in time, about the different
 8    relevant practices, about the different laws of countries, and this
 9    explains the structure of the report.  We want to leave it open to the
10    Court to answer these questions.
11            The timetable of the scheduling order was quite narrow for such an
12    extensive report.  When we received the scheduling order, we had about
13    three weeks.  We decided to take one week for defining the concept and
14    gathering the information, especially by conducting interviews in the
15    various entities.  A second week was devoted to the analysis of
16    information, and the third week we were dealing with the preparation of
17    the report, which means typing, proofreading, printing, sending it out.
18            This short timetable has some consequences on the result of the
19    study.  Using the research standard of the Max Planck, we would have
20    needed a longer time.  It's common practice to the Max Planck Institute,
21    for example, to start with a pilot study, and then to reconsider the
22    concept of the study.  We did not have the time to do this.  For that
23    reason, I'm not presenting you an ordinary study of the Max Planck
24    Institute, which would have taken half a year or a year.  However, I think
25    I can present you a solid pilot study representing significant progress
 1    compared to otherwise existing information on sentencing.  And this was
 2    also the reason why we accepted to do this research within such a short
 3    term of time, because we think having this information might be valuable
 4    for the Court.
 5            Turning to the research method, which we have to use, I would like
 6    to differentiate between the normative research and the empirical
 7    research, as I have just explained.  The normative research was first
 8    facing the problem of country selection, first for the former
 9    territory -- for the territory of the former Yugoslavia, and then for the
10    other countries.
11            In Yugoslavia, we were considering the territory of Yugoslavia in
12    1994, which means the legal framework which was set in 1976 and 1977 in
13    the Socialist Federation of Yugoslavia.  Considering not only the time of
14    1992 but also the time of today, we investigated the situation in -- I'm
15    using alphabetical order -- Croatia, in Bosnia and Herzegovina, where we
16    find a split situation, with laws of the Federation, laws of the
17    Federation of the Republic -- of the Federation and of the Republic of
18    Srpska.  We tried to investigate the situation in Serbia and Montenegro
19    and in the former Yugoslav Republic of Macedonia.  So we tried to do what
20    was possible within this time frame.
21            With respect to the member states of the Council of Europe, we
22    tried to cover as many countries as was possible using reliable methods.
23    This means having in-house staff for these countries or to have reliable
24    partners which could give us country reports for these countries, and this
25    resulted in country reports for the states which you find here on your
 1    computer screen, Austria, Belgium, England, Finland, France, Germany,
 2    Greece, Italy, Poland, the Russian Federation, Spain, Sweden, and Turkey.
 3            In addition, we tried to consider some other representative
 4    countries of the world.  We have chosen Canada and the USA; in South
 5    America, Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Mexico; in Africa, the Ivory Coast and
 6    South Africa; and in addition, in Asia, China; and furthermore, Australia.
 7    In order to give a representative picture which we could provide with
 8    reliable in-house staff or partners.
 9            With respect to understanding the results of our study, it is most
10    important to address the methods of legal comparison, which we have used
11    for fulfilling our tasks.  In legal comparison, you have in principle two
12    methods you can use, as illustrated on the computer screen, I would like
13    to call the first method a law-based method and the second method
14    case-based method.  Doing legal comparison and using a law-based method
15    means comparing statutes.  Using this method, for example, we would
16    compare the statute on murder in one country with the statute on murder in
17    another country.  This method creates various difficulties
18    between -- because the problems which are dealt in one country are often
19    described - for example, in the headline of a statute - with a different
20    term in another country.  So it might happen that questions which are
21    dealt in country A with a section on rape might be covered in another
22    country with a section on aggravated sexual assault or something.
23            Or to give another question:  If we are referring to crimes
24    against humanity, it might happen that in certain legal orders there are
25    no crimes against humanity or crimes against humanity committed by murder.
 1    So if we would only refer in our comparative work to certain statutes, the
 2    answer would be in country B there is no such law.  The respective problem
 3    might be solved, not in a crime by -- against humanity but in a crime
 4    named murder.  However, since there is no statute on crimes against
 5    humanity, the answer would be it's not punished in this country.  Now,
 6    it's obvious that this would not be an adequate answer to the question
 7    which we have to fulfil and to the legal background of the scheduling
 8    order.  For that reason, it seemed to be more appropriate, at least for
 9    certain parts of the research, to turn to a case-based method, comparing
10    results of cases.
11            Using this method, we would not compare certain statutes, but we
12    would compare the results of specific cases.  So the starting point for
13    this case-based method is not a special statute, like crimes against
14    humanity, but it's a kind of case or a model case, like A kills B using an
15    iron stick and he had this and that intention, what is the result of this
16    case in a specific country?
17            Deciding between these methods with respect to the territory of
18    the former Yugoslavia, we relied on a law-based method because in
19    Yugoslavia we had in addition an empirical research with which we did
20    compare the cases, so our approach for the territory of the former
21    Yugoslavia, where we had also very short time for interviews with the
22    judges, was to compare in principle statutes but to widen the view a bit
23    and not only to compare murder but to admit that, for example, war crimes
24    are included in the research.
25            As far as the other countries are concerned, we primarily used the
 1    case-based method.  In our questionnaire, we gave some model cases to our
 2    country reporters and asked them what would have been the sentence range
 3    for this case in your country?  And we left it open to the country
 4    reporter to decide which provision is applicable.
 5            The questionnaire for the country reporters started with general
 6    questions.  We asked them to indicate in the questionnaires information
 7    about sentences for serial criminal offences, factors for determining
 8    actual sentence, changes due to the offender's role as principal or aider
 9    or changes due to a guilty plea.  We furthermore asked about the
10    possibilities to alter sentences after its imposition, especially about
11    early release and parole, rules of pardons, amnesties, and other types of
12    clemency.
13            The second part of our normative questionnaire was dealing with
14    model cases, and it was the task of the country reporters to find out the
15    sentencing ranges for these cases.  Building up these model cases, we
16    orientated this in the counts of indictment and the guilty plea, so we
17    gave our country reporters two variations of murder cases, which are very
18    close to the cases which you have in your indictment; two alternatives of
19    torture, which are mentioned in the indictment; three cases of sexual
20    violence; and three variations of persecutions.  And for each of these
21    cases, the country reporters had to give us the statutes which according
22    to their opinion would apply in their country and what would be the
23    outcome in sentencing ranges.
24            In the questionnaire, we also asked for modifications.  We asked
25    for the situation in 1992 and the situation for today.  Apart from these
 1    changes due to the time passed, we also asked them:  Would the sentences
 2    range in your legal order be changed to a guilty plea?  And furthermore,
 3    would there be a difference if the crime would be viewed as a crime
 4    against humanity or as a general ordinary crime, like murder or rape?
 5            With respect to the second aspect, the empirical research, we
 6    could do this empirical research not in all of these countries.  We
 7    concentrated on the territory of the Socialist Federation of Yugoslavia,
 8    and we developed an empirical questionnaire for judges which we
 9    interviewed in the former Yugoslavia.
10            This questionnaire was different from the normative process for
11    our country reporters.  We had to take into account that the time of the
12    judges is very short, was extremely difficult within one week to find
13    judges which we could interview, and we assumed that we have about 30 to
14    60 minutes with the judges, so we had to be careful that the questionnaire
15    did not become too complicated.  In the questionnaire, we first asked for
16    some normative aspects; these are different reasons.  It helped us to find
17    out the situation in the entities.  It gave us a certain control about the
18    knowledge of the judges and a further aim was that the judges first should
19    consider their legal orders before they come out with a certain sentencing
20    range.
21            For that reason, we asked the judges which we interviewed first
22    for the maximum penalties in their legal order, for the criteria for
23    sentencing, for the specific provisions on murder, torture, rape, and
24    combined offences, and for the differences in the legal situation between
25    1992 and 2003.
 1            The second part of the questionnaire then concerned the previous
 2    courtroom experience of the judges.  We did not only want to ask them to
 3    evaluate artificial model cases, but we wanted them to remember what they
 4    did up till now, and for that reason we asked in the second part of the
 5    empirical questionnaire the judges, what is your previous courtroom
 6    experience with rape, murder, torture, or combined offences?  How many
 7    murder cases did you sentence already?  And what was your highest and your
 8    lowest outcome in these cases?
 9            After these types of questions, we started with a third type in
10    our empirical questionnaire, which was judging model cases.  We built some
11    model cases and asked the judges, what is be your sentence in these cases?
12    These model cases were a bit different from the model cases which we used
13    in the country reports.  With respect to the country reports, we had
14    contributors which could work for a whole week on the cases; here we had
15    judge who only had maybe 60 minutes' time.  For that reason, these model
16    cases were much simpler.  It read, for example, as you can see on the
17    computer animation:  "A 35-year-old man with no prior record commits the
18    following acts against detainees under his authority, and the acts are
19    rape, murder, torture, or the combined offences against five to ten
20    victims."  We also asked them, is there a difference with respect to the
21    point in time?  Would there be a difference if you judged it as crimes
22    against humanity, a term under which these judges also included war crime,
23    as we found out, and would there be a difference if there was a guilty
24    plea or not for your judgement?
25             These types of questions, based on a define questionnaire with
 1    detailed instructions for our interviewers, was posed to a number of
 2    judges.  We tried to find a representative sample of judges.  In Bosnia
 3    and Herzegovina, we interviewed three judges in the Bosnia and Herzegovina
 4    Federation and three judges in Republika Srpska.  In Croatia, we started
 5    with three judges in Central Croatia and two judges which we found in
 6    addition in East Slavonia.  In the former Yugoslav Republic of Macedonia
 7    or Macedonia, we interviewed three judges.  In Serbia and Montenegro, we
 8    managed to find three judges in Montenegro.  We tried to interview judges
 9    in Serbia; however, all these judges needed an authorisation by the
10    president of their court.  We managed to get these authorisations for all
11    the courts which I have mentioned.  It was extremely difficult to do all
12    this in one week.  Only in Serbia the answers were negative; the judges
13    there that they have not yet -- or they don't have an authorisation of
14    their administration, and for that reason we could not interview judges as
15    was foreseen in Serbia.  Slovenia was not covered in our study.
16             The interviews took about 30 minutes to 1 hour.  The atmosphere
17    was very cooperative.  We did not tell the judges that it was a research
18    for this court.  We wanted to avoid any negative influence on this.  We
19    just said it's a research of the Max Planck Institute.  And we also
20    started asking them first the ordinary crimes.  And only at the end we
21    turned to crimes against humanity in order to really get a neutral picture
22    of the situation, and it is my feeling that this -- the results of the
23    studies are not affected by any political or ethnical attitudes of the
24    judges.
25             After having explained the legal and the empirical methods with
 1    which we tried to answer the questions, I would like to turn to the
 2    results of our study.  Coming to the results, I would like to
 3    differentiate first between the results for the territory of the former
 4    Yugoslavia and then to the results for the other states, and within both
 5    sections I would like to differentiate between the normative results and
 6    the empirical results. I'm starting with the territory of the former
 7    Yugoslavia. First, dealing with the normative framework, because the
 8    normative framework sets the standards for the empirical judging, which I
 9    will deal with after.
10            Describing the normative framework in the former Socialist
11    Federation of Yugoslavia is difficult, especially if you are taking into
12    account two points of time and the various republics and entities.  If you
13    are dealing with the situation in 1994, it is necessary to explain the
14    situation in the former Socialist Federation.  If you are dealing with the
15    situation today, you have to give answers for the various states, for
16    Slovenia, Croatia, Bosnia-Herzegovina, Serbia, and Montenegro, and
17    Macedonia.  And in addition, within these countries, you have to deal with
18    the situation in different entities, which is different.  Giving this
19    picture is especially difficult because in all of these entities the laws
20    changed.
21            The situation in these entities is difficult to find out due to
22    problems of access to information.  Due to the specific situation in these
23    entities, it seemed to us that even sometimes it is not completely clear
24    to the judges there which laws they have to apply.
25            In order to illustrate this, I'm referring to a computer
 1    presentation which will gain importance in my future report.  It
 2    illustrates that in the beginning in the former Yugoslavia there was the
 3    Criminal Code of 1951.  The situation then changed with the Constitution
 4    of 1974, which gave a certain at least legal autonomy to the various
 5    republics.  As a result of this autonomy which was given to the different
 6    republics, we have a split situation -- legal situation in Yugoslavia,
 7    starting in 1976 or 1977.  In 1976, the Criminal Code of the Socialist
 8    Federation of Yugoslavia was enacted.  This general code for the whole
 9    Federation was especially dealing with provisions of the general part.
10    Usually you read a description about Yugoslavia; it's just the general
11    part.  However, if you look into more detail in this code, it also
12    contains a number of specific statutes, especially the war crimes.
13            Besides this code of the Federation, there were codes of the
14    various republics - Bosnia and Herzegovina, Croatia, Serbia, and the other
15    ones - these codes were created in 1972 and they were dealing especially
16    with a special part of criminal law, for example, with provisions on
17    murder, on rape, on torture.  You don't find these provisions in the code
18    of the Federation.  You just find it in the code of the republics.
19            However, in addition, these codes of the republics also contained
20    some general parts of criminal law which were not regulated in the code of
21    the Federation.  For example, the provisions on early release were not
22    regulated in the code of the Federation.  They can be found in the codes
23    of the republics.
24            So answering Your Honours' question at the beginning, one would
25    have to say the relationship between these codes was that both codes

 1    existed beside each other and each code dealt with a specific area.
 2            When the old republic broke apart, with the disintegration,
 3    especially in 1992, and the entities became independent, at the beginning
 4    there was no time to develop new criminal codes.  Within the various
 5    republics, there was just a reference to the old codes which were declared
 6    to be applicable.  This was done first by presidential decrees --
 7    declaring the state of war, declaring the continuity of certain laws,
 8    including the criminal codes -- and later on, for example, in Bosnia and
 9    Herzegovina, in 1994 there was a law enacted on the retroactive
10    application of these new laws.  This means that in 1992 the old code of
11    the Federation of 1966 [sic], which became in effect in 1977, as well as
12    the codes of the republics, such as the Bosnia and Herzegovina code of
13    1977, were applicable.
14            Subsequently, the republics enacted their own criminal acts.  For
15    that reason, you find in 1998 a Criminal Code of Bosnia and Herzegovina
16    Federation.  Beside this there is a code for the Republika Srpska of 2000
17    and there is a code for the Brcko district.  In Bosnia and Herzegovina
18    Federation, the situation became a bit more complicated when in 2003 a new
19    code was enacted.  In addition, in the year 2003, the Office of the High
20    Representative enacted a Criminal Code for the whole territory of Bosnia
21    and Herzegovina.  The relationship between the codes of 1998, 2000, and
22    2003, on the one-hand side, for the republics, and the OHR code of 2003 is
23    similar to the relationship of the codes in the Federation of 1976 and
24    1977.  They exist beside each other. Each of the two types of code covers
