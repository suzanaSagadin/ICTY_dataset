 5            JUDGE GUNEY: [Interpretation] Thank you.
 6            As announced in the order scheduling the hearing issued on 25th
 7    September 2007, the Appeals Chamber will deliver its judgement today in
 8    this case.
 9            Following the practice of the International Tribunal, I will not
10    read out the text of the judgement except for the disposition.  Instead, I
11    will summarise the issues on appeal and the findings of the Appeals
12    Chamber.  This summary is therefore not part of the written judgement,
13    which is the only authoritative account of the Appeals Chamber's rulings
14    and reasons.  Copies of the written judgement will be made available to
15    the parties at the conclusion of this hearing.
16            The events underlying this appeal took place in 1993 in Bosnia and
17    Herzegovina, in the village of Grabovica, about 30 kilometres north of
18    Mostar.  The Trial Chamber found that 13 persons of Croatian decent taking
19    no active part in hostilities were killed in this village of Grabovica
20    between 8 and 9 September 1993 by troops billeted in the village.  There
21    is no disagreement among the parties that these crimes occurred.
22            At the time of these events, Sefer Halilovic was Chief of the Main
23    Staff of the Army of the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina.  According to
24    the Prosecution, he was the commander of a military operation called
25    Neretva 1993 -- or Neretva-93.
 1            In its judgement of 16 November 2005, the Trial Chamber found that
 2    the Prosecution had not proven beyond reasonable doubt that
 3    Sefer Halilovic was either the de jure or de facto commander of Operation
 4    Neretva nor that he had effective control over the troops who committed
 5    the crimes in Grabovica.  As a result, the Trial Chamber acquitted him of
 6    the only charge, a charge of murder as violation of the laws or customs of
 7    war, punishable under Article 3 and Article 7(3) of the Statute.
 8            The Prosecution appealed the judgement, seeking the reversal of
 9    the acquittal for the charge of murder with respect to the killings
10    perpetrated in Grabovica.  The parties made oral submissions before the
11    Appeals Chamber in the appeal hearings on 10th and 11th July 2007.
12            The Prosecution's appeal consists of four grounds.  The first
13    ground of appeal, composed of six sub-grounds, hinges upon the issue of
14    whether Sefer Halilovic had effective control over the offending troops in
15    Grabovica.  The second and third grounds concern the other two
16    requirements of superior responsibility under Article 7(3) of the Statute
17    (the knowledge of the criminal conduct of the subordinates and the failure
18    to prevent or punish).  The fourth ground of appeal concerns the admission
19    into evidence of the report and proposed testimony of an expert witness,
20    of a military expert witness.
21            Sefer Halilovic has submitted that the entire appeal of the
22    Prosecution should be summarily dismissed.  He also, alternatively,
23    requested all grounds to be dismissed.  The Appeals Chamber has denied
24    this request as unfounded and has therefore examined the Prosecution's
25    appeal on its merits.
 1            Let me now briefly summarise the conclusions of the Appeals
 2    Chamber with respect to the first ground of appeal.
 3            The Prosecution submits that the Trial Chamber erred in law and in
 4    fact in concluding that the Prosecution failed to prove beyond a
 5    reasonable doubt that Sefer Halilovic was the superior of the troops who
 6    committed the murders in Grabovica and in consequently entering an
 7    acquittal.  The Prosecution requests the Appeals Chamber to apply the
 8    correct legal standards, admit evidence erroneously excluded, and to
 9    proceed by making its own findings of fact regarding the existence of a
10    superior-subordinate relationship between Sefer Halilovic and the
11    perpetrators.
12            The six sub-grounds of appeal advanced under the Prosecution's
13    first ground of appeal are deeply intertwined:  They all go to the issue
14    of whether Sefer Halilovic was the superior of the troops who committed
15    the crimes in Grabovica.  I note that, for reasons explained in the
16    judgement, the Appeals Chamber has not followed the order proposed by the
17    Prosecution in the presentation of its sub-grounds -- of its six
18    sub-grounds.
19            The Appeals Chamber has first addressed sub-ground 6, which seeks
20    the admission into evidence of a statement given by Sefer Halilovic to the
21    Prosecution in 1976 -- 1996.  The Prosecution submits that the Trial
22    Chamber erred in law in refusing to admit into evidence this statement.
23    The Appeals Chamber unanimously found that the Trial Chamber was correct
24    when it rejected the statement.  All Judges agree, Judge Meron and
25    Judge Schomburg on different grounds than the majority, that the Trial
 1    Chamber enforced the procedural safeguards of the Rules of Procedure and
 2    Evidence in order to protect the due process rights of the accused before
 3    the International Tribunal.
 4            The Appeals Chamber has then addressed on the merits sub-ground 1,
 5    under which the Prosecution claims that the Trial Chamber erred in law in
 6    applying the legal standard required to demonstrate effective control.
 7            The Prosecution submits that the Trial Chamber erred in law in
 8    treating "command" as a legal requirement of superior responsibility
 9    pursuant to Article 7(3) of the Statute.  It claims that, as a
10    consequence, the Trial Chamber erroneously focused on Sefer Halilovic's
11    role as commander of Operation Neretva and on his control of combat
12    operations to determine his effective control over the offending troops.
13    The Appeals Chamber has considered carefully the way the Prosecution
14    pleaded its case at trial and the fact that a trier of fact is necessarily
15    bound by the theories of the case advanced by the Prosecution.  It
16    concluded that the Prosecution clearly pleaded that Sefer Halilovic
17    exercised effective control by virtue of his command of Operation Neretva.
18            The Appeals Chamber has therefore dismissed the Prosecution's
19    allegation that the Trial Chamber erred in failing to consider whether
20    Sefer Halilovic had effective control over the perpetrators of the crime
21    by reason of his position as team leader of the inspection team or by
22    virtue of his position as the most senior ranking officer in Herzegovina
23    at the time.  As a result, the Appeals Chamber limited its analysis to the
24    Prosecution's submissions regarding Sefer Halilovic's effective control as
25    commander of Operation Neretva.  In so doing, the Appeals Chamber focused
 1    its assessment specifically on the allegation that he was a de facto
 2    commander, since the Prosecution acknowledged that it failed to prove de
 3    jure command.
 4            The Appeals Chamber notes that the Trial Chamber correctly set out
 5    the elements that must be satisfied to hold a superior responsible under
 6    Article 7(3) of the Statute.  However, the Appeals Chamber has found that
 7    the Trial Chamber erred when it appeared to create a new requirement to
 8    the third element of superior responsibility, that is, failure to take the
 9    necessary and reasonable measures to prevent the criminal act or punish
10    the perpetrators thereof, by unhelpfully adding an artificial distinction
11    between "general" and "specific" obligations to prevent crimes.  In any
12    event, for the reasons I will discuss, this particular error does not have
13    an impact on Sefer Halilovic's acquittal.
14            Under sub-ground 5, the Prosecution submits that the Trial Chamber
15    erred in misapplying the standard of proof throughout the judgement.
16    Under sub-ground 2 and 3, the Prosecution raises two specific allegations
17    of errors related to the same alleged mistake.
18            First, the Appeals Chamber considered that the Prosecution
19    misinterpreted the holding by the Trial Chamber that "any ambiguity or
20    doubt" was to be resolved in favour of the accused.  The Trial Chamber in
21    this case simply reworded the beyond reasonable doubt standard in a manner
22    consistent with the jurisprudence of the Tribunal.
23            The Prosecution also alleges that the Trial Chamber erroneously
24    applied the beyond reasonable doubt standard to all exhibits and findings
25    and not only to the ultimate issue of Sefer Halilovic's guilt.  While not
 1    every factual finding must be established beyond reasonable doubt, every
 2    fact which is indispensable for a conviction must be.  Our case-law has
 3    made it clear that, apart from the elements of the crime and the
 4    requirements for criminal responsibility of the accused, there may be
 5    other facts indispensable for a conviction, depending on the specific
 6    circumstances of the case and on the way the case was pleaded by the
 7    Prosecution.
 8            In any event, since the Prosecution has only identified a handful
 9    of findings that were erroneously reached, the Appeals Chamber has limited
10    itself to consider these.  The Appeals Chamber has, in this respect, found
11    that the Trial Chamber did not require proof of the name Operation Neretva
12    as a necessary precondition for a finding of effective control.  The same
13    can be said of the discussions and decisions taken at a meeting in Zenica
14    to which Halilovic is said -- in which Halilovic is said to have
15    participated.
16            Similarly, despite having found that the Trial Chamber did err in
17    focusing on whether a formal "forward command post" has been established
18    in the town of Jablanica, the Appeals Chamber has concluded that the
19    Prosecution failed to substantiate its claim that had the existence of
20    this post been established at trial, the Trial Chamber would have
21    concluded that Sefer Halilovic was de facto commander of the perpetrators
22    of the crimes.
23            The Appeals Chamber has further considered sub-ground 4 of the
24    first ground of appeal, under which the Prosecution submits that the Trial
25    Chamber erred in concluding that, since Sefer Halilovic did not initiate
 1    or carry forward investigations, he did not have the material ability to
 2    punish the perpetrators of the crimes.
 3            In this regard, the Appeals Chamber first considered that the
 4    wording used by the Trial Chamber might be confusing, since the failure to
 5    initiate investigations is not, as such, an indication of a lack of power
 6    to investigate.  However, the Trial Chamber did not base its conclusion
 7    that Sefer Halilovic lacked the material ability to punish solely on the
 8    evidence that Sefer Halilovic did not initiate investigations.  On the
 9    contrary, it carefully analysed the evidence before it, notably the
10    specific rules and the evidence regarding the authority to conduct such
11    criminal investigations, before concluding that Sefer Halilovic did not
12    have the material ability to punish the perpetrators of these crimes.  The
13    Appeals Chamber has found that it was not an unreasonable conclusion,
14    given the evidence in the record.
15            In any event, even assuming that Sefer Halilovic had the ability
16    to contribute to an investigation or to the punishment of the perpetrators
17    of the crimes committed in Grabovica, these abilities can only amount to
18    effective control relevant for Article 7(3) of the Statute if they are the
19    consequence of a relationship of subordination between Sefer Halilovic and
20    these perpetrators.  The Prosecution failed to show how Sefer Halilovic's
21    alleged position as de facto commander of Operation Neretva or his
22    position as team leader of the inspection team established a chain of
23    command or hierarchical relationship between him and the perpetrators
24    amounting to such a superior-subordinate relationship.
25            Therefore, the Appeals Chamber concluded that the Prosecution
 1    failed to show that no reasonable trier of fact could have reached the
 2    conclusion that Sefer Halilovic did not have the required degree of
 3     "effective control" over the perpetrators, as a commander of Operation
 4    Neretva, to establish his superior responsibility under Article 7(3) of
 5    the Statute.
 6            As a result, the first ground of appeal is dismissed.
 7            The Appeals Chamber, having concluded that the Prosecution has
 8    failed to show that the Trial Chamber erred in finding that a
 9    superior-subordinate relationship between Sefer Halilovic and the
10    offending troops had not been established, the Prosecution's remaining
11    grounds of appeal became moot.
