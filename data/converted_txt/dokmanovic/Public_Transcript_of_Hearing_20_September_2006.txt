14    course, by necessity, then placed in the hands of the government where the

16    witness indicated that he would not attend.  Telephone, informal
17    inquiries, by the witness unit here received a response that he was not
18    prepared to attend under any circumstances.
19            The Chamber has therefore evaluated, at the request of Mr. Vasic,
20    whether in the present circumstances as they are known to the Chamber
21    there is justification for the Chamber to seek to take any further steps
22    in an effort to request the attendance of the witness.  Now, present
23    appreciation is that those steps would only be possible if we were
24    prepared to order the commencement of contempt proceedings against the
25    witness for his failure to respond to the subpoena and then proceed to
 1    have the government of the country where he lives effect his arrest and
 2    transfer.  That is a dramatic step in any trial for a witness of a party.
 3    It's certainly a dramatic step in an International Tribunal where it is
 4    necessary to invoke such powers and compulsive process to ensure the
 5    attendance of a witness.  So dramatic it is perceived that our research
 6    has indicated that no case in the whole history of the Tribunal where that
 7    has occurred at the instance of Prosecution or the Defence.  This Chamber
 8    refused such an application made by the Prosecution in the last trial
 9    which we heard.  There was a recent case where the Chamber itself in
10    another trial concluded that it was essential in the interests of justice
11    that a small number of witnesses who had not been called by either party
12    should give evidence, and one of those witnesses refused to attend and the
13    Chamber in the end instituted contempt proceedings.  They did not proceed
14    because the witness in the circumstances was persuaded to cooperate and
15    did attend.
16            We have looked in particular at what is provided to us as an
17    indication of the evidence which the Defence expects to receive from the
18    witness, and we note that it is based essentially on identification of
19    various positions which the witness is known to have held and to suggest
20    from that that the witness ought to be able to give evidence about various
21    events.  We have also noted the summary provided by the Prosecution when
22    that witness was on their list, and we note there that the evidence of
23    that witness focussed primarily on one other of the accused, not on
24    Mr. Mrksic.
25            The nature of the material by way of summary which the Defence of
 1    Mr. Mrksic has placed before us does not provide any basis upon which we
 2    could conclude on what is presently known to us, that the evidence of this
 3    witness is in some way vital to the question of the guilt or innocence of
 4    Mr. Mrksic.  There's no question that the witness ought to be able to give
 5    relevant evidence, but we would perceive that it ought to be a matter of
 6    very substantial significance in the trial before we consider there was
 7    justification for taking the most unusual step of attempting to achieve
 8    the compulsory attendance of the witness.
 9            In those circumstances the Chamber has not been persuaded that it
10    should initiate any action at the present time in respect of Witness P019,
11    with a view to ensuring under compulsion the attendance of that witness to
12    give evidence.
