     4      MR. CASEY:  May it please the court, good morning, my name is
     5          Lee Casey, and I am appearing on behalf of the Republic
     6          of Croatia and I would like to address some of the legal
     7          issues involved on this appeal.
     8                The subpoena duces tecum issued by Trial Chamber
     9          II to the Republic of Croatia and its Defence Minister
    10          was in clear violation of the accepted rules of
    11          international law and practice, and exceeded the lawful
    12          authority conferred on the International Tribunal by its
    13          founding Statute.
    14                As the Appeals Chamber found in its decision on
    15          jurisdiction in the Tadic case, the International
    16          Tribunal was established by the Security Council as "a
    17          judicial organ", a court of law.
    18                One of the characteristics of a judicial organ is
    19          both the source and limit of its legitimate lawful
    20          authority.  The possession of jurisdiction over the
    21          individual or entity to be affected, and the subject
    22          matter to be decided, is essential to the legitimate
    23          exercise of judicial power.
    24                By issuing a compulsory order to the Republic of
    25          Croatia, and to its Defence Minister, Trial Chamber II
     1          exceeded the International Tribunal's lawful legitimate
     2          authority.
     3                As it has itself acknowledged, the International
     4          Tribunal does not have jurisdiction over States.  In
     5          this regard, the Tribunal's 1995 Yearbook explains
     6          that:
     7                "The Tribunal’s Statute, which was approved by the
     8          Security Council in Resolution 827 (1993) 'is the source
     9          of the Tribunal's jurisdiction,' and 'the Statute does
    10          not, however, give the Tribunal personal jurisdiction
    11          over entities, such as States, organisations and
    12          associations.'"
    13                At no point does the ICTY Statute, either
    14          expressly or by implication, grant the International
    15          Tribunal jurisdiction over States.  Ancillary or
    16          incidental jurisdiction cannot serve as a substitute for
    17          a primary jurisdiction or grant, as incorrectly
    18          suggested by both the Prosecution and Trial Chamber II.
    19          A court's ancillary jurisdiction can be used only in aid
    20          of its primary jurisdiction, and only with respect to
    21          individuals or entities that are otherwise subject to
    22          its legal authority.
    23                Indeed, if courts could issue subpoenas (or other
    24          orders) to individuals or entities beyond their
    25          jurisdiction based upon the pretext of exercising some
     1          ancillary jurisdiction, then any court, domestic or
     2          international, would be entitled to issue binding
     3          process to any individual entity or government anywhere
     4          in the world.
     5                This simply is not the law.  The entire system of
     6          international co-operation using "requests for
     7          assistance" and "letters rogatory" has developed for the
     8          very reason that both domestic courts and international
     9          tribunals are limited by their subject-matter and
    10          personal jurisdiction, and cannot issue legally binding
    11          orders to individuals or entities, including States,
    12          otherwise beyond their jurisdiction.
    13                Consequently, however broadly the ICTY Statute is
    14          interpreted, it cannot support the issuance of a
    15          compulsory order by the International Tribunal to a
    16          State, an entity entirely beyond its legal authority.
    17                Both Trial Chamber II and the Prosecution have
    18          asserted that the power to issue compulsory orders for
    19          the production of documents, whether or not called a
    20          subpoena, is found in most legal systems, and that the
    21          International Tribunal therefore "has the inherent power
    22          to compel the production of documents necessary for a
    23          proper execution of its judicial function".
    24                Although most courts may have the authority to
    25          issue orders for the production of evidence -- although
     1          not the very unique type of subpoena duces tecum issued
     2          by Trial Chamber II to Croatia -- no court may issue
     3          such an order on a compulsory basis to an individual or
     4          entity not subject to its lawful jurisdiction.
     5                Moreover, the International Tribunal is an
     6          international court.  There is no precedent for an
     7          International Tribunal issuing compulsory orders under
     8          threat of sanction to a State.  In fact, the ability of
     9          international tribunals to obtain documentary evidence
    10          from States is highly limited.  The leading
    11          international case authority in this area is the
    12          International Court of Justice's ruling in the Corfu
    13          Channel case, a case where the court requested from
    14          Great Britain certain documents that it thought would be
    15          useful to resolution of the matter.  The United Kingdom
    16          refused to deliver those documents and indeed instructed
    17          its witnesses to refuse to answer questions regarding
    18          them.
    19                The court properly respected this refusal, and
    20          declined to draw any conclusions against Britain based
    21          on the fact that the documents were not produced.  There
    22          is no warrant in either law or reason for the
    23          application of a different rule to Croatia.
    24                Both the Trial Chamber and the Prosecution have
    25          sought to avoid these settled rules of law by suggesting
     1          that the International Tribunal is not subject to the
     2          accepted rules of domestic or international law because
     3          it was established as an enforcement measure pursuant to
     4          Chapter VII of the United Nations Charter.
     5                This extraordinary claim is manifestly incorrect.
     6          The authority to formulate and adopt Chapter VII
     7          enforcement measures against a State represents the very
     8          highest of the Security Council's power.  Its use
     9          involves fundamentally political decisions that are
    10          themselves antithetical to the International Tribunal's
    11          judicial character.  At no point did the Security
    12          Council even purport to vest the International Tribunal
    13          with such vast power.
    14                Indeed, it is very doubtful whether the Security
    15          Council could have delegated the authority to formulate
    16          and adopt Chapter VII enforcement measures to any other
    17          body, since such a delegation would deprive the five
    18          permanent members of the Security Council of their right
    19          to exercise a veto with respect to any particular
    20          measure.
    21                Consequently, the Tribunal does not have, and
    22          cannot exercise, the lawful authority to formulate and
    23          implement compulsory, coercive measures vis-a-vis
    24          States, whether in the form of orders or otherwise.
    25                The relationship of States to the International
     1          Tribunal is, in fact, governed by ICTY Statute Article
     2          29, which requires States to co-operate with the
     3          International Tribunal and to comply with certain
     4          requests for assistance and orders issued by a Trial
     5          Chamber.
     6                Significantly, Article 29 does not purport to
     7          bring States within the Tribunal's jurisdiction or
     8          competence, nor does it constitute a grant of authority
     9          to the International Tribunal with respect to States.
    10          Rather, it is addressed directly to States (and not to
    11          the Tribunal), requiring them to co-operate with the
    12          Tribunal and to assist it in the implementation of its
    13          orders.  Without Article 29, States would have had no
    14          such obligation.
    15                Significantly, Article 29 does not provide any
    16          authority for the International Tribunal to sanction a
    17          State that fails to carry out its obligations, and no
    18          such authority can be found in any other provision of
    19          the ICTY Statute.  This is hardly surprising, since the
    20          ICTY was not vested with either personal jurisdiction
    21          over a State, or with the subject-matter jurisdiction or
    22          competence necessary for it to determine whether any
    23          particular State has, or has not, complied with its
    24          international law obligations.
    25                This authority would, however, be essential to any
     1          determination by the Tribunal that a State has not
     2          carried out its responsibilities vis-a-vis the
     3          International Tribunal, and to the imposition of any
     4          sanction upon a State or its officials.
     5                Trial Chamber II also has erred in ruling that a
     6          subpoena duces tecum could be issued to an individual
     7          state official, requiring the official to take actions
     8          within his official capacity, and that such an official
     9          could be required to appear before the Trial Chamber to
    10          explain any non-compliance.  States, and not
    11          individuals, are the subjects of international law.  To
    12          the extent that States (including Croatia) have
    13          international law obligations, those obligations are a
    14          charge upon the State, not upon individual state
    15          officers.  Consequently, the International Tribunal can
    16          address itself (that is can issue a order or make a
    17          request that might trigger a State's obligations under
    18          Article 29), only to a State.  It cannot issue
    19          directives to the state's officials requiring them
    20          personally to take some official action on behalf of
    21          their State, and calling them to account if their State
    22          fails to do as it is told.
    23                Not surprisingly, there is no international law
    24          precedent for holding the individual officials of a
    25          State accountable either for the execution of a state's
     1          international law obligations, or for their violation.
     2          And, in this respect, Trial Chamber II correctly held
     3          that individual state officers could not personally be
     4          sanctioned for the failure of their State to comply with
     5          the Tribunal's orders.
     6                The same legal and practical considerations that
     7          led the Trial Chamber to hold that individual officers
     8          cannot be sanctioned for failing to comply with an order
     9          apply equally to summoning them to appear before the
    10          Tribunal.
    11                Finally, the Appeals Chamber should consider the
    12          astonishing implications of the Prosecution's assertions
    13          that the International Tribunal may issue orders to
    14          individual state officials requiring them to exercise
    15          their government offices in a particular manner.  Under
    16          the Prosecution's theory, these orders could require any
    17          action that the Tribunal might deem necessary to achieve
    18          its mission, up to and including any level of force the
    19          Tribunal may consider to be appropriate.  Mere
    20          considerations of domestic law, foreign policy and
    21          national security would be entirely irrelevant.
    22                The officials of any State that failed to obey
    23          would be subject to whatever sanction the court might
    24          see fit to impose, whether it be a fine, imprisonment or
    25          removal from office.  Again, the constitutional, legal
     1          or political arrangements of the State in question would
     2          be of no consequence.
     3                It is inherently incredible to suggest that the
     4          Security Council either vested or intended to vest the
     5          International Tribunal with such unlimited and
     6          unprecedented power.
     7                Significantly, the Prosecution has not denied
     8          either the nature or the extent of the power it claims
     9          for the International Tribunal.  Indeed, the Prosecution
    10          itself has been unable to identify any legal limits on
    11          the power it claims.  It has, of course, suggested that
    12          the "mandatory compliance power expressly conferred by
    13          Article 29(2) of the Statute will rarely, if ever, need
    14          to be invoked with respect to" States beyond the
    15          territory limits of the former Yugoslavia.  The
    16          Prosecution states, "these compliance powers should be
    17          used against States when this is really necessary".
    18                Those nations not today in the Prosecution's
    19          sights should take cold comfort from these assurances.
    20          They may well wish to reflect that "necessity" is a
    21          justification that is inherently incapable of
    22          limitation; it cannot be quarantined.
    23                The Appeals Chamber should definitively reject the
    24          Prosecution's assertions that the International Tribunal
    25          may issue orders to individual state officials requiring
     1          them to exercise their government offices in a
     2          particular manner, or face sanction if they refuse to
     3          comply.
     4                The Trial Chamber also has incorrectly concluded
     5          that the issuance of what is, in fact, an
     6          United States-style third party "discovery" device,
     7          known as a "subpoena duces tecum", is a valid exercise
     8          of its authority to issue orders.
     9                Although the Prosecution has urged the Appeals
    10          Chamber not to examine the substance or terms of the
    11          subpoena duces tecum at issue here, the nature and form
    12          of Trial Chamber II's subpoena are very much in issue.
    13          From the very beginning of this process, Croatia has
    14          strongly objected to both the suggestion that the Trial
    15          Chamber could issue a compulsory order to a State, and
    16          to a particular form of order sought by the Prosecution
    17          in this case.
    18                Moreover, a detailed examination of the substance
    19          of the subpoena is necessary to the disposition of two
    20          arguments raised by the Prosecution, that the authority
    21          to issue a subpoena duces tecum to a State is inherent
    22          in the authority of all courts, and hence may be implied
    23          by the International Tribunal, and that the Tribunal has
    24          the "express" power to issue subpoenas and orders, and
    25          so also must be authorised to issue a subpoena duces
     1          tecum like that issued by Trial Chamber II.
     2                Whatever the Tribunal's authority to issue
     3          compulsory orders may be, it cannot be assumed that it
     4          is authorised to issue the type of subpoena duces tecum
     5          at issue here.
     6                The subpoena duces tecum sought by the Prosecutor,
     7          and issued by the Trial Chamber here, is in form and
     8          substance fundamentally different from the orders most
     9          national courts may issue requiring production of
    10          evidence for trial.
    11                It has not, as the Prosecution suggests, existed
    12          since Late Antiquity.  Rather, the subpoena duces tecum
    13          issued to Croatia is in form and substance an
    14          American-style civil discovery request, a coercive order
    15          issued to a non-party and requiring the production of
    16          documents that may or may not exist, and that may or may
    17          not be relevant to the issues before the court.  It is a
    18          classic fishing expedition.
    19                Indeed, the fishing expedition nature of the
    20          Prosecution's requests in this case are revealed by its
    21          own admissions that it has requested these records
    22          merely because they are the sorts of documents that
    23          States normally maintain, not because they are documents
    24          known to exist, to be relevant to the Blaskic
    25          Prosecution, and unavailable from any other source.
     1                Far from representing the normal, "inherent" or
     2          accepted authority of all courts, the American rules
     3          have not been widely copied outside the United States.
     4          Their application beyond the United States (particularly
     5          in the form of a subpoena duces tecum) has been highly
     6          controversial.
     7                By the same token, the reference to Article 29(2)
     8          to "orders for the production of evidence" cannot be
     9          assumed to mean the issuance of a US-style subpoena
    10          duces tecum.  Its most probable meaning, in accordance
    11          with general practice beyond the United States, is that
    12          the Tribunal may issue orders to the parties before it
    13          requiring them to produce evidence known to exist and
    14          that is necessary for trial but not otherwise available
    15          to the requesting party.
    16                In reaching its decision, the Trial Chamber
    17          accepted the Prosecutor's arguments of necessity, and
    18          asserted that "the jurisdiction of the International
    19          Tribunal dictates that the evidence which it requires
    20          will often be in the form of government documents".
    21                This is by no means manifest.  Most of the world's
    22          courts function effectively and fairly without the
    23          ability to compel States, or even individuals, "to
    24          submit whatever material is necessary to evaluate" the
    25          case before them.
     1                Croatia has declined to provide the documents
     2          demanded by the Prosecutor in part because they may
     3          obtain information of national security concern.  Both
     4          the Trial Chamber and the Prosecutor have suggested that
     5          any such concerns must be subject to review and
     6          resolution by the Trial Chamber, much as claims of a
     7          national security "privilege" are resolved in national
     8          courts.
     9                However, the right of certain national courts to
    10          review and determine the "executive privilege" or
    11          "national security" claims of their own governments has
    12          no analogue or application in international law.
    13                The determination of the national security needs
    14          of each State is a fundamental attribute of its
    15          sovereignty.  An international tribunal cannot judge and
    16          determine such claims like a domestic court determining
    17          claims of "privilege".
    18                In international fora like the ICJ, for example, a
    19          State's assertion of national security interests is
    20          universally respected, even where the State is within
    21          the competence of the court; again, the Corfu Channel
    22          case is the leading example in this area.
    23                Nevertheless, the Trial Chamber's opinion in this
    24          regard begins from the mistaken premise that an
    25          International Tribunal may act as do some national
     1          courts, determining whether confidential government
     2          documents may be used in litigation or revealed to the
     3          public.
     4                The authority of national courts to judge national
     5          security claims by their own States, however, cannot
     6          serve as a basis for the exercise of this power by the
     7          International Tribunal.
     8                Although domestic courts may be entitled to weigh
     9          the national security claims made by their own
    10          governments, this is a matter entirely dependent upon
    11          the constituted form of government involved.  In all
    12          such circumstances, the Judges who assess national
    13          security claims are a part of the legitimately
    14          constituted government of such States, and are
    15          accountable as such.
    16                The Tribunal should accept and follow the well
    17          settled international practice, as found in the Corfu
    18          Channel case, to respect the national security claims of
    19          States, for there is not authority in law or reason that
    20          would support a different rule for Croatia.
    21                In conclusion, the International Tribunal does not
    22          have the power to issue a subpoena duces tecum, or any
