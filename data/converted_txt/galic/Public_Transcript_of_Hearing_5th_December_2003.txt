25             Trial Chamber I of the International Criminal Tribunal for the
 1    former Yugoslavia is sitting today to deliver its judgement in the trial
 2    of General Stanislav Galic.
 3             General Galic is accused of having conducted, between September
 4    1992 and August 1994, a campaign of sniping and shelling attacks on the
 5    civilian population of Sarajevo, causing death and injury to civilians,
 6    with the primary purpose of spreading terror among the civilian
 7    population.
 8             For the purposes of this session, the Trial Chamber will give a
 9    brief explanation of the reasons for its judgement.  The only
10    authoritative account, however, is the written judgement.  Copies of this
11    will be made available to the parties and to the public at the conclusion
12    of this session.
13             The Trial Chamber's judgement is rendered by a majority of its
14    members.  Judge Nieto-Navia is dissenting in part, and will explain
15    briefly at the end of my speech the reasons for his dissent.
16             The Majority reached its decisions after being convinced of the
17    correctness of its conclusions as to the law and after impartial
18    appreciation of the facts.
19             I now turn to the facts of this case.  The accused's trial
20    concerned events which took place in and around Sarajevo, the capital of
21    Bosnia and Herzegovina.  At the time of those events, an entity known as
22    Republika Srpska had established itself within the territory of
23    Bosnia-Herzegovina.  The Army of Bosnia-Herzegovina and the Army of
24    Republika Srpska were engaged in armed conflict.
25             The Army of Republika Srpska was known as the VRS, and by
 1    September 1992 one branch of the VRS, called the Sarajevo Romanija Corps,
 2    or SRK, had virtually encircled Sarajevo.
 3             General Galic was the commander of the SRK throughout the
 4    indictment period, that is, from September 1992 to August 1994.
 5             The major part of Sarajevo was under the control of the Army of
 6    Bosnia-Herzegovina, or ABiH.  A confrontation line around Sarajevo
 7    separated the warring sides.
 8             The Prosecutor alleges that General Galic is criminally
 9    responsible for a campaign conducted by the SRK of sniping and shelling
10    attacks on civilians in the parts of Sarajevo controlled by the ABiH.  The
11    campaign is said to have resulted in a large number of deaths of and
12    injuries to civilians.  According to the Prosecutor, there was thus a
13    violation of international humanitarian law.  The principle of
14    distinction, which forms part of that body of law, obliges military
15    commanders to distinguish between military objectives, on the one hand,
16    and civilians, on the other, and not to attack civilians under any
17    circumstances.
18             The Prosecutor claims that the accused ordered the campaign of
19    attacks on civilians, or otherwise failed to prevent or to punish the
20    crimes of his subordinates.  For this General Galic is charged with war
21    crimes and crimes against humanity.  He is also charged with the crime of
22    terror.  Terror, it is said, was the primary purpose of the campaign of
23    attacks.
24             I should point out that the indictment is not concerned with
25    legitimate military attacks by forces under the control of the accused,
 1    even if such attacks may have resulted in unintended civilian casualties.
 2             The Defence contests the allegations and argues that the
 3    Prosecutor did not establish that civilian casualties were caused by
 4    deliberate or indiscriminate shelling or sniping by the SRK.
 5             The Defence case is that civilian casualties were collateral to
 6    legitimate military activity.  The casualties resulted as well from
 7    targeting errors and stray bullets.  According to the Defence, some
 8    casualties may have been the result of ABiH forces firing upon their own
 9    civilians.
10             The Trial Chamber was confronted with a large amount of evidence,
11    testimonial and documentary.  A total of 171 witnesses were heard.  The
12    number of exhibits, including written reports, film, photographs, maps,
13    and sound-recordings, amounted to 1.268 items, in addition to which there
14    were 15 expert reports.
15             Many witnesses were the victims of attacks or had otherwise been
16    caught in -- caught up in sniping or shelling incidents.  Witnesses
17    included international military personnel stationed in Sarajevo, as well
18    as members of the armed forces of the parties to the conflict.  Several
19    expert witnesses, with specialisations ranging from history to ballistics,
20    were called by the Prosecutor and the Defence.
21             The Trial Chamber was mindful when assessing and drawing
22    conclusions from the evidence that one party to the conflict, the ABiH
23    army, was positioned in close proximity with the civilian population of
24    Sarajevo and that much of the fighting was conducted in a confined urban
25    setting in which military targets were not always clearly separated from
 1    civilian objects.
 2             The evidence demonstrates beyond reasonable doubt that Sarajevo
 3    civilians were indeed made the object of deliberate attacks by SRK forces.
 4    The Trial Chamber heard from local witnesses who had experienced a
 5    multiplicity of attacks in their neighbourhoods.  They were attacked while
 6    attending funerals, while in ambulances, trams and buses, and while
 7    cycling.  They were attacked while tending gardens or shopping in markets
 8    or clearing rubbish in the city.  Children were targeted while playing or
 9    walking in the streets.
10             The attacks were mostly carried out in daylight.  They were not
11    in response to any military threat.  The attackers could, for the most
12    part, easily tell that their victims were engaged in everyday civilian
13    activities.
14             The topography of Sarajevo, with its ridges and high-rise
15    buildings, provided vantage points for SRK personnel to target civilians
16    in the city.  Certain locations in Sarajevo became notorious sniping
17    spots.  For example, several witnesses testified that the main
18    thoroughfare in Sarajevo was known as "Sniper Alley."
19             Although civilians adapted to some extent to the frequent attacks
20    by closing schools, by living at night and hiding during the day, by
21    moving around Sarajevo as little as possible, and by setting up steel
22    containers to shield against sniper fire, they were still not safe.  They
23    were still seen and targeted.  There was little effective protection
24    against shelling.

 1    to the frequency and the pattern of shelling and sniping attacks over the
 2    protracted period, which is the subject of the indictment.  Other
 3    witnesses concentrated on specific incidents, which were presented to the
 4    Trial Chamber often in minute detail.
 5             The Prosecutor listed certain sniping and shelling incidents as
 6    exemplary of the campaign against civilians.  The Majority has found that
 7    18 of the 26 listed sniping incidents and all five of the listed shelling
 8    incidents were proven by the Prosecutor as exemplifying the campaign.  I
 9    will not go into these in any detail except to give an example of two
10    sniping incidents accepted by the Trial Chamber and of a shelling incident
11    accepted by the Majority.
12             The first sniping incident I shall discuss concerns the killing
13    of Munira Zametica, a 48-year-old civilian woman on the 11th of July,
14    1993.
15             Mrs. Zametica had gone to the Dobrinja River to fetch water.  She
16    remained for a while on the north-western side of the bridge.  The bridge
17    shielded her from sniping fire that had been ongoing through that day.
18    The half dozen people standing with her hesitated to approach the river
19    bank, for this would have meant leaving the shelter of the bridge.  When
20    Mrs. Zametica overcame her hesitations and went down to the river to fill
21    her bucket, she was struck by a bullet.  The shooting continued.  The
22    bystanders and Mrs. Zametica's daughter, who had arrived in the meantime,
23    could not approach the victim because of the danger.  Mrs. Zametica was
24    face down in the river, bleeding, and she was finally pulled out of the
25    water and taken to hospital, where she died.  The Trial Chamber has
 1    concluded that she was deliberately targeted, deliberately shot from the
 2    area of the Orthodox church in Dobrinja, a well-known source of sniper
 3    fire under SRK control.
 4             In another incident, on a sunny afternoon in September 1993, an
 5    eight-year-old girl, Elma, and her mother went out to collect some
 6    textbooks from one of Elma's fellow pupils.  Their neighbourhood on that
 7    day had been peaceful.  Mother and daughter, holding hands, followed a
 8    line of steel containers which had been put up to provide cover from SRK
 9    snipers operating on Hrasno Hill.  There were no soldiers or other
10    possible military targets in the immediate vicinity.  As the two emerged
11    from the shelter of the containers, they were shot.  A single bullet
12    struck the mother's thigh, passing through the flesh, and penetrated
13    Elma's stomach.  Both fell to the ground.  Another bullet whizzed past
14    them.  No bystanders dared to come to their assistance.  Mother and
15    daughter crawled -- managed to crawl away from the exposed ground.  They
16    spent almost two weeks in hospital.  The evidence establishes beyond
17    reasonable doubt that the fire came from SRK positions.  Elma and her
18    mother were targeted from there deliberately.
19             I will now mention the Majority's finding on one of the listed
20    shelling incidents.  On the 5th of February, 1994, a mortar shell exploded
21    in the Markale market in downtown Sarajevo, killing some 60 people and
22    injuring more than a hundred.  The Trial Chamber heard about this incident
23    in great detail.  We examined the contemporary investigations conducted by
24    the United Nations personnel and by local investigators, as well as the
25    analyses of experts called by the parties in this case.  A variety of new
 1    information was brought to light.  The Majority has concluded that the
 2    mortar shell was caused -- which caused the explosion was fired from
 3    territory controlled by the SRK.  It was a devastating attack against a
 4    civilian target.
 5             The Majority is thus convinced that civilians in Sarajevo were
 6    attacked directly or without distinction from SRK-controlled territory.
 7    The exact numbers of civilian casualties from these attacks is not known.
 8    What is known is that hundreds of civilians were killed and thousands were
 9    injured in sniping and shelling incidents over the two-year period covered
10    by the indictment.  A fraction of these, but no more than a fraction, may
11    have been accidents.
12             Because, to the Majority, it is clear from the evidence that
13    civilians were deliberately attacked by the SRK in a large number of
14    incidents over a long period of time, we are persuaded that the attacks
15    were not isolated incidents but amounted to a widespread or a systematic
16    campaign.
17             In addition to supporting the existence of a campaign, the
18    evidence, as understood by the Majority, reveals that the campaign against
19    civilians was intended primarily to terrorise the civilian population.  It
20    had no discernible significance in military terms.  The frequency of
21    attacks may have fluctuated from day to day, but they always underscored
22    the fact that no civilian of Sarajevo was safe anywhere.
23             There is some testimony, which the Majority has scrutinised
24    carefully, that the ABiH sought to attract the sympathy of the
25    international community by periodically staging attacks on its own
 1    civilians, knowing that the SRK would be blamed for them.  The Majority
 2    does not believe that this evidence amounts to much.  In any case, even if
 3    such things happened occasionally, they do not alter the Majority's
 4    conclusions as to which party perpetrated the vast number of sniping and
 5    shelling attacks on civilians which were considered in the course of this
 6    trial.
 7             I now turn to the legal characterisation of the crimes.
 8             The Prosecutor charged General Galic with the crime of terror and
 9    with the crime of attack on civilians.  Both are said to be prohibited by
10    Article 51 of the Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions of
11    1994 -- 1949 and of Article 13 of Additional Protocol II to the Geneva
12    Conventions of 1949.  These charges are brought under Article 3 of the
13    Statute as violations of the laws or customs of war.
14             Moreover, murder and inhumane acts are charged as crimes against
15    humanity under Article 5 of the Statute.  (The legal notion of "inhumane
16    acts" is meant to cover the nonfatal injuries inflicted in the course of
17    the attacks on civilians.)
18             The Trial Chamber has found that the International Tribunal does
19    indeed have jurisdiction over the crime of attacks -- the crime of attack
20    on civilians under Article 3 of the Statute.  Not only does the crime have
21    a sound basis in customary international law, in the present case it has a
22    foundation in conventional law.  This is because the warring sides were
23    treaty-bound to respect the law on the conduct of hostilities contained in
24    an agreement they signed on the 22nd of May, 1992.  This agreement
25    reproduced the prohibitions contained in Article 51 of Additional Protocol
 1    I.
 2             The Trial Chamber explains in its judgement that the crime of
 3    attack on civilians is constituted of acts of violence, willfully directed
 4    against the civilian population or individual civilians not taking part,
 5    direct part, in hostilities, causing death or serious injury to body or
 6    health within the civilian population.
 7             As for the crime of terror as a violation of the laws and customs
 8    of war, this is the first time that the International Tribunal has had to
 9    pronounce on its material and mental elements.
10             For the reasons detailed in the judgement, the Majority finds
11    that the International Tribunal has jurisdiction over the crime of terror
12    under Article 3 of the Statute.  The prohibition against this crime is
13    found in Article 51 of Additional Protocol I.  That provision had been
14    brought into effect by the aforementioned agreement of the 22nd of May of
15    1992.
16             The Majority's discussion of the crime of terror takes up many
17    pages of the judgement.  Suffice it to say here that the crime is
18    constituted of the same legal elements as the crime of attack on
19    civilians, plus an additional mental element.
20             In particular, for the accused to be convicted of the crime of
21    terror, the Prosecutor must prove that the attack on civilians for which
22    the accused has been shown to be responsible was carried out with the
23    primary purpose of spreading terror among the civilian population.
24             Upon review of the factual findings made in the judgement, the
25    Majority is satisfied that attacks were carried out by SRK forces against

 7             The criminal responsibility of General Galic.
 8             I shall now address the issue of whether responsibility for the
 9    crime of terror, attacks on civilians, and murder and inhumane acts is to
10    be imputed to the accused.
11             The Trial Chamber has considered whether General Galic
12    effectively controlled the actions of his troops and knew of the crimes
13    committed by them.  We are convinced by the evidence that the sniping and
14    shelling activity of the SRK were under the control of the SRK's chain of
15    command.  The Trial Chamber is also satisfied that General Galic had the
16    material ability to punish those who would go against his orders, who
17    violated military discipline, or who committed crimes.
18             It is therefore established that General Galic, as commander of
19    the SRK, had effective control of SRK troops.
20             There is ample evidence that General Galic was informed of the
21    attacks against civilians committed by SRK forces.  Formal complaints were
22    lodged with him, and he was duly informed through his chain of command of
23    the actions of his troops.  The Trial Chamber has no doubt that the
24    accused was well aware of the unlawful activities of his troops.
25             However, in the view of the Majority, General Galic was not
 1    simply kept abreast of the crimes of his subordinates.  He actually
 2    controlled the pace and scale of those crimes.  For example, several
 3    witnesses testified to a reduction in the frequency of attacks on
 4    civilians by the SRK after pressure was put upon General Galic to have
 5    them stopped.  This drop in frequency never lasted long.  The attacks
 6    would again increase in intensity.  The SRK troops performed at the level
 7    which their commander decided they should perform in the circumstances.
 8             The Majority is convinced that the SRK's widespread attacks
 9    against the civilian population of Sarajevo could not have occurred
10    without this being the will of the corps commander.  It is clear that
11    General Galic, through his orders, and by other means of facilitation and
12    encouragement, conducted the campaign of attacks.  He did so with the
13    primary aim to spread terror among the civilian population of Sarajevo.
14             The gravity of the crime for which General Galic is responsible
15    is determined by the scale, pattern, and reiteration of the attacks, on an
16    almost daily basis, over many months.  The civilian population of
17    Sarajevo - men and women of all ages, including children - were killed in
18    their hundreds and wounded in their thousands, with the intent to
19    terrorise the entirety of the population.  The Majority takes into
20    consideration the physical and psychological suffering inflicted on
21    civilians over the two-year span of the indictment period.
22             Finally, the Majority finds that the fact that General Galic
23    occupied the position of corps commander in the VRS, a very senior
24    position of public trust and duty, and repeatedly breached that duty and
25    trust, counts as an aggravating factor in determining the penalty.
 1             The Defence argued that the ABiH itself committed crimes against
 2    civilians of Serbian ethnicity and conducted hostilities under the shield
 3    provided by its own civilian population.  While there is some evidence to
 4    support this view, the occasional unlawful conduct of one party to a
 5    conflict cannot possibly excuse the opposing party's attacking civilians
 6    as part of a protracted campaign of terror.
 7             I now come to the disposition.
 8             General Galic, please rise.
 9             For the reasons I summarised above, the Trial Chamber, with
10    Judge Nieto-Navia dissenting, having considered all of the evidence and
11    arguments of both the Prosecutor and the Defence, makes the following
12    disposition:
13             General Galic, you are found guilty on the following counts,
14    pursuant to Article 7(1) of the Statute:
15             Count 1:  Violation of the laws or customs or war (acts of
16    violence, the primary purpose of which is to spread terror among the
17    civilian population, as set forth in Article 51 of Additional Protocol I
18    to the Geneva Conventions of 1949) under Article 3 of the Statute.
19             Count 2:  Crimes against humanity (murder) under Article 5(a) of
20    the Statute.
21             Count 3:  Crimes against humanity (inhumane acts - other than
22    murder) under Article 5(i) of the Statute.
23             Count 5:  Crimes against humanity (murder) under Article 5(a) of
24    the Statute.
25             Count 6:  Crimes against humanity (inhumane acts - other than
 1    murder) under Article 5(i) of the Statute.
 2             According to the rule against cumulation of convictions for the
 3    same acts, where one crime is only a more specific form of another, and
 4    both crimes are proven, a conviction should be entered for the more
 5    specific crime.  The finding of guilt on Count 1 - terror - has the

14             JUDGE NIETO-NAVIA:  Thank you, Mr. President.
15             I regret to have to speak separately today as a dissenting Judge
16    on many of the factual and legal conclusions reached by the Majority of
17    this Trial Chamber.
18             The principle in dubio pro reo is one of the foundational
19    precepts of criminal law which can be found in domestic and international
20    legal systems as well as the jurisprudence of this Tribunal.  According to
21    this principle, the Prosecution must prove a fact aimed at a conviction
22    beyond a reasonable doubt.  I indicated to the Majority my concerns and
23    doubts about the evidence relating to 8 out of 18 scheduled sniping
24    incidents, 3 out of 5 scheduled shelling incidents - including the Markale
25    incident which is discussed in detail in my opinion - as well as certain
 1    unscheduled incidents.  I considered these doubts to be reasonable.  I had
 2    expected this plural Chamber to accept my doubts as sufficient to
 3    establish that the Prosecution has failed to prove an allegation beyond a
 4    reasonable doubt.  The Majority did not share this expectation, and I have
 5    been obliged to express separately my disagreement with its assessment of
 6    the evidence.
 7             The Prosecution has alleged that the SRK "conducted a protracted
 8    campaign of shelling and sniping upon civilian areas of Sarajevo and upon
 9    the civilian population."  An army characterised by the level of
10    competence and professionalism, ascribed to the SRK by the Prosecution,
11    would be expected, when conducting during 23 months a campaign of
12    purposefully targeting civilians living in a city of 340.000 people, to
13    inflict a high number of civilian casualties in relation to the city's
14    total population.  The results obtained by the Prosecution's demographic
15    experts, based on an analysis of extensive sources, indicate otherwise.
16    Furthermore, the monthly number of civilian casualties dropped
17    significantly over the 23 months of the indictment period.  This evidence
18    leads me to conclude that the SRK forces, under the command of
19    General Galic, did not engage in a campaign of purposefully targeting
20    civilians in Sarajevo throughout the indictment period.  Such a conclusion
21    accords with the evidence regarding the conduct of the SRK leadership,
22    which relinquished voluntarily control of the airport, authorised the
23    establishment of "blue routes" to allow for the distribution of
24    humanitarian supplies and the safe passage of civilians in and out of the
25    city, entered into anti-sniping agreements under the auspices of the
 1    United Nations and agreed to the establishment of the total exclusion
 2    zone.
 3             I now consider issues related to the applicable law and legal
 4    findings.  The Majority has reached the conclusion that the offence of
 5    inflicting terror on a civilian population falls within the jurisdiction
 6    of this Trial Chamber.  When proposing the establishment of this Tribunal,
 7    the UN Secretary-General explained that the application of the criminal
 8    law principle of nullum crimen sine lege would require that this
 9    International Tribunal apply rules which are, beyond any doubt, part of
10    customary law.  This principle has been consistently recognised in the
11    jurisprudence of this Tribunal as requiring that a Trial Chamber verify
12    that an offence alleged in an indictment reflects international customary
13    law.  For the first time in the history of this Tribunal, this
14    Trial Chamber has had to consider whether the offence of inflicting terror
15    on a civilian population falls within its jurisdiction.  I would have,
16    therefore, expected the Trial Chamber to confirm whether such an offence
17    existed as a form of liability under international customary law,
18    attracting individual criminal responsibility under that body of law.  The
19    Majority did not do so and, instead, relied on an argument based on
20    conventional law to conclude that the Trial Chamber may consider such an
21    offence.  In my view, such an approach does not satisfy the jurisdictional
22    requirements of this Tribunal.  Since I am not aware of an established
23    state practice regarding the criminalisation of such an offence sufficient
24    to prove the latter's customary nature, I conclude that the offence of
25    inflicting terror as a civilian population -- on a civilian population
 1    does not fall within the Trial Chamber's jurisdiction.  By concluding
 2    otherwise, without establishing that the offence of inflicting terror on a
 3    civilian population attracted individual criminal responsibility under
 4    international customary law, or even under the conventional rule which it
 5    invokes, the Majority is furthering a conception of international
 6    humanitarian law, which I do not support.
 7             The Majority also concludes that General Galic ordered his forces
 8    to attack civilians in Sarajevo deliberately, thereby finding him
 9    criminally responsible under Article 7(1) of the Statute.  Its conclusion
10    rests entirely on inferences though, since no witness testified to hearing
11    General Galic issue such orders and no written orders were tendered which
12    would indicate that he so instructed his troops.  There is significant
13    evidence which explicitly established the opposite:  Written orders signed
14    by General Galic instructing his troops to respect the Geneva Conventions
15    and other instruments of international humanitarian law, the testimonies
16    of 16 SRK officers and soldiers posted throughout Sarajevo during the
17    indictment period confirming that they had received orders not to target
18    civilians, as well as other written evidence indicating that General Galic
19    launched some internal investigations on a number of occasions when
20    alerted by UN representatives about possible attacks on civilians by his
21    forces.  Based on the available evidence, I conclude that the trial record
22    does not establish beyond a reasonable doubt that General Galic issued
23    orders to target civilians and dissent from the Majority's conclusion on
24    this issue.
25             Despite the aforementioned disagreements with the Majority, I
 1    share in the conclusion that the Prosecution has proved that, in a number
 2    of instances, the SRK either deliberately or recklessly fired upon
 3    civilians in Sarajevo during the indictment period, thereby committing the
 4    crimes of attacks on civilians, murders, and inhumane acts.  I also note
 5    that the evidence presented at trial established that General Galic, as
 6    the commander of the SRK, knew or had reasons to know of these crimes, but
 7    did not take all the necessary and reasonable measures to prevent their
 8    commission or to punish the perpetrators.  I therefore conclude that
 9    General Galic is guilty of the crimes of unlawful attacks against
10    civilians, murder, and inhumane acts under Article 7(3) of the Statute.
11             In light of this finding, I would sentence General Galic to 10
12    years' imprisonment.
13             JUDGE ORIE:  This brings the trial of General Galic to an end.
